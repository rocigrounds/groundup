Fine-Tuning You Can Actually Do: LoRA and Friends

How to adapt an 8B (or 70B) model on hardware you can rent for the price of a pizza: the LoRA math, the QLoRA memory tricks, and a complete weekend recipe.

In this chapter
  1. Is fine-tuning even the right tool?
  2. What full fine-tuning actually costs
  3. LoRA: the low-rank hypothesis
  4. QLoRA: the 4-bit base
  5. The rest of the PEFT zoo
  6. A weekend project, end to end
  7. Where this leaves us

Every chapter in this part so far has described things you almost certainly cannot do. You will not pretrain a frontier model (Chapter 34); you will probably never run reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) at scale (Chapter 35). Those chapters exist so you understand the artifacts other people build.

This chapter is different. Everything in it is something you — one person, one rented graphics processing unit (GPU), one weekend — can actually do. Open-weight models like Llama, Qwen, and Gemma are sitting on Hugging Face right now, and with the techniques in this chapter you can teach an 8-billion-parameter model your document format, your house style, your niche task, for a few dollars of compute. Teams do exactly this every day: a base model plus a small, cheap, task-specific adaptation is one of the standard deployment patterns of the 2020s.

The obstacle is memory. Fine-tuning a model the “honest” way — updating every parameter with an optimizer, exactly as in pretraining — costs roughly sixteen bytes of GPU memory per parameter before you’ve processed a single token, which puts even an 8B model out of reach of any single affordable GPU. The escape hatch is a family of methods called parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT), and its undisputed champion is LoRA — low-rank adaptation. LoRA rests on one empirical observation with a clean linear-algebra formulation: the change a model needs to learn your task is low-rank, so you can represent it with two skinny matrices instead of touching the giant ones. This chapter teaches you the observation, the math, the memory accounting, the 4-bit extension (QLoRA — quantized LoRA) that fits a 65B model on one GPU, and a complete, realistic recipe — including the ways it goes wrong.

Is fine-tuning even the right tool?

Before spending a weekend, spend five minutes on the question most often skipped. You have three ways to change what a model does, in increasing order of effort:

  1. Prompting. Write better instructions; include a few worked examples (few-shot); pin down the output format. Costs nothing, iterates in seconds, and modern instruction-tuned models are startlingly steerable this way.
  2. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). Fetch relevant documents at query time and put them in the context window (Chapter 45). This is how you give a model access to knowledge — your files, this week’s news, a client’s contracts — without training anything.
  3. Fine-tuning. Continue training on your own examples so the desired behavior moves into the weights.

The clean way to choose is to ask what kind of gap you are closing. Fine-tuning excels at style, format, and skill: making outputs reliably match a schema, adopting a voice, mastering a narrow transformation (medical-note summarization, SQL dialect translation, legal-citation formatting) that the base model does inconsistently. It is the right tool when the behavior is stable, the task is narrow, and you will make many calls — because a fine-tuned small model can replace a long, expensive prompt on a large one.

Fine-tuning is usually the wrong tool for knowledge injection — teaching the model facts it does not know.

⚠️Careful

Fine-tuning for facts is usually a mistake. A few thousand fine-tuning examples nudge weights that were shaped by trillions of pretraining tokens; the result is a model that has seen your facts, not one that reliably knows them. It will paraphrase them, half-remember them, and hallucinate confidently in the gaps — and the knowledge goes stale the moment your documents change. Retrieval puts the actual text in front of the model at answer time, cites its sources, and updates by re-indexing a folder. Use fine-tuning to teach the model how to answer; use retrieval to give it what to answer with. (The combination — a fine-tuned model behind a retrieval pipeline — is common and works well.)

One more honest wrinkle: prompting and fine-tuning trade off against inference cost. A 40-example few-shot prompt might match a fine-tune’s quality — but you pay for those prompt tokens on every single call (Chapter 39). Fine-tuning is, among other things, a way of compiling the prompt into the weights.

What full fine-tuning actually costs

Suppose you ignore this chapter’s advice and fine-tune the honest way: load the model, compute gradients for every parameter, run AdamW. What does that cost in memory? Chapter 33 built the ledger; here is the recap, per parameter, for standard mixed-precision training:

  • Weights: 2 bytes (bfloat16, “bf16”).
  • Gradients: 2 bytes (bfloat16).
  • Optimizer state: 12 bytes — AdamW (Chapter 11) keeps a first-moment estimate and a second-moment estimate, plus a full-precision master copy of the weights, all in 32-bit floats (“fp32”): 4+4+44+4+44+4+4 bytes.

Total: 16 bytes per parameter, before a single activation is stored. For an 8B model that is 8×109×16=1288\times10^9 \times 16 = 1288×109×16=128 GB. The largest single GPUs you can rent in 2026 top out around 80–192 GB, and the affordable ones (consumer-class cards) have 24 GB. Full fine-tuning of even a small modern model is a multi-GPU project with all the sharding machinery of Chapter 33 — which is exactly the machinery a weekend practitioner does not want to operate.

Notice where the money goes: only 2 of those 16 bytes are the model itself. The other 14 exist because parameters are trainable. Every parameter you freeze sheds its gradient and its optimizer state — 14 of 16 bytes. That observation is the entire economic logic of PEFT: if we could train only a tiny fraction of the parameters and freeze the rest, memory would collapse toward the 2-byte floor of merely holding the model. The question is whether a tiny trainable fraction can possibly be enough.

LoRA: the low-rank hypothesis

It can — and the reason why is one of the most satisfying empirical facts in this book.

💡Intuition

Pretraining did almost all the work. A base model already contains the representations, the grammar, the world knowledge, the skills; your fine-tune is not teaching it language, it is rotating its behavior slightly — prefer this format, adopt this voice, apply this known skill in this setting. A slight, coherent change to behavior should not require independently adjusting billions of numbers. It should be describable with few numbers — a low-dimensional correction. LoRA is the bet that the update to each weight matrix, as a matrix in its own right, is (approximately) low-rank: recall from Chapter 2 that a rank-rrr matrix, no matter how large its shape, only really does rrr independent things — it maps everything through an rrr-dimensional bottleneck.

This bet has direct evidence behind it. In 2020, Aghajanyan et al. measured the intrinsic dimensionality of fine-tuning: instead of training all parameters, they trained ddd numbers and mapped them into the full parameter space through a fixed random projection, asking how small ddd could be while still reaching 90% of full fine-tuning performance. For RoBERTa-large — 355 million parameters — some standard tasks needed a ddd in the low hundreds (the exact figures vary by task; treat the magnitude, not the digits, as the finding). Hundreds of effective dimensions, out of hundreds of millions. And the intrinsic dimension fell as the pretrained model got bigger and better: the stronger the base model, the smaller the correction a new task requires.

Hu et al. (2021) turned this into a method. Rather than a random projection over all parameters, constrain each weight matrix’s update to be low-rank, and learn the low-rank factors directly.

∑The math

Take one weight matrix W0∈Rdout×dinW_0 \in \R^{d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}}}W0​∈Rdout​×din​ — say, an attention projection. Full fine-tuning would learn an update ΔW\Delta WΔW of the same shape and use W0+ΔWW_0 + \Delta WW0​+ΔW. LoRA instead parameterizes the update as a product of two skinny matrices:

W′  =  W0  +  αr BA,B∈Rdout×r,    A∈Rr×din,W' \;=\; W_0 \;+\; \frac{\alpha}{r}\, B A, \qquad B \in \R^{d_{\text{out}} \times r},\;\; A \in \R^{r \times d_{\text{in}}},W′=W0​+rα​BA,B∈Rdout​×r,A∈Rr×din​,

where rrr is the rank (a small integer you choose, typically 4–64, with r≪dinr \ll d_{\text{in}}r≪din​) and α\alphaα is a scalar scaling hyperparameter. During training, W0W_0W0​ is completely frozen — no gradients, no optimizer state — and only AAA and BBB are trained. The forward pass for input xxx is

h  =  W0x  +  αr B(Ax):h \;=\; W_0 x \;+\; \frac{\alpha}{r}\, B (A x):h=W0​x+rα​B(Ax):

the frozen path computes what the base model always computed, and the bypass first compresses xxx to rrr dimensions (AxAxAx), then expands back to doutd_{\text{out}}dout​ (B⋅B \cdotB⋅), adding a rank-≤r\le r≤r correction. The extra compute is negligible: two thin matrix multiplies.

Why divide by rrr? So that the update’s overall scale stays roughly constant when you change the rank. If you double rrr, the sum BAxBAxBAx has twice as many rank-one contributions; the α/r\alpha/rα/r factor compensates, which means the learning rate and α\alphaα you tuned at r=8r=8r=8 transfer reasonably to r=32r=32r=32. (A later refinement, rank-stabilized LoRA, argues the theoretically right factor is α/r\alpha/\sqrt{r}α/r​ for large ranks — rsLoRA — and most libraries offer it as a flag.)

Initialization. AAA is initialized with small random Gaussian values and BBB is initialized to zero. Then BA=0BA = 0BA=0 at step zero: the adapted model starts out computing exactly what the base model computes, and training grows the correction smoothly from nothing. Both matrices still learn — the gradient with respect to BBB is nonzero as soon as AxAxAx is nonzero. (Initializing both to zero would be a bug: every gradient path through the bypass would be zero and nothing would ever train. One side must break the symmetry.)

131,07216,777,216≈0.78%\frac{131{,}072}{16{,}777{,}216} \approx 0.78\%16,777,216131,072​≈0.78%

of the original — a 128× reduction on this matrix, and the frozen 16.8M cost 2 bytes each instead of 16.

Scaling up to a real model: Llama-3.1-8B has 32 transformer blocks; applying r=16r=16r=16 LoRA to every linear layer in every block (the four attention projections plus the three multi-layer perceptron (MLP) matrices) gives roughly 1.3M trainable parameters per block, about 42M total — around 0.5% of 8B. The trainable parameters, their gradients, and their optimizer states together need 42M×16≈0.742\text{M} \times 16 \approx 0.742M×16≈0.7 GB. The full memory picture: 16 GB of frozen bfloat16 base + 0.7 GB of trainable machinery ≈ 17 GB, versus 128 GB for full fine-tuning — before activations in both cases. An 80 GB GPU fits it easily; a 24 GB card fits it with gradient checkpointing and modest batch sizes.

Which matrices, which rank, which alpha

Three practical questions dominate every LoRA configuration file, and the honest answers are part empiricism, part folklore.

Which matrices get adapters? The original paper adapted only the attention query and value projections (WQW_QWQ​, WVW_VWV​) and found rrr as low as 1–4 surprisingly effective. Later, systematic experiments — most influentially the ablations in the QLoRA paper — found that adapting all linear layers (all four attention projections plus the MLP matrices) matters more than raising the rank. In 2026 the near-universal default is all-linear. Embedding and output-head matrices are usually left frozen unless you are adding new tokens.

Which rank? Performance rises quickly from r=1r=1r=1 to r≈8r\approx 8r≈8–16, then flattens for most instruction-style tasks; the LoRA paper’s subspace analysis showed that the directions learned at high rank substantially overlap those learned at low rank — the extra capacity mostly learns redundant or noise directions. Common practice: r=16r=16r=16 as the default, r=32r=32r=32–64 when the task is hard or the dataset large (code, math, a new language), r=8r=8r=8 when memory is tight. If you find yourself needing r=256r=256r=256 to match quality, that is evidence your task is not a small correction — consider full fine-tuning or question the dataset.

Which alpha? Folklore says α=2r\alpha = 2rα=2r (so α/r=2\alpha/r = 2α/r=2); the QLoRA paper used α=16\alpha=16α=16 with r=64r=64r=64; plenty of successful runs use α=r\alpha=rα=r. Because α\alphaα multiplies the whole update, it trades off directly against the learning rate — doubling α\alphaα is nearly equivalent to doubling the learning rate on the bypass. Pick one convention, tune the learning rate, and stop worrying. Speaking of which: LoRA fine-tunes run at learning rates around 1×10−41\times10^{-4}1×10−4 to 3×10−43\times10^{-4}3×10−4 — roughly 10× higher than full fine-tuning of the same model, because you are training small freshly-initialized matrices, not billions of delicately pretrained ones.

✎Note

How close does LoRA get to full fine-tuning? Biderman et al. (2024) ran the careful comparison and titled it accurately: LoRA learns less and forgets less. On hard target domains far from pretraining (their tests: code and math, with large datasets), full fine-tuning still wins on the target task; LoRA gives up some target-domain gain but preserves markedly more of the base model’s general ability, acting as an implicit regularizer. For the style/format/skill tasks this chapter recommends fine-tuning for, the gap is small to nonexistent — and the forgetting resistance is a feature, not a consolation prize.

Merging: zero inference cost

After training you hold two artifacts: the untouched base model and a small adapter file (AAA and BBB for every adapted matrix — tens of megabytes). You can deploy either way:

  • Merged. Compute W′=W0+αrBAW' = W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r}BAW′=W0​+rα​BA once, offline, and save the result as an ordinary model. The deployed network has identical architecture, latency, and memory to the base model — the adapter has dissolved into the weights. This is LoRA’s headline advantage over adapter methods that insert extra layers: zero added inference latency.
  • Unmerged, multi-adapter. Keep the base frozen and the adapters separate — which unlocks an economy of its own.
⚙️In practice

One base, many LoRAs is a real business model. An 8B base occupies ~16 GB of GPU memory; each r=16r=16r=16 all-linear adapter is ~80–170 MB. A serving stack that keeps one copy of the base resident and hot-swaps (or better, batches across) hundreds of customer adapters can sell “your own fine-tuned model” to each customer for roughly the marginal cost of serving the shared base. Systems like S-LoRA and Punica showed how to run the bypass computation for many different adapters inside a single batched kernel, serving thousands of adapters from one GPU. This is how fine-tuning-as-a-service platforms (Together, Fireworks, and the fine-tuning endpoints of the closed labs) keep per-customer prices near base-model prices — and why LoRA, not full fine-tuning, is what most such services actually train unless you pay for more.

QLoRA: the 4-bit base

LoRA removed 14 of the 16 bytes per frozen parameter. QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023) went after the remaining 2: if the base model is frozen anyway — never updated, only read — why store it in 16-bit precision? Quantize it to 4 bits and you shrink the dominant memory term by another 4×. The paper’s headline demonstration: fine-tuning a 65B-parameter model on a single 48 GB GPU, producing the Guanaco family of chatbots, with fine-tuned quality matching 16-bit LoRA.

Three ideas make it work, and each is understandable on its own.

💡Intuition

NormalFloat (NF4), in plain English. A 4-bit number has sixteen possible values. Naive 4-bit quantization spaces those sixteen values evenly, like millimeter marks on a ruler. But neural-network weights are not evenly spread — within a block they are approximately bell-curve (normally) distributed: crowded near zero, sparse in the tails. An even ruler wastes half its marks out in the tails where almost no weights live. NF4 instead places its sixteen values at the quantiles of a standard normal distribution — dense near zero, spread out in the tails, with one value pinned exactly at 0 — so each of the sixteen codes is used about equally often. Same 4 bits, meaningfully less rounding error for bell-curved data; the paper calls it information-theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights.

∑The math

Concretely, NF4 quantization works block-by-block. Split each weight matrix into blocks of 64 consecutive weights. For each block, record its absmax — the largest absolute value, stored in higher precision — and divide the block by it, mapping every weight into [−1,1][-1, 1][−1,1]. Then snap each normalized weight to the nearest of the 16 fixed NF4 levels and store its 4-bit index. Dequantization is the reverse: look up the level, multiply by the block’s absmax.

Two refinements complete the recipe:

Double quantization. Those per-block absmax constants add up: one 32-bit float per 64 weights is 32/64=0.532/64 = 0.532/64=0.5 extra bits per parameter. QLoRA quantizes the constants themselves — 8-bit quantization over blocks of 256 constants — cutting the overhead to about 0.127 bits per parameter and saving roughly 0.37 bits/parameter overall (about 3 GB on a 65B model).

Paged optimizers. Even with a 4-bit base, activation spikes (a long sequence in the batch) can momentarily exceed GPU memory. QLoRA registers the optimizer states in unified memory so the GPU can transparently page them to CPU RAM during a spike and page them back after — trading a brief slowdown for not crashing at hour nine of a ten-hour run.

Compute still happens in 16-bit. Each weight block is dequantized to bfloat16 on the fly immediately before its matrix multiply; the LoRA adapters A,BA, BA,B — and their gradients and optimizer states — remain in regular 16/32-bit precision throughout. Gradients flow through the frozen quantized weights into the adapters; no gradient with respect to a 4-bit number is ever needed, which is what makes training a quantized model coherent.

✏️Worked example

The ledger, three ways, for an 8B model (weights and training machinery only — activations come on top of each):

  • Full fine-tuning: 8B×168\text{B} \times 168B×16 bytes =128= 128=128 GB. Multi-GPU territory.
  • LoRA (bf16 base): frozen base 8B×2=168\text{B} \times 2 = 168B×2=16 GB; trainable adapters ~42M params × 16\times\,16×16 bytes ≈0.7\approx 0.7≈0.7 GB. ≈ 17 GB. Fits a 24 GB card with care, an 80 GB card trivially.
  • QLoRA (NF4 base): frozen base 8B×≈0.538\text{B} \times \approx 0.538B×≈0.53 bytes (4 bits + quantization constants) ≈4.3\approx 4.3≈4.3 GB; adapters as above. ≈ 5 GB. Fits a 12 GB consumer card; a 24 GB card fine-tunes a 30B-class model; 48 GB reaches 65–70B.

That last line is the punchline of the chapter’s title: the gap between “needs a research cluster” and “needs one rented GPU for an afternoon” is these two techniques stacked.

⚠️Careful

QLoRA’s quantization is a training-time memory trick, and it is not free: NF4 rounding slightly perturbs the base model, and quality at the end depends on the adapters learning around that perturbation (the paper shows they do, and that adapting all linear layers is what recovers the gap). Also keep the deployment story straight: if you merge a QLoRA adapter, merge it into the 16-bit base weights, not the 4-bit ones — merging into dequantized-then-requantized weights compounds rounding error. And whatever precision you serve at (Chapter 39 covers inference quantization) is a separate decision from what you trained at.

The rest of the PEFT zoo

LoRA won, but it won a crowded field, and the losers are worth one line each — partly because their ideas resurface elsewhere in the book, and partly because a few remain the right tool in niches.

  • Adapter layers (Houlsby et al., 2019) — the original: insert tiny bottleneck MLPs in series between transformer sublayers and train only those. Effective, but the extra layers sit on the critical path and add inference latency — the exact flaw LoRA’s mergeable parallel bypass was designed to fix.
  • Prefix tuning (Li and Liang, 2021) — train a handful of virtual tokens: learned key/value vectors prepended at every attention layer, steering a frozen model the way a very good prompt would, but in continuous space. Consumes context budget and trains less stably; its conceptual descendants live on in steering vectors.
  • Prompt tuning (Lester et al., 2021) — prefix tuning’s minimalist sibling: learned soft tokens at the input layer only. Weak on small models, but the paper’s lasting finding is that the gap to full fine-tuning shrinks as the base model grows — an early data point for “bigger base models need smaller corrections.”
  • IA³ (Liu et al., 2022; the acronym expands to “Infused Adapter by Inhibiting and Amplifying Inner Activations”) — even cheaper: learn per-channel rescaling vectors for keys, values, and MLP activations (element-wise multiplies, no new matrices at all). Very few parameters; best in few-shot regimes.
  • DoRA (weight-decomposed low-rank adaptation; Liu et al., 2024) — the notable modern refinement: decompose each weight matrix into a magnitude (per-column norm) and a direction, train the magnitudes directly and apply LoRA to the direction only. Its updates behave more like full fine-tuning’s, and it reliably beats plain LoRA at equal rank — especially low rank — at the cost of a somewhat heavier training step. Widely supported in the mainstream libraries; a reasonable “try if LoRA plateaus” option.

The family resemblance is the point: freeze the expensive pretrained bulk, add a small number of well-placed trainable parameters, and let the base model’s competence carry the load. Where the methods differ is where the small parameters go — in series (adapters), in parallel (LoRA/DoRA), in the attention state (prefix), or on the activations (IA³).

A weekend project, end to end

Theory done. Here is the whole practical arc, compressed into one realistic project you could run. The framing task: teach an open 8B model to draft a specific genre of legal document — say, turning a structured summary of facts into a properly formatted demand letter in a firm’s house style. This is a textbook fine-tuning case by the earlier framework: pure style/format/skill, a stable spec, high volume — and the facts of each matter arrive in the prompt, so we are not asking the weights to memorize knowledge.

✏️Worked example

Step 1 — Data. Collect 1,000–5,000 examples of (input summary → finished letter). Quality dominates quantity at this scale: 1,000 consistent, carefully-reviewed examples beat 20,000 scraped inconsistent ones, because the model will learn your inconsistencies every bit as faithfully as your standards. Format each example as a chat transcript — system message (fixed instructions), user message (the structured facts), assistant message (the gold letter). Deduplicate near-copies, then split off a held-out set (say 200 examples) before looking at anything else.

Step 2 — Configuration. A defensible 2026 starting point, learned mostly from the LoRA and QLoRA papers plus community defaults:

Step 3 — Train. On a rented 24 GB card, 3,000 examples × 2 epochs at these settings is on the order of a few GPU-hours — roughly single-digit dollars at 2026 rental prices (approximate and falling). Watch train and eval loss; eval loss turning upward mid-epoch-2 means stop.

Step 4 — Evaluate like you mean it. Before-and-after, on the held-out 200: (a) format adherence — does the output parse/match the required structure? (script-checkable, so check it with a script); (b) style quality — rubric scoring by a strong large language model (LLM) judge, base vs fine-tuned, ideally position-swapped (Chapter 21 covers judge pitfalls); (c) regression check — a general-capability sample (a few hundred mixed-domain prompts or a slice of a broad benchmark like MMLU, the Massive Multitask Language Understanding suite) to confirm you haven’t lobotomized the model at everything else. A typical successful run: format adherence jumps from ~70% to ~99%, style rubric climbs meaningfully, general capability moves within noise.

⚠️Careful

The chat template is the silent killer. Every instruct model was trained with an exact serialization of conversations — special tokens marking system, user, and assistant turns (Chapter 35). Your fine-tuning data must be rendered with the same template the base model expects, token for token, and inference must use it too. Get it subtly wrong — a missing end-of-turn token, a hand-rolled template that differs by one newline, training on raw text while serving through a chat API — and you get a model that seems mysteriously worse than the base, or that rambles past where it should stop (the classic symptom of a mislearned end-of-turn). This single mistake likely accounts for more failed community fine-tunes than any hyperparameter. Use the tokenizer’s built-in chat template; never write your own from a blog post.

Three more failure modes complete the checklist. Catastrophic forgetting: train too long or too hot on a narrow distribution and general capability erodes — LoRA’s low capacity softens this (the “forgets less” result above) but does not repeal it; the regression check in step 4 is your alarm. Format overfitting: the model learns your format too well and applies it to everything — ask it a casual question and it returns a demand letter. Mitigate by mixing 5–10% general instruction data into training, and by keeping the fine-tuned model scoped to its task in deployment rather than serving as your general assistant. Data leakage: near-duplicates that straddle your train/eval split make your evals a happy lie — deduplicate across the split, not only within the training set. And a variant worth naming for any professional field: whatever is in the training set is, in a diffuse and unpredictable way, in the weights. Confidential client material does not belong in a model you might later share.

⚙️In practice

The 2026 tooling map, so the names in every tutorial slot into place. Hugging Face PEFT implements the adapter zoo (LoRA, DoRA, IA³, prompt/prefix tuning) as wrappers around any transformer; TRL supplies the training loops (its SFTTrainer handles chat templates, packing, and completion-only masking — i.e., most of the bugs above); bitsandbytes provides NF4 and the paged optimizers. Axolotl and LLaMA-Factory wrap all of that behind a single YAML config and are the fastest route from dataset to trained adapter. Unsloth re-implements the hot paths with fused kernels for roughly 2× faster, meaningfully lighter single-GPU LoRA/QLoRA training and is a community favorite on consumer cards. If you would rather not touch a GPU at all, managed services — Together, Fireworks, and the closed labs’ fine-tuning endpoints — run (typically LoRA) training on uploaded data and serve the result per-token, with the multi-adapter economics described earlier. Prices and speed rankings here are fast-moving; the shape of the stack has been stable for a couple of years.

🕰History

The arc is short and satisfying. 2019: Houlsby et al.’s adapters prove that frozen-base adaptation works at all (on BERT-scale models). 2020: Aghajanyan et al. explain why — intrinsic dimensionality collapses as pretraining improves. 2021: Hu et al. at Microsoft, working on GPT-3 — a model nobody could afford to fully fine-tune per customer — make the update low-rank, parallel, and mergeable, and LoRA quietly becomes the most deployed idea in applied natural language processing. 2023: Dettmers et al.’s QLoRA collides with the Llama open-weight moment and ignites the hobbyist fine-tuning scene — 65B on one GPU meant a lab of one could customize a near-frontier model. 2024–2026: refinements (DoRA, rsLoRA), serving systems (S-LoRA), and a settling of defaults; LoRA also escapes fine-tuning entirely, becoming standard for personalizing diffusion models and common as the trainable component in preference-tuning setups like DPO on modest hardware.

Where this leaves us

  • You can choose the right tool: retrieval for knowledge, prompting for anything a prompt can fix, fine-tuning for narrow, stable, high-volume style, format, and skill — and you can say why fine-tuning for facts usually disappoints.
  • You can reproduce the memory ledger: 16 bytes per trainable parameter (bf16 weights + grads, fp32 Adam states and master copy) → 128 GB for an 8B model; freezing kills 14 of those bytes, quantizing the frozen base to NF4 kills most of the rest → ≈ 17 GB (LoRA) and ≈ 5 GB (QLoRA).
  • You can state the low-rank hypothesis, back it with the intrinsic- dimensionality evidence, and write the mechanics from memory: W′=W0+αrBAW' = W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r}BAW′=W0​+rα​BA, AAA Gaussian, BBB zero, both trained, base frozen; for a 4096×4096 matrix at r=16r=16r=16 that is 131k trainable parameters standing in for 16.8M.
  • You know the field-tested configuration folklore — all linear layers, rrr in the 8–64 flat zone, α=2r\alpha=2rα=2r, learning rate ~10× full fine-tuning — and what merging buys (zero inference latency) versus what staying unmerged buys (one base serving hundreds of adapters).
  • You can explain QLoRA’s three tricks — NF4’s normal-quantile code levels, double quantization of the quantization constants, paged optimizers — and why gradients can flow through 4-bit frozen weights into 16-bit adapters.
  • You can place the rest of the PEFT zoo (adapters, prefix, prompt tuning, IA³, DoRA) by where each puts its trainable parameters.
  • You could run the weekend recipe — and you know its four classic failure modes: chat-template mismatch, catastrophic forgetting, format overfitting, and cross-split data leakage.

Your adapter is trained and merged. Now it has to answer users — fast, cheap, and in parallel — which is a different engineering discipline entirely: Next: Inference: Sampling, Quantization, Serving.

Key papers & readings in this chapter
  • LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models — Hu et al., 2021 The paper this chapter works through end to end; Section 4 has the method, Section 7 the rank experiments.
  • QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs — Dettmers et al., 2023 NF4, double quantization, paged optimizers — the paper that put 65B fine-tuning on one GPU.
  • Intrinsic Dimensionality Explains the Effectiveness of Language Model Fine-Tuning — Aghajanyan et al., 2020 The evidence that task adaptation lives in a shockingly low-dimensional subspace — the "why LoRA should work at all".
  • Parameter-Efficient Transfer Learning for NLP — Houlsby et al., 2019 The original adapter-layers paper; the ancestor of the whole PEFT family.
  • Prefix-Tuning: Optimizing Continuous Prompts for Generation — Li and Liang, 2021 Steering a frozen model by learning virtual key/value prefixes at every layer.
  • The Power of Scale for Parameter-Efficient Prompt Tuning — Lester et al., 2021 Soft prompts at the input only — and the finding that they close the gap as models grow.
  • DoRA: Weight-Decomposed Low-Rank Adaptation — Liu et al., 2024 Splits weights into magnitude and direction; the strongest of the LoRA successors.
  • LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less — Biderman et al., 2024 The careful empirical comparison of LoRA vs full fine-tuning — read before believing folklore in either direction.
