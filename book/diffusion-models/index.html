Diffusion and Image/Video Generation

How diffusion models generate images and video by learning to undo noise — from the forward process and ε-prediction through DDIM, classifier-free guidance, latent diffusion, DiT, and flow matching.

  8. The landscape in 2026: image, video, and the autoregressive rival

Every model in this book so far generates data the same way: one token at a time, left to right, each choice conditioned on everything before it. That factorization is natural for language, which really is produced as a sequence. It is much less natural for a photograph. A 512×512 image is 786,432 numbers with no privileged reading order, and its structure is spatial: the sky has to agree with the lighting on the ground, both eyes have to point the same way, and a mistake in the top-left corner can invalidate the bottom-right. For most of the 2010s the field’s answer was the generative adversarial network (GAN) — a generator network trained to fool a discriminator — which could produce stunning faces but was notoriously unstable to train and prone to mode collapse: quietly dropping whole regions of the data distribution it found hard.

Diffusion models solved image generation with an idea that sounds almost too modest to work: don’t learn to create; learn to clean up. Destroying an image with noise is trivial — sprinkle Gaussian static on it, over and over, until nothing remains but static. Diffusion models train a network to undo one small sprinkle. That’s the whole trick. Generation is then running the cleanup a few hundred times starting from pure noise: static becomes vague blobs, blobs become composition, composition becomes a photograph. Between 2020 and 2022 this recipe went from a curiosity to DALL·E 2, Imagen, and Stable Diffusion; by 2024 it had absorbed the transformer, been reformulated as flow matching, and become the engine of video generation; in 2026 it is the second great pillar of generative AI alongside next-token prediction — and this chapter gives it the same depth of treatment.

The plan: the forward (noising) process and its convenient closed form; why training collapses to a plain regression loss; how sampling went from 1000 steps to 50 to 1; classifier-free guidance, the knob every image tool exposes; latent diffusion, which made all of this affordable; the U-Net-to-transformer shift and flow matching; and the 2026 landscape of image and video models, including the autoregressive rival that is converging with diffusion inside omni-models.

The idea in plain English

Start with why generating in one shot is hard. A network asked to map a random seed directly to a finished photograph must make every decision at once — global layout, object identities, textures, lighting — with one forward pass and no ability to revise. GANs do exactly this, which is part of why their training is fragile: the generator must land on the data distribution in a single leap, and the only signal it gets is a discriminator that is itself being trained and can be fooled in degenerate ways.

Now consider the opposite problem. I hand you a photograph with a tiny amount of Gaussian noise added — say 1% static — and ask you to estimate the clean version. This is nearly trivial: the answer looks almost exactly like the input, and local smoothing plus weak knowledge of what images look like gets you most of the way. It stays a well-posed regression problem at every noise level. At 50% noise the network can no longer recover fine detail, but it can still say “this region is probably sky, this blob is probably a dog,” and predict the average of all plausible clean images. At 99% noise it can barely do better than predicting gray — but “barely better than gray” is still a learnable target.

Diffusion chains these easy problems together. Define a corruption ladder from clean image (rung 0) to pure noise (rung TTT, traditionally 1000). Train one network, told which rung it is on, to step down a single rung. To generate: sample pure static, which is by construction exactly what rung TTT looks like, then descend the ladder. Early steps make coarse, low-commitment guesses (overall layout, palette); later steps refine edges and textures. Every individual step is an easy, well-posed denoising problem, and errors made early can be revised later because the image stays “molten” — partially noisy — until the very end.

💡Intuition

Three mental models, all accurate:

Sculpting: one-shot generation is carving a statue with a single hammer blow; diffusion is a thousand light taps, each guided by “make this look slightly more like a real image.”

Curriculum: the model never faces the impossible task “produce a photograph from nothing.” It only ever faces “improve this slightly,” at every level of degradation — a curriculum of sub-problems, each barely harder than the identity function.

Distribution morphing: the noising process gradually deforms the complicated distribution of natural images into a single standard Gaussian — a distribution we can sample from exactly. The model learns the reverse deformation. Generation = sample from the easy distribution, apply the learned morph. (Gaussians are doing the same job here they did in the probability chapter: the one distribution whose math cooperates at every step.)

🕰History

The idea has two independent roots. Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015) imported the noising-and-reversing framework from non-equilibrium thermodynamics — five years too early for the hardware and tricks needed to make it shine. Separately, Song & Ermon developed score-based models: learn the gradient of the data’s log-density, then follow it out of noise. Ho, Jain & Abbeel (2020) — the denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPM) paper — found the simplifications that made the thermodynamics version work, and Song et al. (2020) then proved the two roots were the same tree: both are discretizations of one continuous-time process. Dhariwal & Nichol (2021) showed diffusion beating GANs on image quality outright, and the field flipped almost overnight.

The forward process: destroying images on purpose

The forward process is fixed — no learning, no parameters. Starting from a data sample x0x_0x0​ (an image, flattened into a vector of pixel values scaled to roughly [−1,1][-1, 1][−1,1]), we produce a sequence x1,x2,…,xTx_1, x_2, \dots, x_Tx1​,x2​,…,xT​ by repeatedly adding a small amount of Gaussian noise while slightly shrinking what remains:

Why does the schedule matter? Because it decides how the model’s capacity is spent. Steps where αˉt\bar\alpha_tαˉt​ changes fast are steps where the model must do a lot of work; a schedule that lingers near αˉ≈1\bar\alpha \approx 1αˉ≈1 wastes training on nearly-clean images where denoising is trivial. The linear schedule above destroys information too aggressively at the start; the cosine schedule (Nichol & Dhariwal, 2021) spends more time at moderate noise levels and trains noticeably better. Modern systems tune schedules per resolution — larger images need noisier schedules, because at high resolution the same per-pixel noise destroys less global structure (neighboring pixels average it away).

Learning to reverse it

The reverse process is where the parameters live. We want pθ(xt−1∣xt)p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)pθ​(xt−1​∣xt​): given a noisy image at rung ttt, produce a slightly cleaner one at rung t−1t-1t−1. A key fact makes this learnable: when the per-step noise βt\beta_tβt​ is small, the true reverse conditional is itself approximately Gaussian. So the network doesn’t need to represent some wild multimodal distribution per step — it needs to output the mean of a Gaussian (the variance can be fixed to a schedule-derived constant), and the hard, multimodal part of generation emerges from composing hundreds of these small Gaussian steps.

Here is where the famous simplification enters. The principled way to train pθp_\thetapθ​ is maximum likelihood via the evidence lower bound (ELBO) — the same variational objective used by variational autoencoders. Writing out the ELBO for this chain (we will not grind through it; Ho et al. do, in their Section 3) produces a sum over timesteps of Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergences, one per rung, each comparing the model’s reverse step against the true reverse step. Because everything is Gaussian, each KL term collapses — via the KL-between-Gaussians formula from Chapter 7 — into a squared distance between means, with a ttt-dependent weighting factor out front. And after a change of variables, “predict the mean” becomes “predict the noise”:

∑The math

Parameterize the network as εθ(xt,t)\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)εθ​(xt​,t) — “given the noisy image and the rung number, estimate the noise ε\varepsilonε that was mixed in.” The ELBO then reduces (exactly) to a weighted denoising objective:

Lsimple=Et, x0, ε[ ∥ε−εθ(xt,t)∥2 ].\mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}} = \E_{t,\, x_0,\, \varepsilon} \left[\, \| \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \|^2 \,\right].Lsimple​=Et,x0​,ε​[∥ε−εθ​(xt​,t)∥2].

This is plain mean-squared-error (MSE) regression — the first loss you met in Chapter 9 — on a target you construct yourself for free. The honest summary: diffusion training is maximum likelihood, up to a reweighting of timesteps, and the reweighting is a feature. The true wtw_twt​ puts enormous weight on low-noise steps (which barely affect perceptual quality); uniform weighting redirects effort toward the middle noise levels where composition is decided. Samples get better; measured log-likelihood gets slightly worse; nobody minds.

One training iteration is therefore: sample an image x0x_0x0​ from the dataset, a uniform random rung ttt, a noise vector ε\varepsilonε; form xtx_txt​ with the one-line jump formula; run the network once; take a gradient step on the MSE. No adversary, no instability — the loss curve goes down like a supervised model’s, which after the GAN years felt like a miracle.

💡Intuition

There is a deeper reading of εθ\varepsilon_\thetaεθ​, from the score-based side of the family: the (negated, scaled) noise prediction equals the score — the gradient ∇xtlog⁡q(xt)\nabla_{x_t} \log q(x_t)∇xt​​logq(xt​) of the log-density of noisy images. In words: at every noise level, the network has learned a compass that points from where you are toward “more probable images.” Sampling is compass-following with controlled randomness. Hold onto this; classifier-free guidance is about to edit the compass.

To see the reverse process do something a one-shot model finds awkward, shrink the problem to one dimension. Let the data distribution have two modes — values cluster near −2-2−2 or +2+2+2, nothing in between (think “this pixel is either dark or bright”). Noising blurs the two humps into one broad Gaussian. Now run the reverse process and watch the crowd of samples:

This toy exposes the mechanism worth remembering: a single denoising prediction is an average over the plausible answers, and averages of valid images are invalid images (the mean of “either dark or bright” is gray; the mean of all possible dogs is fog). Diffusion never needs the average to be a valid sample — it only needs it to be a good direction, because injected noise plus many small steps lets each trajectory commit to one mode. That is the precise sense in which iterative generation beats one-shot.

Sampling: from 1000 steps to 1

DDPM sampling is the forward process run backwards with the network in the loop: start at xT∼N(0,I)x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)xT​∼N(0,I), and repeat for t=T,…,1t = T, \dots, 1t=T,…,1:

xt−1=1αt(xt−βt1−αˉt εθ(xt,t))+σtz,z∼N(0,I),x_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\right) + \sigma_t z, \qquad z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I),xt−1​=αt​​1​(xt​−1−αˉt​​βt​​εθ​(xt​,t))+σt​z,z∼N(0,I),

where the first term is “subtract the predicted noise, appropriately scaled, and rescale” and σtz\sigma_t zσt​z re-injects a controlled amount of fresh noise (the stochasticity that lets trajectories split, as in the toy above). It works, at a cost of T≈1000T \approx 1000T≈1000 full network evaluations per image — in 2020, minutes per sample. Three ideas, in escalating ambition, attacked that cost:

DDIM (denoising diffusion implicit models; Song, Meng & Ermon, 2020) observed that many different step-by-step processes share the same marginals q(xt∣x0)q(x_t \mid x_0)q(xt​∣x0​) — including a fully deterministic one. The DDIM sampler uses the network’s implied clean-image estimate x^0\hat{x}_0x^0​ (the flip we computed in the example above) to jump between rungs without injecting noise, using the same trained network, no retraining. It tolerates skipping: 50 evaluations instead of 1000 with minor quality loss. Determinism also gave diffusion a latent space: one noise seed maps to one image, so you can interpolate seeds and edit reproducibly. The continuous-time view explains why this works: sampling is numerically solving an ordinary differential equation (ODE), and better solvers (DPM-Solver and friends, 2022+) take bigger accurate steps — 20–30 evaluations became the practical default.

Distillation trains a new network to compress the sampler. In progressive distillation (Salimans & Ho, 2022), a student learns to reproduce two teacher steps in one; repeat, halving each round: 1000 → 500 → … → 4 steps.

Consistency models (Song et al., 2023) aim directly at one step: train a network mapping any point on a noising trajectory straight to that trajectory’s clean endpoint, enforcing that all points on one trajectory give consistent answers. One to four evaluations, near-real-time generation — this family (with adversarial-assisted variants) is what powers “instant” image tools and the interactive draft modes of 2025–26 products.

⚙️In practice

Nobody ships 1000-step DDPM in 2026. Typical settings: quality-tier image generation runs 20–50 solver steps; fast tiers run distilled or consistency-style models at 1–8 steps (Flux’s “schnell” variant, SDXL-Turbo lineage, and the draft modes in commercial apps). Video models, where each step is enormously expensive, lean hard on distillation. The training-vs-serving asymmetry mirrors large language models (LLMs): you pay for a big slow teacher once, then serve cheap students — the same logic as distillation and quantization for LLM inference.

Classifier-free guidance: the knob everyone turns

Everything so far generates unconditional samples — “an image.” Products need “an image of a corgi in a spacesuit, watercolor.” Conditioning on a text prompt ccc is architecturally straightforward (feed an embedding of ccc into the network; details in the latent-diffusion section). But a plainly conditioned model, trained on noisy internet captions, obeys the prompt only loosely — captions are weak labels, and the model happily hedges toward generic imagery.

The fix is one of the highest leverage-per-line tricks in modern AI. Classifier-free guidance (CFG) — Ho & Salimans, 2022 — trains a single network in two modes: during training, drop the prompt some fraction of the time (10–20%), replacing it with a null token ∅\varnothing∅. The one network then knows both εθ(xt,c)\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, c)εθ​(xt​,c) (“denoise toward images matching the caption”) and εθ(xt,∅)\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, \varnothing)εθ​(xt​,∅) (“denoise toward images in general”). At sampling time, compare them — and exaggerate the difference:

∑The math
ε~=εθ(xt,∅)+s⋅(εθ(xt,c)−εθ(xt,∅)).\tilde\varepsilon = \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, \varnothing) + s \cdot \big(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, c) - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, \varnothing)\big).ε~=εθ​(xt​,∅)+s⋅(εθ​(xt​,c)−εθ​(xt​,∅)).

The difference vector εθ(xt,c)−εθ(xt,∅)\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, c) - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, \varnothing)εθ​(xt​,c)−εθ​(xt​,∅) isolates what the prompt changes about the denoising direction, with everything generic subtracted out. The guidance scale sss then extrapolates along it: s=0s = 0s=0 ignores the prompt, s=1s = 1s=1 is the plain conditional model, and s>1s > 1s>1 — always, in practice — pushes past the conditional prediction, away from the unconditional one. In score language (our compass): since ∇log⁡p(c∣xt)=∇log⁡p(xt∣c)−∇log⁡p(xt)\nabla \log p(c \mid x_t) = \nabla \log p(x_t \mid c) - \nabla \log p(x_t)∇logp(c∣xt​)=∇logp(xt​∣c)−∇logp(xt​) by Bayes’ rule, guidance adds an amplified “does this look like the caption?” gradient — a classifier’s opinion, extracted from the generative model itself with no classifier trained. Equivalently, it samples from a sharpened distribution ∝p(x∣c) p(c∣x) s−1\propto p(x \mid c)\, p(c \mid x)^{\,s-1}∝p(x∣c)p(c∣x)s−1: images that are not merely consistent with the prompt but recognizably so.

One component of the noise prediction: unconditional says ε∅=0.10\varepsilon_\varnothing = 0.10ε∅​=0.10, conditional says εc=0.30\varepsilon_c = 0.30εc​=0.30. The prompt-specific difference is 0.200.200.20. With guidance scale s=5s = 5s=5: ε~=0.10+5×0.20=1.10\tilde\varepsilon = 0.10 + 5 \times 0.20 = 1.10ε~=0.10+5×0.20=1.10 — over three times the conditional prediction. That selectivity is why CFG improves prompt adherence so much more than it distorts — up to a point. A prediction of magnitude 1.10, when typical noise components are order-1, is how oversaturation begins.

⚠️Careful

The guidance scale is a genuine tradeoff dial, not a quality dial. Raising sss improves prompt adherence and apparent polish — until it degrades into the “CFG look”: oversaturated colors, burned highlights, waxy over-rendered detail, and collapsing diversity (every sample converging on the same prototypical composition, for the same mode-sharpening reason the math above predicts). Classic Stable-Diffusion-era models ran s≈7s \approx 7s≈7–121212; modern flow-based models with better conditioning run much lower (≈2\approx 2≈2–555, and Flux’s distilled variants bake guidance into the weights so the user knob disappears entirely). If your generations look like an HDR fever dream, turn sss down.

Latent diffusion: making it affordable

Running hundreds of denoising passes over raw pixels is brutally expensive, and — worse — most of that expense buys nothing. The overwhelming majority of bits in a 512×512 image are perceptually irrelevant micro-texture: exact grain positions, imperceptible color jitter. Spending a giant network’s forward pass deciding them, hundreds of times per image, is waste.

Latent diffusion (Rombach et al., 2021 — the architecture of Stable Diffusion) splits the job in two. First train a variational autoencoder (VAE): an encoder E\mathcal{E}E that compresses an image into a small grid of latent vectors, and a decoder D\mathcal{D}D trained to reconstruct the image from them, with perceptual losses so reconstructions look right rather than merely matching pixel-wise. Then run the entire diffusion process in latent space: noise the latents, train the denoiser on latents, sample latents from noise — and only at the very end, decode once to pixels.

✏️Worked example

The economics, computed. A 512×512 RGB image is 512×512×3=786,432512 \times 512 \times 3 = 786{,}432512×512×3=786,432 numbers. Stable Diffusion’s VAE downsamples 8× spatially into 4 channels: 64×64×4=16,38464 \times 64 \times 4 = 16{,}38464×64×4=16,384 numbers — 48× fewer. Every one of the ~50 denoising passes now runs on a figure 48× smaller. And it compounds: the denoiser’s attention layers scale quadratically in the number of spatial positions (see the KV-cache chapter for why), and 642=409664^2 = 4096642=4096 positions versus 5122=262,144512^2 = 262{,}1445122=262,144 positions is a 64×64\times64× reduction per side of the attention matrix — a ∼4096×{\sim}4096\times∼4096× smaller attention computation. Latent diffusion is the difference between “data center” and “gaming GPU” (graphics processing unit), and it is why Stable Diffusion running on consumer hardware in August 2022 detonated an ecosystem.

The second thing the latent-diffusion paper standardized is text conditioning via cross-attention — and here Chapter 22 pays off again. The prompt is encoded by a frozen text encoder (originally the text tower of CLIP — Contrastive Language–Image Pre-training; T5-class and LLM encoders in later systems) into a sequence of embeddings. Inside the denoiser, interleaved cross-attention layers let every spatial position query that sequence: queries come from image latents, keys and values from the text — the same QK⊤VQK\T VQK⊤V machinery as translation, with “which source word do I look at while producing this word” becoming “which prompt phrase do I consult while denoising this patch.” A patch being denoised into fur attends to “corgi”; a patch near the top of the canvas attends to “spacesuit helmet.” This is also where prompt-editing tricks live: attention maps between patches and words are interpretable enough that early Stable Diffusion tooling manipulated them directly to move or swap objects.

✎Note

The VAE is a quiet dependency of everything downstream: if the decoder cannot render text glyphs or fine faces from 4-channel latents, no amount of diffusion skill fixes it. One consistent trend in 2024–26 systems is fatter latents (16 channels in Stable Diffusion 3 and Flux versus Stable Diffusion 1’s 4) precisely to raise this ceiling. When you see a diffusion model suddenly get much better at typography, suspect the autoencoder as much as the denoiser.

From U-Net to DiT — and from diffusion to flow

Two questions remain about the denoiser: what architecture, and what objective. Both answers changed after 2022, in ways that rhyme with the rest of this book.

Architecture. The original denoiser was a U-Net, a convolutional encoder-decoder from 2015-era image segmentation (a direct descendant of the convolutional neural network (CNN) lineage): downsample the image through convolution stages, upsample back, with skip connections carrying fine-grained detail from each resolution across to its mirror stage — drawing the letter U. It has strong built-in spatial priors and served through Stable Diffusion 1/2/XL, Imagen, and DALL·E 2. But by now you can guess the next move, because it is the same move transformers made everywhere else: replace the hand-designed inductive bias with a generic sequence model and let scale learn the priors. The Diffusion Transformer (DiT) (Peebles & Xie, 2022) chops the latent grid into patches, embeds each patch as a token (a 64×64 latent with 2×2 patches becomes a 1024-token sequence), and runs a standard transformer over them, with the timestep and conditioning injected by scaling and shifting each block’s normalization layers (“adaLN” — adaptive LayerNorm). Its headline result was an LLM-shaped one: sample quality tracked training compute smoothly across model sizes — scaling-law behavior, no U-Net required. The consequences compounded: DiTs inherit the entire transformer toolchain (the parallelism and kernel work of Chapter 33), handle variable resolutions and aspect ratios by varying sequence length, and — decisively — extend to video by adding a time axis to the patch grid. Every frontier image and video system of 2024+ (SD3, Flux, Sora, Veo) is transformer-based.

Objective. The 2024+ reformulation is flow matching (Lipman et al., 2022; the closely related rectified flow of Liu et al., 2022). Drop the Markov-chain scaffolding and ask directly: what is the simplest path from noise to data? A straight line. It is the same “predict how to move toward data” idea with the curved, schedule-laden trajectory of DDPM replaced by straight lines — and straight target paths mean a well-behaved velocity field that coarse solvers handle in few steps, plus no βt\beta_tβt​/αˉt\bar\alpha_tαˉt​ bookkeeping anywhere. Stable Diffusion 3 (SD3) validated the recipe head-to-head against diffusion objectives at scale; Flux, and the major video models since, are flow-matching DiTs. When you read a 2026 systems paper, “diffusion” almost always means, concretely: flow matching, in a VAE latent space, with a transformer.

⚙️In practice

The 2026 default text-to-image stack, concretely: a 16-channel VAE (8× spatial downsampling); a DiT of roughly 2–12 billion parameters (SD3 ships at 2B and 8B; Flux at 12B) processing joint sequences of text and image tokens; text encoded by CLIP plus a T5-class or LLM encoder; rectified-flow training with the timestep sampling biased toward middle noise levels; sampled in ~20–30 steps at guidance 2–5, or 1–8 steps for distilled variants. Parameter counts stay small next to frontier LLMs — generation cost is dominated by the many forward passes per output, not by parameters.

The landscape in 2026: image, video, and the autoregressive rival

Image. The open lineage runs Stable Diffusion 1.x → SDXL → SD3/3.5, with Black Forest Labs’ Flux family (founded by Stable Diffusion’s original authors) the strongest open-weights line; both are rectified-flow DiTs. Google’s Imagen lineage (Saharia et al., 2022) proved early that scaling the text encoder matters more than scaling the image model — prompt understanding was the bottleneck — and its descendants power image generation in Gemini. Midjourney remains the closed aesthetic-first house. And OpenAI’s GPT-4o-native image generation (March 2025) marked a genuine fork: images generated autoregressively by the omni-model itself — the multimodal-models chapter’s “one transformer, many modalities” thesis applied to output — reportedly with a diffusion-style decoder rendering final pixels from the transformer’s image tokens (details unpublished). Its standout strengths — following intricate multi-part instructions, rendering long correct text, iterating conversationally on an image — come from the LLM side of the marriage, and pushed the whole field toward tighter LLM-generator integration.

Video. A video clip is a stack of frames with brutal token math: at LLM-ish granularity, one minute of video is measured in the hundreds of thousands to millions of tokens. The architecture that made it tractable is the image recipe plus one axis: a causal 3D (spatiotemporal) VAE compresses across time as well as space (typical factors ~4× temporal, 8× spatial), the latent volume is chopped into space-time patches, and a flow-matching DiT attends across the whole volume — so a patch late in the clip can attend to any patch early on, which is where temporal coherence and the models’ intuitive, if unreliable, physics come from. OpenAI’s Sora (February 2024) demonstrated the recipe and framed video models as “world simulators”; by 2026 the frontier tier — Google’s Veo 3 (native synchronized audio+video generation), OpenAI’s Sora line, Kuaishou’s Kling, Runway’s Gen series, and strong open-weights entries from China (Wan, HunyuanVideo) — produces coherent clips on the order of tens of seconds, at costs orders of magnitude above image generation; per-clip economics, not quality, is the main brake on deployment. Numbers here are the fastest-moving in this book — treat all of them as a 2026 snapshot.

Convergence. So there are two live paradigms: diffusion/flow (parallel refinement of the whole canvas) and autoregression (sequential token emission, unified with the LLM). The 2026 trajectory is not one defeating the other but convergence inside omni-models: an LLM backbone plans and conditions; a diffusion-style decoder renders pixels; interleaved image-text-audio training ties them together. Traffic flows the other way too: diffusion language models — text generation by iterative unmasking rather than left-to-right emission, with Google’s Gemini Diffusion (2025) the most prominent demo — bet that parallel refinement can beat autoregression on speed for text itself. Whether next-token or denoise-everything wins any given modality looks increasingly like an engineering question about parallelism and cost, not a philosophical one.

Evaluation, briefly, because it shapes the leaderboards you see. The standard automatic metric is Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) (Heusel et al., 2017): embed large sets of real and generated images with an ImageNet-trained Inception network, fit a Gaussian to each embedding cloud, and measure the distance between the Gaussians — lower is better. Its limits are structural: it measures distributional similarity in a 2015-era classifier’s feature space, so it is blind to prompt adherence, weak on aesthetics and text rendering, and saturates precisely where frontier models compete. CLIP-based scores add a prompt-alignment check but inherit CLIP’s own blind spots. So frontier comparison has migrated — exactly as LLM evaluation did — to human preference: head-to-head arena voting (text-to-image and video arenas with Elo-style ratings) plus targeted probes for compositional prompts, typography, and physics. The same caveats from the benchmarks chapter apply: preference voting rewards instant visual appeal, which is not identical to correctness, controllability, or physical plausibility.

✎Note

An honest open question to carry out of this chapter: why few-step generation works as well as it does is better established empirically than theoretically. The clean story — ELBO, exact reverse-time equations — justifies many small steps; consistency training and aggressive distillation discard most of that justification and mostly keep the quality. As with much of deep learning, the theory explains why the method is reasonable, and the recipe outruns the theory.

Where this leaves us

  • Diffusion replaces “generate in one shot” with “iteratively undo small corruptions”: every step is an easy regression problem, and multimodal structure emerges from composing hundreds of near-Gaussian steps.
  • The forward process is fixed Gaussian corruption; αˉt\bar\alpha_tαˉt​ tracks surviving signal variance, and xt=αˉt x0+1−αˉt εx_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilonxt​=αˉt​​x0​+1−αˉt​​ε lets training jump to any noise level in one line.
  • Training is plain MSE on predicting the injected noise — the ELBO reduces to a weighted denoising loss, and dropping the weights (Ho et al., 2020) is a deliberate, beneficial reweighting toward the noise levels that decide composition.
  • Sampling cost fell three ways: DDIM’s deterministic skipping (~50 steps, same network), better ODE solvers (~20–30), and distillation/consistency models (1–8).
  • Classifier-free guidance extrapolates from the unconditional to the conditional prediction with scale sss; it is a prompt-adherence dial with a known failure mode (oversaturation, collapsed diversity) at high sss.
  • Latent diffusion moves the whole process into a VAE’s latent space — 786,432 pixel values become 16,384 latent values, a 48× reduction before attention’s quadratic savings — which is what put image generation on consumer GPUs.
  • The modern stack is flow matching (straight-line noise→data paths, velocity prediction) with a DiT backbone; video adds a temporal axis to the VAE and patch grid, at compute costs that still dominate deployment decisions.
  • Diffusion and autoregression are converging inside omni-models: LLMs provide understanding and control, diffusion-style decoders render pixels — and diffusion is making a countermove into text.

Diffusion transformers attending over hundreds of thousands of space-time patches share a bottleneck with LLMs digesting entire codebases: attention over very long sequences. That is the next chapter: Long Context.
