Neurons, Layers, and MLPs

How a single neuron computes, why stacking linear layers collapses to one, and how sums of ReLU hinges let a network approximate any function.

In this chapter
  1. One neuron: multiply, add, squash
  2. A layer: many neurons in parallel
  3. Stacking layers — and why linearity collapses
  4. Universal approximation: any function from hinges
  5. What hidden layers learn
  6. Where MLPs live now: inside every transformer
  7. Where this leaves us

Part I gave you a toolbox: vectors as points with meaning, matrices as transformations, dot products as similarity meters, gradients as improvement directions, probability as the language of prediction. This chapter is where the toolbox becomes a machine. We are going to build the simplest possible neural network — the multi-layer perceptron (MLP) — from a single moving part, and by the end you will know exactly what every arrow in every network diagram you have ever squinted at computes.

Here is the plan. First, one artificial neuron: a weighted sum, a threshold, a squash — about one line of arithmetic, and you will do that arithmetic by hand. Then many neurons in parallel, which is nothing but the matrix multiplication from Chapter 2. Then layers stacked on layers, where we hit the chapter’s central surprise: stacking linear layers is pointless, because any stack of them collapses into a single layer. One small nonlinear function, wedged between the layers, rescues everything — and we will prove, graphically and by hand, that with it a network can approximate almost any function you can draw.

The payoff is not historical. The MLP is not a stepping stone the field abandoned on the way to transformers; it is roughly two-thirds of every transformer. When you reach the transformer, piece by piece, the block labeled “feed-forward network” will be — parameter for parameter, the biggest part of the model — exactly the object built in this chapter.

One neuron: multiply, add, squash

An artificial neuron takes a vector of inputs x=(x1,…,xn)x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)x=(x1​,…,xn​) and produces a single number. It does so in three steps that you can hold in your head permanently:

  1. Multiply: scale each input xix_ixi​ by a weight wiw_iwi​.
  2. Add: sum the scaled inputs and add a bias bbb.
  3. Squash: pass the total through a nonlinear activation function φ\varphiφ.

In symbols:

a  =  φ(w⋅x+b)  =  φ ⁣(∑i=1nwixi+b),a \;=\; \varphi\big(w \cdot x + b\big) \;=\; \varphi\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i + b\right),a=φ(w⋅x+b)=φ(i=1∑n​wi​xi​+b),

where w=(w1,…,wn)w = (w_1, \dots, w_n)w=(w1​,…,wn​) is the weight vector (learned), bbb is the bias (learned, a single number), φ\varphiφ is a fixed nonlinear function chosen by the designer, and aaa is the neuron’s output, its activation. The quantity inside the parentheses, z=w⋅x+bz = w \cdot x + bz=w⋅x+b, is called the pre-activation — you will meet it constantly in later chapters, because it is where normalization and initialization analysis happens.

Each piece of this formula has a geometric meaning you already own from Part I. Let’s pin each one down before we scale up.

💡Intuition

The weight vector is a learned direction, and the neuron is a similarity detector. In Chapter 1 you saw that the dot product w⋅xw \cdot xw⋅x is large when xxx points the same way as www, near zero when they are perpendicular, and negative when they oppose. So a neuron is a tiny pattern-matcher: www is the pattern, written as a direction in input space, and w⋅xw \cdot xw⋅x scores how strongly the current input contains that pattern. The bias is the threshold: since φ\varphiφ typically “turns on” around z=0z = 0z=0, the neuron fires when w⋅x>−bw \cdot x > -bw⋅x>−b — the bias sets how much evidence the pattern-matcher demands before it speaks up. Training a network, which we get to in Chapter 10, is the process of choosing which patterns are worth detecting.

The activation function φ\varphiφ deserves — and gets — an entire chapter of its own. For now, two concrete choices are all we need. The sigmoid σ(z)=1/(1+e−z)\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})σ(z)=1/(1+e−z) squashes any real number into (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1), like a soft on/off switch. The rectified linear unit (ReLU), ReLU(z)=max⁡(0,z)\mathrm{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z)ReLU(z)=max(0,z), is even blunter: negative in, zero out; positive in, unchanged out. ReLU is the modern default, and its very bluntness is what will make the universal approximation argument later in this chapter something you can see.

✎Note

About the word “neuron.” The name comes from a real analogy — biological neurons collect weighted signals at their dendrites and fire when the total crosses a threshold, and the 1943 McCulloch–Pitts model plus Rosenblatt’s 1958 perceptron were honest attempts to formalize that. But the analogy stops early. Real neurons spike in time, adapt their own chemistry, run on dozens of neurotransmitters, and are wired by development rather than gradient descent; none of that is in the equation above. It is most accurate to think of the artificial neuron as loosely inspired by biology and then optimized for being differentiable and cheap on hardware. Nothing later in this book depends on the brain analogy, and when the analogy and the math disagree, the math is what the model actually does.

A layer: many neurons in parallel

One neuron gives one number — one pattern-match score. Interesting inputs deserve many scores at once: this input is bright and round and not moving. So we run mmm neurons on the same input in parallel, each with its own weight vector and bias, and stack their outputs into a vector. That stack of neurons is a layer (also called a fully connected or dense layer, or in framework code a Linear layer plus an activation).

Here the machinery of Chapter 2 snaps into place. Write neuron iii‘s weight vector as the iii-th row of a matrix WWW, and its bias as the iii-th entry of a vector bbb. Then all mmm dot products happen in one matrix–vector multiplication:

h  =  φ(Wx+b),h \;=\; \varphi\big(Wx + b\big),h=φ(Wx+b),

where x∈Rnx \in \R^{n}x∈Rn is the input, W∈Rm×nW \in \R^{m \times n}W∈Rm×n holds one neuron per row, b∈Rmb \in \R^{m}b∈Rm, and φ\varphiφ is applied elementwise — each neuron squashes its own pre-activation independently. A layer is not a new concept; it is mmm copies of the previous section running simultaneously, and matrix multiplication is the bookkeeping. This identity — rows of the weight matrix are neurons, and a layer is one matmul — is the single most useful fact for reading real model code, where you will never see a “neuron” object, only weight matrices.

Notice what a layer does geometrically: it takes a point in a 3-dimensional input space and re-describes it as a point in a 4-dimensional feature space, where each coordinate answers one learned question about the input. The layer changed the meaning of the axes, not just their values. That re-description is the move deep learning makes over and over.

Stacking layers — and why linearity collapses

If one re-description is good, several should be better: let the second layer’s questions be about the first layer’s answers. Feed the output of layer 1 into layer 2, and so on:

h(1)=φ(W(1)x+b(1)),h(2)=φ(W(2)h(1)+b(2)),y^=W(3)h(2)+b(3).h^{(1)} = \varphi\big(W^{(1)}x + b^{(1)}\big), \qquad h^{(2)} = \varphi\big(W^{(2)}h^{(1)} + b^{(2)}\big), \qquad \hat{y} = W^{(3)}h^{(2)} + b^{(3)}.h(1)=φ(W(1)x+b(1)),h(2)=φ(W(2)h(1)+b(2)),y^​=W(3)h(2)+b(3).

This stack is the multi-layer perceptron (MLP) — also called a feed-forward network, because information flows one way, input to output, with no loops. The layers between input and output are hidden layers: “hidden” only in the sense that the training data never specifies what their values should be — the network invents their meaning itself, a point we will return to. (The name “perceptron” is a historical fossil: Rosenblatt’s perceptron used a hard step-function threshold, which modern networks abandoned because a step has no useful gradient. The name stuck anyway.) Note the last layer above has no φ\varphiφ: the final layer’s job is usually to produce raw scores — logits for a classifier, real values for a regression — and squashing is left to the loss function or to softmax.

Before celebrating depth, we have to face an inconvenient theorem. Suppose we stacked layers without the activation function — pure linear maps, h=Wxh = Wxh=Wx (fold the bias in mentally; it does not change the story). Two layers would compute W(2)(W(1)x)W^{(2)}(W^{(1)}x)W(2)(W(1)x). But matrix multiplication is associative, so this is (W(2)W(1))x(W^{(2)}W^{(1)})x(W(2)W(1))x — a single matrix, applied once. The two-layer “deep” network is exactly equivalent to some one-layer network. By induction, so is a hundred-layer one.

This is why the nonlinearity is load-bearing. The humble φ\varphiφ wedged between layers is not a garnish; it is the only thing standing between “deep network” and “one matrix.” ReLU\mathrm{ReLU}ReLU breaks associativity: W(2) ReLU(W(1)x)W^{(2)}\,\mathrm{ReLU}(W^{(1)}x)W(2)ReLU(W(1)x) does not factor into any single matrix, because ReLU’s output depends on the sign pattern of its input — which inputs landed positive and which negative — and no linear map can express that conditional behavior. Universal approximation: any function from hinges

The XOR construction generalizes into one of the most quoted results in the field. The universal approximation theorem (Cybenko, 1989; Hornik, Stinchcombe & White, 1989) says: a network with one hidden layer and enough neurons can approximate any continuous function on a bounded region, to any accuracy you demand. Follow-up work (Hornik in 1991, then Leshno and colleagues in 1993) made clear the particular activation doesn’t matter — any non-polynomial nonlinearity works. The architecture (linear combinations of shifted, scaled squashes) is what is universal.

Stated abstractly it sounds mystical. With ReLU it is something you can draw.

💡Intuition

A one-hidden-layer ReLU network computes f^(x)=∑ivi ReLU(wix+bi)+c\hat{f}(x) = \sum_i v_i \,\mathrm{ReLU}(w_i x + b_i) + cf^​(x)=∑i​vi​ReLU(wi​x+bi​)+c — a weighted sum of hinges. Each hinge is a function that is flat at zero until a “knee” at x=−bi/wix = -b_i / w_ix=−bi​/wi​, then climbs (or descends) as a straight ramp. Adding hinges is like bending a wire: each hinge you add changes the wire’s slope at one chosen point, by a chosen amount, and leaves everything to the left of the knee untouched. Any curve you can draw can be approximated by a wire bent at enough points — trace it with short straight segments, and place one hinge at each bend. That is the whole theorem, minus epsilon bookkeeping: one hidden layer of ReLU neurons can build any piecewise-linear function, and piecewise-linear functions can trace any continuous curve as closely as you like.

What the theorem does and does not promise

The universal approximation theorem is routinely over-quoted, so let us be precise about its fine print.

It promises existence, not learnability. The theorem says the right weights exist somewhere in weight space. It says nothing about whether gradient descent will find them from a random start, how many training examples that would take, or whether the resulting network generalizes beyond the training set. Those are entirely separate questions — hard ones, occupying Chapter 12 and Chapter 13 — and the theorem’s proof techniques offer no help with them. A lookup table is also a universal approximator; nobody is impressed.

It is silent on cost, and the cost of width is brutal. For functions of one variable, our bump construction is cheap. For a function of nnn inputs, covering the domain with bumps requires a number of hidden units growing exponentially in nnn in the worst case — the curse of dimensionality from Chapter 3 wearing a new mask. A one-hidden-layer network that matches a real image classifier might need more neurons than there are atoms available to build the computer.

Depth is the escape hatch — in practice. Empirically, making networks deeper buys expressiveness far more cheaply than making them wider, and there is supporting theory: a deep ReLU network folds space repeatedly, and the number of distinct linear regions it can carve grows exponentially with depth but only polynomially with width (Montúfar et al., 2014). There are also explicit functions computable by a small deep network that provably require exponentially many neurons for any shallow one (Telgarsky’s sawtooth constructions are the standard example). The intuition: depth lets the network reuse computation — layer 5 does not rebuild “is there an edge here” from pixels; it composes the answer layer 1 already produced. Honest caveat: the theory of depth separations covers carefully constructed functions, not the functions real data happens to need, and “why exactly does depth help this much on natural data” remains an open research question. The empirical verdict, though, has been unambiguous since 2012.

What hidden layers learn

The collapse argument told us hidden layers are only useful because of nonlinearity; the bump construction told us what they can express. What do trained hidden layers actually contain?

The canonical story comes from vision networks, where you can look. In a trained image classifier, first-layer neurons respond to tiny local patterns — oriented edges, color gradients. Second- and third-layer neurons combine those into textures, corners, and simple motifs. Middle layers respond to object parts — an eye, a wheel arch, a patch of fur — and late layers to whole object categories. Zeiler and Fergus (2013) made this hierarchy famous with visualizations of what maximally excites neurons at each depth, and later work at OpenAI and Google (the “feature visualization” and early circuits literature) sharpened it. The slogan: hidden layers learn features of features — each layer’s neurons are pattern-detectors over the previous layer’s pattern-detections, which is exactly what our stacked equation h(2)=φ(W(2)h(1)+b(2))h^{(2)} = \varphi(W^{(2)}h^{(1)} + b^{(2)})h(2)=φ(W(2)h(1)+b(2)) says they must be.

✎Note

The honest caveat. The edge→shape→object story is real but cleaner in the telling than in the tensors. Many neurons in real networks are polysemantic — one neuron fires for several unrelated concepts — and many learned features do not correspond to anything a human would name. The reason, we now believe, is that networks pack more features than they have neurons by storing features as directions that share neurons, a phenomenon called superposition. Un-tangling it is a central project of modern interpretability, and it gets a full treatment in Chapter 50 and Chapter 51. For now: trust the hierarchy as a first approximation, not as a neuron-by-neuron catalog.

🕰History

Feature learning vs feature engineering: the 2012 shift. For decades, the recipe for machine perception was: humans design the features, the model does the last step. Computer-vision experts spent careers crafting descriptors (SIFT, the scale-invariant feature transform; HOG, histograms of oriented gradients) that turned pixels into vectors a linear classifier could use; speech had MFCCs (mel-frequency cepstral coefficients); natural language processing had hand-built parsers and word lists. The features were the product; the learning was a thin layer on top.

AlexNet (Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton, 2012) ended that. A deep network trained end-to-end on raw pixels — its features learned by gradient descent, not designed — cut the ImageNet top-5 error rate from roughly 26% (the best feature-engineered system) to about 15.3%, a gap larger than the sum of several previous years of hand-engineering progress. Within about three years, hand-crafted features had all but vanished from vision research. The lesson generalized far beyond vision and is arguably the founding lesson of the modern era: given enough data and compute, learned features beat designed features, because the network can discover regularities its designers did not know to look for. Every model in the rest of this book — transformers included — is an elaboration of that bet.

Where MLPs live now: inside every transformer

You might expect the MLP to be a pedagogical fossil — the thing you learn before the real architecture. The opposite is true. Strip a modern transformer to its skeleton and you find two alternating sublayers: attention (which moves information between positions — the subject of Part IV) and a feed-forward network (FFN) applied identically at every position — which is precisely a one-hidden-layer MLP, up-projection, nonlinearity, down-projection, as specified in Section 3.3 of “Attention Is All You Need”.

Interpretability work also suggests the FFN layers are where much of a model’s factual knowledge is stored — a thread picked up in Chapter 54.

⚠️Careful

Two vocabulary traps. First, in transformer papers “MLP block,” “FFN,” and “feed-forward sublayer” are the same object; do not go hunting for a difference. Second, a bare “MLP” in 2026 usage almost never means stacks-of-sigmoid-perceptrons: activations are GELU/SwiGLU-family, biases are often omitted entirely (Llama-style models set them to zero or drop the terms), and normalization layers are interleaved. The skeleton — linear, squash, linear — is unchanged from this chapter; the dressing evolves, and Chapter 15 covers the dressing.

Where this leaves us

  • You can compute a neuron by hand — a=φ(w⋅x+b)a = \varphi(w \cdot x + b)a=φ(w⋅x+b) — and read it geometrically: www is a learned direction, the dot product scores similarity to it, bbb sets the firing threshold, and one neuron draws one hyperplane.
  • A layer h=φ(Wx+b)h = \varphi(Wx + b)h=φ(Wx+b) is many neurons in parallel: one neuron per row of WWW, at a cost of mn+mmn + mmn+m parameters for nnn inputs and mmm outputs — you counted 16 for a 3→4 layer.
  • Stacked linear layers collapse: W(2)(W(1)x)=(W(2)W(1))xW^{(2)}(W^{(1)}x) = (W^{(2)}W^{(1)})xW(2)(W(1)x)=(W(2)W(1))x, verified with 2×2 numbers — so the elementwise nonlinearity is the load-bearing wall of deep learning, and with it two hidden ReLU units compute XOR exactly.
  • One hidden ReLU layer can build any piecewise-linear function by summing hinges — you built a bump from three — which is the universal approximation theorem made visible; but the theorem promises existence, not learnability, and shallow universality can cost exponentially many neurons, which is why practice chooses depth.
  • Since AlexNet in 2012, features are learned, not engineered — hidden layers discover a hierarchy of features-of-features, with the honest caveat that real neurons are messier (polysemantic, superposed) than the tidy edge→shape→object story.
  • The MLP is not superseded by transformers; it is most of one — the FFN sublayer holding roughly two-thirds of a dense transformer’s non-embedding parameters.
