Part VI · The Modern AI Landscape · Chapter 43

Long Context

What a million-token context window really buys you: the three costs, the engineering stack that pays them, honest evaluation from needle-in-a-haystack to RULER, and the economics of prompt caching.

  3. The stack: how a million-token window is actually built
  4. Evaluating long context honestly

  6. Long context versus RAG: complements, not rivals
  7. Context engineering: the agent preview

Somewhere in the last few years, a line in every model card quietly became one of the most consequential specs in AI: context length. GPT-3 shipped in 2020 with 2,048 tokens — a few pages. By 2026, frontier models advertise one or two million tokens, and at least one open model has claimed ten million. That is the difference between “paste in a paragraph” and “paste in a codebase, a season of legal discovery, or three novels, and ask questions.”

This chapter is a synthesis, not a new mechanism. You have already built every piece of machinery involved: attention and its quadratic cost, rotary position embeddings (RoPE) and the surgery that stretches it, the key–value (KV) cache and its brutal memory arithmetic, sequence parallelism and length curricula. What you have not yet seen is how those pieces assemble into the capability the market actually sells — and how honest that capability is. Because there is a real gap, in 2026, between the context length a model advertises and the context length at which it still thinks well, and knowing how to measure that gap is worth more than any single spec.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to read a claim like “10M-token context window” the way an engineer does: as the start of a conversation, not the end of one.

What a context window actually is

A model’s context window is the maximum number of tokens it can attend over in one forward pass: the prompt you send plus everything it has generated so far, all sitting in the KV cache, all visible to every attention head at every layer. Nothing outside the window exists for the model. There is no swap file, no “it sort of remembers” — tokens beyond the window are exactly as available as a book you never opened.

💡Intuition

The most useful frame: context is the model’s working memory. Its parameters are long-term memory — everything it absorbed during pretraining, compressed and fuzzy, frozen at the training cutoff. The context window is the desk it is working at right now: perfectly sharp, verbatim, but strictly bounded in size. Long-context engineering is the art of making the desk enormous without making every glance across it unaffordably slow. And like a human desk, bigger is not automatically better: a desk piled with 900 pages of irrelevant paper can make you worse at finding the one page that matters.

To make the sizes concrete, here is roughly what fits at various scales, using the usual English-text rule of thumb of about 1.3 tokens per word (code is denser in tokens — roughly 10–15 tokens per line; all figures approximate):

  • 8k tokens (GPT-4 at launch, 2023): about 6,000 words — a long magazine article, or one chapter of a book.
  • 100k–200k tokens: a typical novel (75,000–120,000 words ≈ 100k–160k tokens); a full-day deposition transcript (~300 pages ≈ 100k tokens); a quarter’s worth of a company’s board materials.
  • 1M tokens: roughly War and Peace plus change (~587,000 words ≈ 750k tokens); a mid-sized codebase of 60,000–100,000 lines; ten hours of transcribed meetings.
  • 10M tokens (advertised, not yet honest — more below): a small company’s entire document store, or the complete works of a prolific author.

The qualitative shift is real. At 8k you summarize documents; at 200k you read documents; at 1M you read collections — and questions like “which of these forty contracts has the non-standard indemnification clause, and does it conflict with the master agreement?” become a single prompt rather than a pipeline.

🕰History

The window grew in lurches: 512 tokens (BERT, 2018), 2,048 (GPT-3, 2020), 8k–32k (GPT-4 variants, March 2023), 100k (Claude, May 2023 — the first “paste in a whole book” moment), 200k (Claude 2.1, late 2023), then the big one: 1M–10M in the Gemini 1.5 report (February 2024). Since then: 2M (Gemini 1.5 Pro, mid-2024), 1M (GPT-4.1, 2025; Claude Sonnet 4, 2025), and a 10M-token advertised window (Llama 4 Scout, 2025) that became the field’s favorite cautionary tale about advertised versus effective context. Numbers are a snapshot; the trend line is the point.

Why long context is hard: three costs

If long context were free, every model would have shipped with a billion-token window years ago. It is not free. Three separate costs grow with length, and each one, unmanaged, becomes the binding constraint at a different scale.

Cost one: attention compute grows quadratically

Every token attends to every earlier token. Double the sequence and you quadruple the pairwise interactions. You saw this in Chapter 23; here is what it means at modern scales.

∑The math

For a model with model dimension ddd, LLL layers, and a causal sequence of nnn tokens, the attention interaction cost — computing QK⊤QK\TQK⊤ scores and the attention-weighted sum of values, across all layers — is approximately

FLOPsattn  ≈  2 d L n2,\text{FLOPs}_{\text{attn}} \;\approx\; 2\, d\, L\, n^2,FLOPsattn​≈2dLn2,

counting a multiply-add as two floating-point operations (FLOPs) and the causal mask’s factor of 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​ against the factor of 2 for doing both the score matrix and the value aggregation. Meanwhile the linear parts of the model — the projections and multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs), which touch each token independently — cost about 2Nn2 N n2Nn FLOPs for a model with NNN parameters. The linear cost grows like nnn; attention grows like n2n^2n2. At short lengths attention is a rounding error. At long lengths it is the entire bill.

✏️Worked example

Take an 8-billion-parameter model with d=4096d = 4096d=4096 and L=32L = 32L=32 (Llama-3.1-8B-shaped). At n=8,192n = 8{,}192n=8,192: linear cost ≈2×8×109×8192≈1.3×1014\approx 2 \times 8 \times 10^9 \times 8192 \approx 1.3 \times 10^{14}≈2×8×109×8192≈1.3×1014 FLOPs; attention cost ≈2×4096×32×(8192)2≈1.8×1013\approx 2 \times 4096 \times 32 \times (8192)^2 \approx 1.8 \times 10^{13}≈2×4096×32×(8192)2≈1.8×1013 FLOPs — about 13% of the linear cost. Ignorable. At n=1,048,576n = 1{,}048{,}576n=1,048,576 (1M): linear cost ≈1.7×1016\approx 1.7 \times 10^{16}≈1.7×1016 FLOPs; attention cost ≈2×4096×32×(1.05×106)2≈2.9×1017\approx 2 \times 4096 \times 32 \times (1.05 \times 10^6)^2 \approx 2.9 \times 10^{17}≈2×4096×32×(1.05×106)2≈2.9×1017 FLOPs — now seventeen times the linear cost. The sequence got 128× longer; the attention work got 1282=16,384128^2 = 16{,}3841282=16,384× larger. Total prefill: roughly 3×10173 \times 10^{17}3×1017 FLOPs. On a single H100 sustaining ∼5×1014\sim 5 \times 10^{14}∼5×1014 FLOP/s, that one prompt would take ~10 minutes of pure compute — which is why million-token prefill is spread across many graphics processing units (GPUs) working on the same sequence at once.

Cost two: KV memory is linear — but linear times huge

The KV cache grows only linearly with length, which sounds benign until you multiply it out. Chapter 28’s formula, reused verbatim:

KV bytes  =  2⏟K and V×L×nkv×dh×S×B×b,\text{KV bytes} \;=\; \underbrace{2}_{\text{K and V}} \times L \times n_{kv} \times d_h \times S \times B \times b,KV bytes=K and V2​​×L×nkv​×dh​×S×B×b,

where LLL is layers, nkvn_{kv}nkv​ the number of KV heads, dhd_hdh​ the head dimension, SSS the sequence length, BBB the batch size, and bbb bytes per number (2 for BF16). For the same 8B model with grouped-query attention (GQA: nkv=8n_{kv}=8nkv​=8, dh=128d_h=128dh​=128), one token costs 2×32×8×128×2=131,0722 \times 32 \times 8 \times 128 \times 2 = 131{,}0722×32×8×128×2=131,072 bytes — 128 KB. At S=1S = 1S=1M tokens, batch of one: ~131 GB of cache for a single user’s single conversation — more than an entire 80 GB H100, on top of the 16 GB of weights. Without GQA (32 KV heads instead of 8) it would be 524 GB. This is why every long-context architecture decision in Chapter 28 — GQA, DeepSeek’s multi-head latent attention (MLA), sliding windows, hybrid layers — exists: the cache, not the compute, is what caps how many long conversations a serving fleet can hold in memory at once.

Cost three: training data with genuine long-range structure is scarce

The sneakiest cost. You can pay for compute and memory; you cannot easily buy documents whose meaning actually spans a million tokens. Most web pages are under a thousand tokens. Concatenating unrelated short documents into a long training sequence teaches the model nothing about long-range dependency — the correct thing to learn from such data is that distant tokens are irrelevant, which is precisely the wrong lesson. Genuine long-range structure lives in a few places: books (a character introduced in chapter 2 matters in chapter 40), large code repositories (a function defined in one file is called from thirty others), long transcripts and legal records, and increasingly synthetic tasks manufactured to require long-range retrieval and reasoning. This scarcity is why long-context ability is trained in a short, targeted extension phase on a specially up-weighted mixture (Chapter 34) rather than throughout pretraining — and why retrieval-style skills (find the fact) have improved faster than synthesis skills (integrate the book), a gap we will return to.

The stack: how a million-token window is actually built

No single trick produces a working 1M-token model. Shipping one means solving four problems at four different layers of the system, each covered in an earlier chapter — this is where they assemble.

Layer 1 — positions. A model trained at 8k has never seen the rotation angles that RoPE assigns to position 400,000; feed it a long sequence naively and attention degrades into noise. The fix is the RoPE surgery of Chapter 26: raise the base bbb (Llama 3’s rope_theta: 500000), or interpolate positions (PI), or do per-frequency-band interpolation with an attention-temperature correction (YaRN). This makes long positions representable. It does not, by itself, make them usable — the model still has to learn what to do with them.

Layer 2 — attention and memory. The KV cache must shrink and the attention kernel must stop materializing an n×nn \times nn×n matrix. GQA or MLA cuts cache size 4–57× (Chapter 28); FlashAttention computes exact attention in tiles without ever writing the full score matrix; and many 2025–2026 architectures interleave local layers (sliding-window attention over a few thousand recent tokens) with occasional global layers that see everything — paying the quadratic price in only a fraction of the layers. State-space and hybrid models push this further by replacing most attention layers with fixed-size recurrent state.

Layer 3 — systems. Even with efficient kernels, a million-token activation set does not fit on one GPU. Ring Attention and its descendants — “context parallelism” in Chapter 33’s taxonomy — shard the sequence itself across devices: each GPU holds a block of tokens, and KV blocks circulate around the ring so every query block eventually attends over every key block, with communication hidden behind computation. This is what makes both training on and serving million-token sequences physically possible.

Layer 4 — training. Context extension is a phase, not a property of the whole run. The main pretraining run uses short sequences (4k–8k) because they are vastly cheaper per token; then a final stage — typically a fraction of a percent to a few percent of total tokens — trains at progressively longer lengths on a mixture up-weighted toward books, code repositories, and synthetic long-range tasks. Llama 3.1, for instance, extended from 8k to 128k in six staged increments using roughly 800B of its 15.6T total training tokens (approximate, from the Llama 3 report). The Gemini 1.5 report showed this whole stack scaling to 10M tokens in research settings — the existence proof that the recipe has no hard wall before that point.

⚙️In practice

What ships in 2026 (all numbers fast-moving snapshots): Gemini 2.5 Pro serves 1M tokens (2M announced for earlier versions); GPT-4.1-class OpenAI models and Claude Sonnet-class models serve 1M; most strong open-weight models (Llama, Qwen, DeepSeek) advertise 128k–256k, usually via YaRN-style extension noted right in their config files. Nearly every one of them uses GQA or MLA, mixes local and global attention layers, and was context-extended in a late training phase rather than trained long from scratch. The advertised ceiling is increasingly a pricing and serving decision as much as a capability one — serving 1M-token requests means reserving enormous KV memory per user, which is why long-context tiers often cost more per token beyond a threshold (e.g., higher input pricing above 200k tokens).

Evaluating long context honestly

Now the uncomfortable part. A model that accepts a million tokens has a million-token window. Whether it uses a million tokens is an empirical question, and the evaluation story is a ladder of increasingly hard tests — each introduced because models saturated the previous rung without actually being good at long context.

Needle in a haystack: necessary, nowhere near sufficient

The original test, from Greg Kamradt’s 2023 repo: hide one distinctive sentence (the “needle” — famously, the best thing to do in San Francisco involves a sandwich and Dolores Park) inside a long haystack of Paul Graham essays, and ask the model to retrieve it. Sweep needle depth × context length, plot a heatmap, hope for solid green.

By 2024–2025, frontier models made the heatmap solid green nearly everywhere — Gemini 1.5 reported ~99% single-needle recall out to 1M tokens and beyond. But saturation revealed the test’s weakness rather than the models’ strength. A single semantically out-of-place sentence is the easiest possible retrieval target: it is the one thing in the haystack that looks nothing like the haystack. Attention heads that do induction-style matching can find it without anything you would call reading comprehension. Passing needle-in-a-haystack (NIAH) is like proving you can find your name in a phone book: necessary, and almost meaningless alone.

⚠️Careful

A green NIAH heatmap in a model announcement tells you the positional machinery works — RoPE extension didn’t break, attention reaches all positions. It tells you nearly nothing about whether the model can reason over long inputs. Treat it as a smoke test the marketing department has learned to frame as a capability claim.

Harder rungs: multi-needle, multi-hop, RULER

The successors make retrieval compositional. Multi-needle tests hide several facts and require all of them. Multi-hop variants chain them: fact A points to fact B (“the code for the vault is written on the back of the painting”; 400k tokens later, “the painting hangs in the east corridor”), so the model must retrieve, then retrieve again conditioned on the first result. Aggregation tasks ask about the haystack itself — count occurrences, find the most frequent name — so no single lucky retrieval can succeed.

RULER (Hsieh et al., 2024) packaged thirteen such tasks into a length-configurable synthetic benchmark and introduced the concept this chapter keeps leaning on: effective context length — the longest length at which a model still clears a fixed quality bar. The headline finding: of the models then claiming 32k or more, roughly half fell below the bar before reaching their claimed length. Models advertising 128k commonly had effective lengths of 4k–64k on RULER’s harder tasks. Realistic-document suites (LongBench and its descendants, long-document question answering (QA) and summarization sets) paint the same picture with messier data: scores decline with length long before the advertised wall, and decline fastest on tasks that need more than lookup.

Lost in the middle: position matters, not only length

Liu et al. (2023) found something stranger than degradation-with-length: degradation-with-position. In multi-document question answering — twenty retrieved documents, one containing the answer — accuracy traced a U-curve over where the gold document sat. Models were sharp when the answer was at the very beginning (primacy) or the very end (recency) of the context, and dramatically worse in the middle. The most damning detail: with the answer sitting mid-context, GPT-3.5-Turbo scored below its own closed-book performance — having the answer in front of it, in the middle of the pile, was worth less than not having it at all.

Two honest caveats. First, the U-curve was measured on 2023 models; frontier models in 2026 have flattened it substantially on tasks of that difficulty — long-context training explicitly targets it, often with synthetic data that plants needles uniformly across positions. Second, it has not disappeared: positional bias re-emerges in weaker models, at longer lengths, and on harder tasks, and practitioners still order their prompts around it (instructions and the most important material at the beginning or end, never buried at token 400,000). A bias this consistent with human serial-position effects — and with the recency structure that both RoPE’s decay and training data impose — should be assumed present until measured absent.

Retrieval versus synthesis: the real frontier

Every task above is, at heart, find things. The capability that would justify the working-memory framing is use everything: summarize the book, trace the character’s arc, determine whether the codebase’s error handling is consistent, reconcile forty contracts. Synthesis requires representing and integrating the whole input, not locating a piece of it — and here the honest 2026 assessment is that models are far better than they were and still worse than the marketing implies. On benchmarks built from genuine book-length comprehension — claims about novels that require having followed the plot (e.g., the NoCha benchmark of claim verification over novels), or full-repository code tasks — frontier models score far above chance and far below humans who have actually read the text. Length degrades everything, but it degrades synthesis first.

✎Note

Effective versus advertised context — the 2026 scorecard. Advertised context is a hard number (the position limit the application programming interface (API) accepts). Effective context is task-dependent and softer, but the pattern across RULER-style and realistic evals is consistent enough to state: for frontier closed models, retrieval-flavored performance holds into the many-hundreds-of-thousands of tokens; synthesis-flavored performance typically degrades noticeably beyond roughly 100k–200k. For most open-weight models, effective context on hard tasks runs around a quarter to a half of the advertised number. The 10M-token advertisements of 2025 were, by any effective-context measure, aspirational. None of these figures is stable; all of them are improving; the gap itself is the durable fact to remember.

Prompt caching: the serving-side complement

Long context has a second cost problem that shows up not in the model but in the bill. A conversation is stateless at the API level: every turn resends the entire history, and the provider must prefill — run the full forward pass over — everything before generating the next token. From Chapter 39 you know prefill is the compute-hungry phase. Now imagine an agent holding a 500k-token codebase in context across a 50-turn session: without help, the serving fleet re-processes half a million tokens per turn, 25 million cumulative token-prefills, nearly all of it recomputing keys and values for text that has not changed since the last turn.

The fix is the KV cache wearing a new hat: prefix caching (prompt caching). The provider keeps the computed KV cache for a prompt prefix — keyed by an exact-match hash of the prefix tokens — and when the next request begins with the same prefix, prefill skips straight to the first new token. The physics you already know (KV cache = the state of a processed sequence) becomes an economic instrument: cached input is billed at a steep discount because the provider does almost no work for it.

✏️Worked example

Agent session: 150k-token fixed prefix (system prompt + codebase), 30 turns, each turn adding ~2k tokens of new conversation. Without caching: turn kkk prefills roughly 150,000+2,000k150{,}000 + 2{,}000k150,000+2,000k tokens; summed over 30 turns that is 30×150,000+2,000×(1+2+⋯+30)=4,500,000+930,000≈5.430 \times 150{,}000 + 2{,}000 \times (1 + 2 + \cdots + 30) = 4{,}500{,}000 + 930{,}000 \approx 5.430×150,000+2,000×(1+2+⋯+30)=4,500,000+930,000≈5.4M token-prefills. With prefix caching at a typical ~10× read discount: the 150k prefix is paid in full once, then read 29 times at one-tenth price — 150k+29×15k+930k≈1.5150\text{k} + 29 \times 15\text{k} + 930\text{k} \approx 1.5150k+29×15k+930k≈1.5M effective full-price tokens, about 3.6× cheaper, and the discount grows with more turns and longer prefixes. Latency improves even more than cost: a 150k-token prefill that took tens of seconds becomes a near-instant cache hit, which is the difference between an agent that plods and one that flows.

⚙️In practice

Pricing patterns as of 2026 (approximate and fast-moving): cache reads typically cost around 10% of normal input price (Anthropic, DeepSeek — which made caching automatic and default) to 25–50% (OpenAI’s automatic caching for prefixes over 1k tokens). Some providers charge a modest premium (~25%) to write a cache entry, and entries expire after minutes unless refreshed or paid for at longer durations. The design consequence flows backward into how you build prompts: stable content first. System prompt, documents, code — the unchanging bulk — must be a byte-identical prefix; anything that varies per turn (the user’s question, the timestamp you were tempted to put at the top) goes last, because the first changed byte invalidates everything after it. Every serious agent framework in 2026 is architected around this rule.

One more serving trick deserves a mention because it is often confused with long context: StreamingLLM-style attention sinks. Xiao et al. noticed that models dump surprisingly large attention mass on the first few tokens — a stable “sink” — and that keeping only those first ~4 tokens plus a sliding window of recent ones lets a model generate indefinitely without perplexity blowing up. That gives you infinite generation length, which is not long context: tokens that fall out of the window are gone, and the model remains fluent while forgetting them. Advertised “infinite streaming” and genuine million-token recall are different claims; only one of them involves remembering.

Long context versus RAG: complements, not rivals

When context windows hit 1M, a chorus announced the death of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) — why retrieve snippets when you can paste everything? The chorus was wrong for a boring reason: corpora are bigger than context windows and always will be. A million tokens is one shelf of books; an enterprise document store, a legal database, or the live web is millions of shelves. Retrieval is how you select which million tokens deserve the desk. What long context changed is the shape of RAG: retrieval can now be generous — pull whole documents, not paragraph shards — because the window can afford to read broadly and let the model, rather than the ranker, judge relevance.

SituationFavors
Corpus fits in the window and the task needs cross-document reasoningLong context — paste it all
Corpus is millions of documents / terabytesRAG — retrieval is the only option
Same large document interrogated across many turnsLong context + prompt caching (pay prefill once)
Data changes constantly; freshness per query mattersRAG — re-retrieve, don’t re-train your prompt
One deeply interdependent artifact (a codebase, a contract stack)Long context — chunking destroys the dependencies
Answers must cite auditable sources with provenanceRAG — retrieval hands you the citation for free
Cost-sensitive, high-volume, simple lookupsRAG — shipping 500k tokens per query is ruinous

The 2026 default for serious systems is both: retrieval selects generously, the long window reads generously, and caching makes the reading affordable. Chapter 45 builds the retrieval side properly.

Context engineering: the agent preview

There is one consumer of long context that deserves its own mention, because it is where all of this chapter’s threads knot together: agents. An agent running for hours accumulates context relentlessly — every tool call, every file read, every error message appends to the transcript — and it hits three walls in order: the quality wall (performance degrades as the window fills with stale tool output — the degradation patterns from the evaluation section, experienced live, which practitioners have taken to calling “context rot”), the cost wall (even cached, a bloated context taxes every turn), and eventually the hard wall (the window’s actual limit).

The emerging discipline of context engineering treats the window as the scarce resource it is: compaction (summarize old turns into a short digest and drop the raw transcript), memory files (persist durable facts to disk — notes the agent writes and re-reads — so knowledge survives outside the window), and sub-agents (delegate a subtask to a fresh context, keep only the result). If that sounds like an operating system managing random-access memory (RAM) with paging and swap, it should — the working-memory metaphor from the top of this chapter runs deep enough that the same design patterns reappear. Chapter 44 takes this up properly.

Where this leaves us

  • Context is the model’s working memory: verbatim and sharp, hard-bounded, and expensive in three distinct ways — quadratic attention compute (×16,384 from 8k to 1M), linear-but-huge KV memory (~131 GB for one million-token user on an 8B GQA model), and the scarcity of training data with genuine long-range structure.
  • A shipped 1M-token window is a four-layer stack: RoPE surgery makes positions representable, GQA/MLA + FlashAttention + local–global interleaving make attention affordable, Ring-style context parallelism makes sequences fit across GPUs, and a late length-curriculum stage teaches the model to actually use distance.
  • You can now read long-context evals critically: needle-in-a-haystack is a saturated smoke test; RULER-style suites measure effective context, which routinely runs a quarter to a half of advertised on hard tasks; the “lost in the middle” U-curve means position matters, not only length; and the retrieval–synthesis gap is the real frontier.
  • Prompt caching is the KV cache turned economic instrument: stable-prefix prompt design plus ~10× cache-read discounts are what make long documents and long agent sessions affordable at all.
  • Long context and RAG are complements: retrieval chooses which million tokens deserve the desk; the window and the cache make reading them practical.
