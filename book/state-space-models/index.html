State-Space Models and Hybrids

How linear recurrence came back — S4, Mamba, the duality with attention, why fixed-size states fail at recall, and the hybrid stacks shipping in 2026.

In this chapter
  1. The ledger: what attention pays and what recurrence promises
  2. The linear state-space recurrence
  3. Mamba: making the recurrence selective
  4. Mamba-2: the duality with attention
  5. The recall problem: where a fixed state fails
  6. Hybrids: a few attention layers go a long way
  7. Where this leaves us

In Chapter 17 we buried recurrent neural networks (RNNs). They processed sequences one token at a time, carrying a fixed-size hidden state — an idea killed by two diseases: gradients that vanished or exploded across time steps, and training that could not be parallelized because step ttt had to wait for step t−1t-1t−1. Attention won because it had neither disease. Every token looks directly at every other token; no information has to survive a thousand squashing nonlinearities, and the whole computation is one big matrix multiply that a graphics processing unit (GPU) can chew through in parallel.

But attention’s victory came with a bill, and Chapter 28 itemized it: training compute that grows with the square of sequence length, and a key-value (KV) cache that grows linearly with every token generated. At a 100K-token context, that cache is measured in gigabytes per request. The bill gets paid on every long document, every long conversation, every agent transcript.

This chapter is about the counterattack: a family of models that brought recurrence back — a fixed-size state, linear-time processing, constant-memory generation — while curing both of the old diseases. The cure turns out to hinge on one word: linear. Make the recurrence linear in the state, and it can be unrolled into a convolution (parallel training, disease two cured) and its memory behavior can be engineered rather than hoped for (disease one cured). Then a second idea, selectivity, makes the linear recurrence content-aware enough to compete with attention on language. The result — S4, then Mamba, then Mamba-2 — matches similar-sized transformers on many tasks. It also has one honest, well-understood weakness, which is why the systems actually shipping in 2026 are mostly hybrids: stacks that are seven-eighths state-space layers with a few attention layers standing guard.

By the end you will be able to derive the recurrence–convolution duality with a pencil, explain what “selective” means and why it forced a new parallel algorithm, state the Mamba-2 duality result in one sentence, and read a 2026 hybrid architecture diagram like a native.

The ledger: what attention pays and what recurrence promises

Put the two paradigms side by side, for a sequence of nnn tokens.

Attention, training. Every token attends to every earlier token: the score matrix QK⊤QK\TQK⊤ has roughly n2/2n^2/2n2/2 entries that must be computed and stored (or recomputed, with FlashAttention-style tricks — see Chapter 28). Double the context, quadruple the attention compute.

Attention, generation. Generating token n+1n+1n+1 requires the keys and values of all nnn previous tokens — that is the KV cache. Per-token generation cost grows linearly in context length (O(n)O(n)O(n) work per token), and the cache occupies memory proportional to nnn.

A recurrent state, training. A recurrence reads the sequence once: O(n)O(n)O(n) total work. But a naive RNN must do it serially — step 500 waits for step 499 — which wastes the massive parallelism of a GPU.

A recurrent state, generation. This is the seductive part. To generate the next token, the model needs only the current state — a fixed-size vector or matrix. O(1)O(1)O(1) work per token, O(1)O(1)O(1) memory, regardless of how long the context is. No cache to store, no cache to read.

✏️Worked example

Concrete numbers, approximate but representative. A transformer with 48 layers, 8 KV heads of dimension 128, storing keys and values in 16-bit floats, pays per token: 2×8×128×22 \times 8 \times 128 \times 22×8×128×2 bytes =4= 4=4 KB per layer, so 48×448 \times 448×4 KB =192= 192=192 KB per token of context. At a 100K-token context that is ≈19\approx 19≈19 GB of cache — for one request. A Mamba-2-style model of similar size carries instead a fixed state of roughly 1–2 MB per layer, ≈\approx≈ 50–100 MB total — and it stays that size whether the context is 1K tokens or 1M. That gap — a factor of roughly 200–400 — is the entire economic case.

So why did anyone ever tolerate attention’s bill? Because Chapter 17’s RNNs, despite their cost profile, could not use long context: the gates of a long short-term memory network (LSTM) leaked, gradients through hundreds of nonlinear steps died, and serial training made scaling them miserable. The question this chapter answers is: what changed?

The linear state-space recurrence

Here is the object at the center of the chapter. A state-space model (SSM) maintains a hidden state vector ht∈RNh_t \in \R^Nht​∈RN (think N=16N = 16N=16 to 256256256 per channel) and updates it once per token:

ht=Aˉ ht−1+Bˉ xt,yt=C ht.h_t = \bar{A}\, h_{t-1} + \bar{B}\, x_t, \qquad y_t = C\, h_t.ht​=Aˉht−1​+Bˉxt​,yt​=Cht​.

Every symbol earns its keep. xtx_txt​ is the input at step ttt (for now think of one scalar channel of the residual stream; real models run many channels in parallel). hth_tht​ is the state — the model’s entire memory of everything up to step ttt, compressed into NNN numbers. Aˉ∈RN×N\bar{A} \in \R^{N \times N}Aˉ∈RN×N is the transition matrix: it decides how the old state decays or rotates before the new input arrives. Bˉ∈RN×1\bar{B} \in \R^{N \times 1}Bˉ∈RN×1 decides how the new input is written into the state, and C∈R1×NC \in \R^{1 \times N}C∈R1×N decides how the state is read out into the output yty_tyt​.

Notice what is missing, compared to an LSTM: there is no nonlinearity inside the recurrence. No tanh⁡\tanhtanh, no sigmoid gates wrapped around ht−1h_{t-1}ht−1​. The update is a pure linear map. That looks like a downgrade — didn’t Chapter 8 teach us that stacking linear maps buys nothing? Within one layer’s recurrence, yes, it is deliberately less expressive. The expressive nonlinearity gets moved between layers (each SSM layer is wrapped in the usual pre-norm residual block with elementwise nonlinearities, exactly like the blocks of Chapter 25). What the linearity buys inside the recurrence is the whole ballgame, and we will cash it in twice below.

From continuous time to discrete steps

One oddity to acknowledge before it confuses you: why the bars on Aˉ\bar{A}Aˉ and Bˉ\bar{B}Bˉ? Because this machinery was born in control theory, where the state evolves in continuous time as a differential equation, h′(t)=A h(t)+B x(t)h'(t) = A\,h(t) + B\,x(t)h′(t)=Ah(t)+Bx(t) — the same kind of object you met in Chapter 4, a rule for how the state’s rate of change depends on its current value. Text arrives in discrete tokens, so the continuous system is discretized: chopped into steps of size Δ\DeltaΔ, producing the discrete matrices Aˉ\bar{A}Aˉ and Bˉ\bar{B}Bˉ from the continuous AAA, BBB, and Δ\DeltaΔ.

The exact formulas involve a matrix exponential (Aˉ=exp⁡(ΔA)\bar{A} = \exp(\Delta A)Aˉ=exp(ΔA), with a matching expression for Bˉ\bar{B}Bˉ), and we are going to wave at them rather than derive them — honestly, because the details matter less than the one intuition you should keep: Δ\DeltaΔ is a step size that controls how much each token “advances the clock.” A tiny Δ\DeltaΔ makes Aˉ≈I\bar{A} \approx IAˉ≈I (identity): the state barely changes, the token is nearly ignored. A large Δ\DeltaΔ pushes the system many time-constants forward: the old state decays hard and the new input dominates. File that away — Δ\DeltaΔ becomes the protagonist when we reach Mamba.

Magic property one: the recurrence unrolls into a convolution

Now the first payoff of linearity. Because the update has no nonlinearity, we can unroll it by pure substitution. Start from an empty state h−1=0h_{-1} = 0h−1​=0 and write out three steps:

∑The math

Step 0:   h0=Bˉx0\;h_0 = \bar{B}x_0h0​=Bˉx0​, so y0=CBˉ x0y_0 = C\bar{B}\,x_0y0​=CBˉx0​.

Step 1: substitute h0h_0h0​ into the update:

h1=Aˉh0+Bˉx1=AˉBˉ x0+Bˉ x1,y1=CAˉBˉ x0+CBˉ x1.h_1 = \bar{A}h_0 + \bar{B}x_1 = \bar{A}\bar{B}\,x_0 + \bar{B}\,x_1, \qquad y_1 = C\bar{A}\bar{B}\,x_0 + C\bar{B}\,x_1.h1​=Aˉh0​+Bˉx1​=AˉBˉx0​+Bˉx1​,y1​=CAˉBˉx0​+CBˉx1​.

Step 2: substitute again:

h2=Aˉh1+Bˉx2=Aˉ2Bˉ x0+AˉBˉ x1+Bˉ x2,y2=CAˉ2Bˉ x0+CAˉBˉ x1+CBˉ x2.h_2 = \bar{A}h_1 + \bar{B}x_2 = \bar{A}^2\bar{B}\,x_0 + \bar{A}\bar{B}\,x_1 + \bar{B}\,x_2, \qquad y_2 = C\bar{A}^2\bar{B}\,x_0 + C\bar{A}\bar{B}\,x_1 + C\bar{B}\,x_2.h2​=Aˉh1​+Bˉx2​=Aˉ2Bˉx0​+AˉBˉx1​+Bˉx2​,y2​=CAˉ2Bˉx0​+CAˉBˉx1​+CBˉx2​.

The pattern is unmistakable. Each output is a weighted sum of all past inputs, with weights that depend only on the gap kkk between input and output:

yt=∑k=0tCAˉkBˉ⏟a scalar, call it Kk  xt−k.y_t = \sum_{k=0}^{t} \underbrace{C\bar{A}^{k}\bar{B}}_{\text{a scalar, call it } K_k}\; x_{t-k}.yt​=k=0∑t​a scalar, call it Kk​CAˉkBˉ​​xt−k​.

That is precisely a convolution of the input sequence with the fixed kernel K=(CBˉ,  CAˉBˉ,  CAˉ2Bˉ,  … )K = (C\bar{B},\; C\bar{A}\bar{B},\; C\bar{A}^2\bar{B},\; \dots)K=(CBˉ,CAˉBˉ,CAˉ2Bˉ,…) — the same operation from Chapter 17’s convolutional neural networks (CNNs), just with a kernel as long as the sequence.

This is the trick that resurrects recurrence. At training time, when the whole sequence is sitting in memory at once, you don’t run the recurrence at all: you precompute the kernel KKK and convolve — and a long convolution can be computed with fast Fourier transforms in O(nlog⁡n)O(n \log n)O(nlogn) time, fully parallel across the sequence, no step waiting on the previous one. At generation time, you switch personalities and run the cheap recurrence, one O(1)O(1)O(1) state update per token. Same model, two execution modes. Structured State Spaces (S4) — Gu, Goel & Ré, 2021 — was the paper that made this dual-mode machinery numerically stable and fast enough to be practical.

✏️Worked example

A pencil-sized check that the two modes agree. Take a scalar state (N=1N=1N=1) with aˉ=0.5\bar{a} = 0.5aˉ=0.5, bˉ=1\bar{b} = 1bˉ=1, c=2c = 2c=2, and inputs x=(3,1,4)x = (3, 1, 4)x=(3,1,4).

Recurrent mode: h0=1⋅3=3h_0 = 1 \cdot 3 = 3h0​=1⋅3=3, so y0=2⋅3=6y_0 = 2 \cdot 3 = 6y0​=2⋅3=6. Then h1=0.5⋅3+1=2.5h_1 = 0.5 \cdot 3 + 1 = 2.5h1​=0.5⋅3+1=2.5, so y1=5y_1 = 5y1​=5. Then h2=0.5⋅2.5+4=5.25h_2 = 0.5 \cdot 2.5 + 4 = 5.25h2​=0.5⋅2.5+4=5.25, so y2=10.5y_2 = 10.5y2​=10.5.

Convolutional mode: the kernel is K=(cbˉ, caˉbˉ, caˉ2bˉ)=(2, 1, 0.5)K = (c\bar{b},\, c\bar{a}\bar{b},\, c\bar{a}^2\bar{b}) = (2,\, 1,\, 0.5)K=(cbˉ,caˉbˉ,caˉ2bˉ)=(2,1,0.5). Then y2=K0x2+K1x1+K2x0=2(4)+1(1)+0.5(3)=10.5y_2 = K_0 x_2 + K_1 x_1 + K_2 x_0 = 2(4) + 1(1) + 0.5(3) = 10.5y2​=K0​x2​+K1​x1​+K2​x0​=2(4)+1(1)+0.5(3)=10.5. ✓

Same answer, two algorithms — one serial and stateful, one parallel and stateless.

Magic property two: memory you can engineer

The second payoff attacks the vanishing-gradient disease directly. In the unrolled sum, the influence of an input from kkk steps ago is carried by Aˉk\bar{A}^kAˉk. In an old RNN the analogous quantity was tangled up with nonlinearities and learned weights, and Chapter 12 showed what repeated multiplication does to it: shrink to nothing or blow up. Here, the memory horizon is set by the eigenvalues of one matrix, in plain sight — and we can choose that matrix.

✏️Worked example

Stay with the scalar case, where Aˉk\bar{A}^kAˉk is aˉk\bar{a}^kaˉk. With aˉ=0.9\bar{a} = 0.9aˉ=0.9, an input’s influence after 50 steps is 0.950≈0.0050.9^{50} \approx 0.0050.950≈0.005 — half a percent — and after 200 steps it is ≈7×10−10\approx 7 \times 10^{-10}≈7×10−10: gone. With aˉ=0.999\bar{a} = 0.999aˉ=0.999, influence after 1{,}000 steps is 0.9991000≈0.370.999^{1000} \approx 0.370.9991000≈0.37 — still very much alive. And with aˉ=1.01\bar{a} = 1.01aˉ=1.01, after 1{,}000 steps the factor is 1.011000≈21,0001.01^{1000} \approx 21{,}0001.011000≈21,000: the state explodes. The recurrence remembers for exactly as long as aˉ\bar{a}aˉ sits close to — but below — 1.

An NNN-dimensional state gives you NNN of these knobs at once: a spectrum of decay rates, some fast (recent-context detail) and some slow (long-range gist). The deep question is which structured AAA makes an NNN-number state the best possible summary of an arbitrarily long history. That question has a principled answer — the HiPPO matrix (Gu et al., 2020), derived from online polynomial approximation of the input history — and initializing AAA with HiPPO structure is a large part of why S4 worked when a decade of earlier linear-RNN attempts had not. We name-check it and move on; the takeaway is that in a linear recurrence, long memory stops being an accident of training and becomes a design parameter.

⚠️Careful

A tempting misreading: “linear recurrence + engineered memory = strictly better RNN.” Not so. The price of the convolution trick is that Aˉ\bar{A}Aˉ, Bˉ\bar{B}Bˉ, CCC are the same at every time step — the system is time-invariant. It treats every token identically: the same write-strength, the same decay, whether the token is “the” or the one name that matters in the paragraph. An LSTM, for all its faults, could gate — decide per token what to keep and what to forget. S4 gave that up to get parallel training, and on language it showed: S4-family models lagged transformers at equal size. The missing ingredient is content-awareness, and restoring it is the next section.

Mamba: making the recurrence selective

Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2023) makes one change with cascading consequences: the parameters that were frozen across time become functions of the current input. At each step,

Δt=softplus(WΔxt),Bt=WBxt,Ct=WCxt,\Delta_t = \mathrm{softplus}(W_\Delta x_t), \qquad B_t = W_B x_t, \qquad C_t = W_C x_t,Δt​=softplus(WΔ​xt​),Bt​=WB​xt​,Ct​=WC​xt​,

where the WWW‘s are small learned projections and softplus (log⁡(1+ez)\log(1 + e^z)log(1+ez)) keeps the step size positive. The transition matrix follows along, since it is built from Δ\DeltaΔ: Aˉt=exp⁡(ΔtA)\bar{A}_t = \exp(\Delta_t A)Aˉt​=exp(Δt​A). The recurrence is still linear in the state —

ht=Aˉt ht−1+Bˉt xt,yt=Ct hth_t = \bar{A}_t\, h_{t-1} + \bar{B}_t\, x_t, \qquad y_t = C_t\, h_tht​=Aˉt​ht−1​+Bˉt​xt​,yt​=Ct​ht​

— but its coefficients now change every step, steered by content. Gu and Dao call this selectivity, and the name is apt: the model selects, token by token, what to remember.

💡Intuition

Recall what Δ\DeltaΔ does: it is the “how much does this token advance the clock” dial. Making it input-dependent turns it into a gate. When the model computes a small Δt\Delta_tΔt​ for a token, Aˉt≈I\bar{A}_t \approx IAˉt​≈I and Bˉt≈0\bar{B}_t \approx 0Bˉt​≈0: the state coasts through unchanged — this token is filler, ignore it. When it computes a large Δt\Delta_tΔt​, the old state is decayed hard and the input written in strongly: this matters, clear space and store it. Input-dependent BtB_tBt​ controls what gets written; input-dependent CtC_tCt​ controls what the current query reads out of the state. If you remember LSTMs from 2017: Δ\DeltaΔ plays the role of the forget and input gates coupled into one dial — but acting on a linear state with engineered decay, not a tanh⁡\tanhtanh-squashed one. The gates are back; the diseases are not.

Selectivity is what recovers attention-like abilities. The canonical demonstration is the induction head task from Chapter 52: having seen ”…A B … A”, predict “B”. A time-invariant SSM struggles — its fixed kernel weights inputs by position gap only, and cannot say “store the token after A, retrieve it when A recurs,” because that rule depends on content, not position. A selective SSM can learn exactly that policy: spike Δ\DeltaΔ on the trigger, write the payload, read it back when the trigger reappears. In the Mamba paper’s synthetic tests, this solves induction and extrapolates to sequences of a million tokens — far beyond anything seen in training.

Selection breaks the convolution — the parallel scan repairs it

There is no free lunch, and here is the check arriving: the convolution trick of magic property one required time-invariance. The kernel entry Kk=CAˉkBˉK_k = C\bar{A}^k\bar{B}Kk​=CAˉkBˉ only makes sense if Aˉ\bar{A}Aˉ, Bˉ\bar{B}Bˉ, CCC are the same at every step. With per-token parameters, every output is a product of a different chain of matrices — there is no shared kernel to convolve with. Have we just re-broken parallel training?

No — because linearity gives us a second, less famous parallelization: the parallel scan (also called a prefix scan). The civilian version: suppose you need all the running totals of a list of numbers. The serial way adds left to right, nnn steps, each waiting on the last. The parallel way combines pairs of adjacent entries, then pairs of pairs, building a tree: after log⁡2n\log_2 nlog2​n rounds of simultaneous combines (plus a cheap downward pass), every running total is known. A thousand additions collapse into ten rounds. The only property this needs is that the combining operation is associative — grouping doesn’t matter, (a+b)+c=a+(b+c)(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)(a+b)+c=a+(b+c) — because the tree regroups operations freely.

Linear state updates are associative in exactly the required sense. Each step is “multiply the state by aˉt\bar{a}_taˉt​, then add bˉtxt\bar{b}_t x_tbˉt​xt​,” and two such steps compose into one step of the same form — so steps can be merged pairwise, tree-style, like the running totals.

✏️Worked example

Scalar again, all aˉt=0.5\bar{a}_t = 0.5aˉt​=0.5, bˉt=1\bar{b}_t = 1bˉt​=1, inputs x=(3,1,4,2)x = (3, 1, 4, 2)x=(3,1,4,2). Represent each step as a pair (aˉt, bˉtxt)(\bar{a}_t,\ \bar{b}_t x_t)(aˉt​, bˉt​xt​): steps are (0.5,3)(0.5, 3)(0.5,3), (0.5,1)(0.5, 1)(0.5,1), (0.5,4)(0.5, 4)(0.5,4), (0.5,2)(0.5, 2)(0.5,2). The compose rule for “do step 1, then step 2” is (a1,b1)⊕(a2,b2)=(a2a1,  a2b1+b2)(a_1, b_1) \oplus (a_2, b_2) = (a_2 a_1,\; a_2 b_1 + b_2)(a1​,b1​)⊕(a2​,b2​)=(a2​a1​,a2​b1​+b2​) — the second step’s decay hits everything the first step produced.

Round 1 (two combines, in parallel): (0.5,3)⊕(0.5,1)=(0.25,  0.5⋅3+1)=(0.25, 2.5)(0.5,3)\oplus(0.5,1) = (0.25,\; 0.5\cdot 3 + 1) = (0.25,\, 2.5)(0.5,3)⊕(0.5,1)=(0.25,0.5⋅3+1)=(0.25,2.5) and (0.5,4)⊕(0.5,2)=(0.25,  0.5⋅4+2)=(0.25, 4)(0.5,4)\oplus(0.5,2) = (0.25,\; 0.5\cdot 4 + 2) = (0.25,\, 4)(0.5,4)⊕(0.5,2)=(0.25,0.5⋅4+2)=(0.25,4).

Round 2 (one combine): (0.25,2.5)⊕(0.25,4)=(0.0625,  0.25⋅2.5+4)=(0.0625, 4.625)(0.25, 2.5)\oplus(0.25, 4) = (0.0625,\; 0.25\cdot 2.5 + 4) = (0.0625,\, 4.625)(0.25,2.5)⊕(0.25,4)=(0.0625,0.25⋅2.5+4)=(0.0625,4.625).

Applied to the empty starting state, that final pair says h3=4.625h_3 = 4.625h3​=4.625 — and the serial recurrence agrees: h=3→2.5→5.25→4.625h = 3 \to 2.5 \to 5.25 \to 4.625h=3→2.5→5.25→4.625. Two rounds instead of four steps; at n=106n = 10^6n=106, twenty rounds instead of a million steps. The full scan recovers all intermediate states with the same tree, which is what training needs.

The second half of Mamba’s contribution is making this scan fast on real hardware — the same discipline you saw in FlashAttention in Chapter 28. The naive implementation would materialize the expanded states (sequence length × state size × channels) in the GPU’s main memory (high-bandwidth memory, HBM) and drown in memory traffic. Mamba’s fused kernel loads the parameters once, runs the entire discretize-scan-readout pipeline inside the GPU’s fast on-chip SRAM (static random-access memory), and writes only the outputs back, recomputing intermediate states during the backward pass instead of storing them. “Hardware-aware” is not marketing garnish here; without the fused scan, selective SSMs are slower than the attention they were meant to beat.

What Mamba delivered

The results, reported honestly. At sizes up to about 3B parameters trained on the Pile, Mamba matched or exceeded transformers of the same size on language modeling and downstream zero-shot tasks, and roughly matched transformers of twice its size — the first recurrence-based architecture to do so. Inference throughput ran up to ~5× a similar-size transformer’s, since generation is one small state update per token with no cache reads. On long-sequence domains where attention’s quadratic cost bites hardest — audio waveforms, DNA — it set new marks, and its compute scaled linearly all the way out. What the paper did not show was frontier scale: the largest Mambas were thousands of times smaller than the largest transformers of the day, and (as we will see) a weakness was hiding in the fixed-size state. But as a proof that recurrence was back in the game, it landed — and it is why late 2023 headlines briefly asked whether the transformer’s reign was ending.

Mamba-2: the duality with attention

Six months later, the same authors delivered a twist worthy of a good novel: the rival architectures are relatives. Dao & Gu, 2024 — the Mamba-2 paper — proves a result they call structured state-space duality (SSD): a selective SSM (with a modest restriction on AAA) computes exactly the same function as a form of causal linear attention.

The bridge is shorter than you might expect. Take the attention of Chapter 23 and delete the softmax. The output becomes yt=∑j≤t(qt⋅kj) vjy_t = \sum_{j \le t} (q_t \cdot k_j)\, v_jyt​=∑j≤t​(qt​⋅kj​)vj​, and because everything is now linear, the sum can be regrouped: maintain a running matrix St=St−1+ktvt⊤S_t = S_{t-1} + k_t v_t\TSt​=St−1​+kt​vt⊤​ — an outer-product memory accumulated over time — and read it with the query, yt=St⊤qty_t = S_t\T q_tyt​=St⊤​qt​. That is a linear recurrence with a matrix-valued state: softmax-free attention is an RNN (Katharopoulos et al., 2020). The SSD theorem runs the bridge in both directions and adds decay: a selective SSM is this same computation with a learned forget factor on StS_tSt​, and the dictionary between the two worlds reads

The SSM’s “write vector” was a key all along; its “read vector” was a query. What the softmax version has that the linear version lacks is the normalized, sharply peaked lookup — hold that thought for the next section.

Why does a theorem speed anything up? Because the two views come with different algorithms, and SSD lets you mix them. The attention view computes y=Mxy = M xy=Mx where MMM is a lower-triangular matrix of pairwise interactions — matmul-shaped work that GPU tensor cores devour. The recurrent view is scan-shaped: cheap in floating-point operations (FLOPs), awkward for tensor cores. Mamba-2’s algorithm chunks the sequence, uses the matmul form within each chunk and the recurrence between chunks, and inherits the best of both: roughly 2–8× faster than Mamba-1’s scan in practice. The speedup is not vanity — it gets spent directly on capability, funding state sizes ~8× larger (from N=16N = 16N=16 up to 128–256) at similar cost. A bigger state means a bigger memory, which softens (though does not cure) the weakness we turn to now.

The recall problem: where a fixed state fails

Time for the honest accounting. Everything an SSM knows about the past 100K tokens lives in a fixed-size state — those 50–100 MB from our earlier example, versus attention’s 19 GB cache. You cannot compress 19 GB into 100 MB without losing something, and what gets lost is precisely the thing attention is best at: exact, high-fidelity lookup of arbitrary details.

The clean version of the argument is a counting one, made rigorous in “Repeat After Me” (Jelassi et al., 2024). Ask a model to copy an input string verbatim. A transformer does this almost for free — an induction-head circuit (Chapter 52) looks back at the actual tokens and transcribes them, at any length within context. A fixed-state model must squeeze the entire string into its state before reproducing it; once the string’s information content exceeds the state’s capacity in bits, perfect copying becomes impossible — not hard, impossible — no matter how the model is trained. The experiments match the theory: transformers copy far beyond their training lengths; SSMs degrade once inputs outgrow the state.

The same failure has a field-test version you can run on any model — call it the phone-book test. Paste in a few hundred name–number pairs, then ask for one: “What is Dana Okafor’s number?” A transformer’s attention can jump directly back to the exact row — the query “Dana Okafor” matches the stored keys, softmax puts nearly all its mass on the right entry, and the digits come back exact. An SSM had to decide at write time what to keep, without knowing Dana would be the one asked about; a few hundred pairs into an NNN-dimensional memory, retrievals come back blurred — a number, plausibly formatted, subtly wrong. Benchmarks that formalize this pattern (associative recall and its harder multi-query variants) show the same gap, and it is the single most consistent finding in the SSM-versus-transformer literature: parity on broad language modeling, deficit on precise recall.

⚠️Careful

The duality section explains the mechanism. Linear attention — and its SSM relatives — accumulates all key–value pairs superimposed into one matrix, then reads with a dot product. Without softmax there is no winner-take-almost-all normalization; a read of S⊤qS\T qS⊤q returns the target value plus a weighted smear of every other value whose key overlaps the query at all. Softmax’s exponential sharpening is exactly what suppresses that interference — this is the superposition-and-interference story of Chapter 50 wearing a different hat. Bigger states (Mamba-2) and cleverer write rules reduce the smear; nothing eliminates it at fixed state size.

So each side holds a card the other lacks: the SSM has constant-cost generation and linear-cost training; attention has random access to raw context. Which raises a question with an obvious answer: why choose?

Hybrids: a few attention layers go a long way

Interleave them. Build a stack that is mostly SSM layers — collecting the linear-time, constant-memory economics — and insert a few attention layers to serve as the precision-recall organ. Those layers still keep a KV cache, but a cache for 4 layers instead of 32 is an ~8× smaller bill, and the SSM layers add none.

Jamba (AI21, 2024) was the first production-scale demonstration and set the template. Its repeating block is eight layers: one attention layer and seven Mamba layers — the ~1:7 ratio the field then converged toward — with mixture-of-experts (MoE, Chapter 29) multi-layer perceptron (MLP) blocks on alternate layers for cheap capacity: 52B total parameters, ~12B active per token, a 256K-token context window — of which up to ~140K fits on a single 80 GB GPU. The paper’s ablations are the lasting contribution: pure-Mamba stacks at this scale trailed on exactly the in-context tasks the recall analysis predicts, and a sprinkle of attention recovered transformer-level quality — more attention beyond the sprinkle bought little. Zamba (Zyphra, 2024) pushed the economics further with a single shared attention block that the backbone re-invokes at multiple depths — attention as a recurring subroutine rather than distinct layers.

⚙️In practice

Where this stands as of mid-2026 — with the caveat that this is the fastest-moving section of the chapter. Hybrid SSM-or-linear-attention stacks have moved from research curiosities to shipping open-weight model lines: NVIDIA’s Nemotron-H family and IBM’s Granite 4.0 are Mamba-2/transformer hybrids built explicitly for cheap long-context inference; TII’s Falcon-H1 line is in the same mold; AI21 has continued the Jamba line. Adjacent and converging: several major open models hybridize attention with linear attention variants rather than SSMs proper — MiniMax-01’s “lightning attention” (7:1 linear-to-full ratio) and Qwen’s 2025-era Gated-DeltaNet hybrids among them — which, by the SSD duality, is a distinction of parameterization more than of kind. Typical attention fractions cluster around one in six to one in eight. The honest status report at the top of the market: the publicly documented frontier — GPT-5-class, Claude-class, Gemini-class, DeepSeek-V3-descended MoEs — remains full-attention transformer-MoE, with efficiency sought through sparse/sliding attention and cache engineering (Chapter 28) rather than recurrence. SSM hybrids own a growing middle tier where tokens are cheap and contexts are long: edge and on-device models, high-throughput serving, and long-document/agent-transcript workloads (Chapter 43). Whether recurrence reaches the frontier or remains its efficiency wing is an open question; treat every name in this box as a 2026 snapshot.

✎Note

A broader family note: Mamba is the most famous member of a whole research lineage of “linear-time sequence mixers” — RWKV (an RNN reformulation with transformer-parallel training, developed as a community open-source project), RetNet, GLA, DeltaNet, and successors. They differ in how the state decays and how writes are structured, but all live inside the same design space the SSD paper maps: a fixed-size state, linear updates, some form of gating. When you meet an unfamiliar 2026 architecture name, asking “what is its state, and what gates its writes?” will usually orient you in one minute. The idea has also crossed modalities: Vision Mamba applies the same selective-scan machinery to image patches as a linear-cost alternative to the Vision Transformer.

Where this leaves us

  • The ledger: attention pays O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) training compute and an O(n)O(n)O(n) KV cache; a recurrent state pays O(n)O(n)O(n) training and O(1)O(1)O(1) generation — and the old RNN reasons not to take that deal (vanishing gradients, serial training) were cured, not endured.
  • Linearity is the cure. A linear recurrence ht=Aˉht−1+Bˉxth_t = \bar{A}h_{t-1} + \bar{B}x_tht​=Aˉht−1​+Bˉxt​ unrolls into a convolution with kernel Kk=CAˉkBˉK_k = C\bar{A}^k\bar{B}Kk​=CAˉkBˉ (you derived this by substitution), enabling parallel training; and its memory horizon is set by the eigenvalues of Aˉ\bar{A}Aˉ, which HiPPO-style structure sets deliberately (S4).
  • Selectivity is the capability. Mamba makes Δ\DeltaΔ, BBB, CCC functions of the input, turning the step size into a content-aware forget/write gate — recovering induction-style behavior. That breaks the convolution, so training runs as an associative parallel scan: log⁡2n\log_2 nlog2​n tree rounds, fused into on-chip GPU kernels.
  • The duality: Mamba-2’s SSD theorem shows selective SSMs and causal linear attention are one computation — BtB_tBt​ is a key, CtC_tCt​ is a query — which yields matmul-friendly chunked algorithms (2–8× faster) and ~8× larger states.
  • The honest weakness: a fixed-size state provably cannot copy or recall beyond its capacity; attention’s softmax-sharp lookup wins the phone-book test. Hence hybrids — roughly one attention layer per seven SSM layers (Jamba’s template) — keeping recall while shrinking the cache bill ~8×.
  • 2026 status: hybrids ship in the efficiency tier (Nemotron-H, Granite 4.0, Falcon-H1, and linear-attention cousins); the documented frontier is still attention-plus-MoE.
