Scaling Laws

Why loss falls in straight lines on log-log plots, how C ≈ 6ND turns a budget into a prediction, what Chinchilla actually showed, and how labs plan nine-figure training runs.

Suppose you are about to spend a nine-figure sum of money training a language model, and someone asks you the only question that matters: how good will it be? For most of the history of machine learning, the honest answer was “we’ll find out.” Results depended on architecture tricks, hyperparameter luck, and folklore. You could not tell your CFO what an extra ten million dollars of compute would buy, because nobody could.

Scaling laws changed that. Between 2017 and 2022, a series of empirical studies established something strange: the pretraining loss of a language model — the cross-entropy we met in Chapter 9 — is a smooth, boringly predictable function of three numbers. How many parameters the model has (NNN), how many tokens it trains on (DDD), and how much compute the run burns (CCC). Plot loss against any of them on log-log axes and you get a straight line, holding across seven orders of magnitude. No other property of these systems is remotely this well-behaved. Attention patterns are inscrutable, training curves are noisy, benchmark scores wobble — but the loss falls along a line you can draw with a ruler.

That one empirical fact is, more than any single architectural idea, why the last decade happened. It converted “train a bigger model” from a gamble into an investment with a forecastable return, and it is the reason labs were willing to scale from millions of dollars to billions. It also contained a subtle error that caused nearly every model of the GPT-3 era to be built the wrong shape — an error worth understanding in detail, because the correction (the “Chinchilla” result) and the correction to the correction (the overtraining era) are the story of how modern models get their sizes.

This chapter builds the whole apparatus honestly: what a power law is and why it is special, where the famous C≈6NDC \approx 6NDC≈6ND formula comes from (we will count the floating-point operations — FLOPs — ourselves), what Kaplan found and what Chinchilla fixed, why 2024–2026 models deliberately violate Chinchilla anyway, what scaling laws are used for day-to-day inside labs, and the contested question of whether capabilities “emerge” suddenly or only appear to.

Power laws for civilians

A power law is a relationship of the form

L(N)=k N−α,L(N) = k \, N^{-\alpha},L(N)=kN−α,

where LLL is the thing you care about (for us: loss), NNN is the thing you scale (parameters, tokens, compute), kkk is a constant, and α\alphaα (alpha) is the exponent — the single number that controls how fast returns arrive.

Two properties make power laws special, and both matter for everything that follows.

They are straight lines on log-log axes. Take the logarithm of both sides: log⁡L=log⁡k−αlog⁡N\log L = \log k - \alpha \log NlogL=logk−αlogN. That is the equation of a line with slope −α-\alpha−α. So if you plot loss against parameters with both axes logarithmic and the points fall on a line, you have found a power law, and the slope tells you α\alphaα. This is not a cosmetic trick — it is the reason scaling laws are useful. A straight line is the easiest thing in the world to extrapolate: fit it on cheap small models, extend it to the model you cannot afford to train twice.

They are scale-free. Multiply NNN by 10 and the loss gets multiplied by 10−α10^{-\alpha}10−α — no matter where you started. Going from 1 million to 10 million parameters buys the same fractional improvement as going from 1 billion to 10 billion. There is no special size where the law changes character, no knee in the curve, no point of diminishing returns beyond the diminishing that was already priced in. Contrast an exponential decay e−Ne^{-N}e−N, which has a characteristic scale and effectively finishes; a power law never finishes, it only gets expensive.

💡Intuition

A power law is the mathematical signature of “returns diminish, but never hit a wall, and diminish at the same rate forever.” Every doubling of scale buys the same percentage improvement as the last doubling — and costs twice as much in absolute terms. That is why scaling feels simultaneously miraculous (progress never stops) and brutal (each step of progress costs what all previous steps combined cost). The entire economics of frontier AI — the ever-larger clusters, the datacenter buildouts — is downstream of this one functional form.

✏️Worked example

Kaplan et al. measured α≈0.076\alpha \approx 0.076α≈0.076 for loss versus parameters. What does that buy concretely?

  • 10× the parameters: loss multiplies by 10−0.076≈0.83910^{-0.076} \approx 0.83910−0.076≈0.839 — a 16% reduction.
  • 2× the parameters: 2−0.076≈0.9492^{-0.076} \approx 0.9492−0.076≈0.949 — about 5% off, per doubling, every doubling.
  • Cut the loss in half? You need Nα=2N^{\alpha} = 2Nα=2, so N=21/0.076=213.2≈9,000×N = 2^{1/0.076} = 2^{13.2} \approx 9{,}000\timesN=21/0.076=213.2≈9,000× more parameters.

Small exponents mean scale is a slow lever: real progress, but you pay four orders of magnitude for a halving. (This toy calculation ignores the irreducible-loss term we will add in the Chinchilla section — with it, halving the loss may be impossible at any scale.)

🕰History

Power-law learning curves were documented before transformers were fashionable. Hestness et al. (2017) at Baidu measured them across translation, speech, and image models and argued that error was “predictable, empirically” — but the community mostly filed it as a curiosity. It took Kaplan et al. (2020), with the transformer and OpenAI’s compute behind it, to turn the observation into a planning tool.

The compute triangle: C ≈ 6ND

Scaling has three currencies — parameters NNN, training tokens DDD, and floating-point operations (FLOPs) CCC — and they are locked together by an identity you can derive by counting multiplications. It is worth doing this honestly once, because the formula C≈6NDC \approx 6NDC≈6ND is used everywhere: in papers, in budget spreadsheets, in export-control regulations.

∑The math

Forward pass: 2 FLOPs per parameter per token. Almost all the parameters in a transformer sit inside matrix multiplications (attention projections and the MLP blocks — see Chapter 25). When a token’s activation vector is multiplied by a weight matrix, every individual weight wijw_{ij}wij​ participates in exactly one multiply-accumulate: one multiplication (wij⋅xjw_{ij} \cdot x_jwij​⋅xj​) and one addition (into the running sum). That is 2 floating-point operations per weight per token. Summed over all weights: the forward pass on one token costs about 2N2N2N FLOPs.

Backward pass: twice the forward. Backpropagation (Chapter 10) does two matrix multiplications for every one the forward pass did: one to compute the gradient with respect to the layer’s input (so the gradient can keep flowing backward), and one to compute the gradient with respect to the layer’s weights (so we can update them). Each costs the same 2N2N2N as the forward. Backward total: 4N4N4N FLOPs per token.

Together: 2N+4N=6N2N + 4N = 6N2N+4N=6N FLOPs per token, and training on DDD tokens costs

C  ≈  6 N D.C \;\approx\; 6\,N\,D .C≈6ND.

The ”≈” hides real omissions: the attention score computation QK⊤QK\TQK⊤ scales with sequence length squared rather than with NNN (a few percent at typical context lengths, more at very long context), and embedding lookups are nearly free. For dense transformers at ordinary context lengths, the approximation is good to within roughly 10%.

✏️Worked example

A real FLOP budget. Train an 8-billion-parameter model on 15 trillion tokens — the recipe of Llama-3-8B:

C=6×(8×109)×(1.5×1013)=7.2×1023 FLOPs.C = 6 \times (8\times10^{9}) \times (1.5\times10^{13}) = 7.2\times10^{23} \text{ FLOPs}.C=6×(8×109)×(1.5×1013)=7.2×1023 FLOPs.

An NVIDIA H100 graphics processing unit (GPU) delivers roughly 101510^{15}1015 FLOP/s of BF16 (bfloat16) peak throughput. Real training runs sustain 35–45% of peak — a ratio called model FLOPs utilization (MFU) — so call it 4×10144\times10^{14}4×1014 FLOP/s per GPU. Then:

7.2×10234×1014=1.8×109 GPU-seconds≈500,000 GPU-hours≈57 GPU-years.\frac{7.2\times10^{23}}{4\times10^{14}} = 1.8\times10^{9} \text{ GPU-seconds} \approx 500{,}000 \text{ GPU-hours} \approx 57 \text{ GPU-years}.4×10147.2×1023​=1.8×109 GPU-seconds≈500,000 GPU-hours≈57 GPU-years.

On a 16,384-GPU cluster that is about 30 hours of wall-clock — and at a market-rate $2 per H100-hour, about $1 million of compute for this one run (all figures approximate; see Chapter 33 for where the utilization losses go). The same arithmetic scaled to a frontier run at 102610^{26}1026 FLOPs gives ~140× the cost — which is how “hundreds of millions of dollars per pretraining run” stops being a metaphor.

The identity C≈6NDC \approx 6NDC≈6ND turns scaling into a constrained allocation problem. A budget CCC is one number, but it can be spent in many shapes: a huge model glancing at little data (big NNN, small DDD), or a small model grinding through an enormous corpus (small NNN, big DDD). The central question of this chapter is: for a fixed CCC, what split of NNN and DDD gives the lowest loss? Kaplan and Chinchilla are two famous, incompatible answers.

Kaplan 2020: loss becomes predictable

Kaplan et al.’s “Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models” (OpenAI, January 2020) trained hundreds of transformer language models spanning sizes from under a million to over a billion parameters, and measured cross-entropy loss as each of NNN, DDD, and CCC varied. Three findings defined the next two years.

First: smooth power laws everywhere. Loss followed clean power laws in each variable — approximately L∝N−0.076L \propto N^{-0.076}L∝N−0.076 with data unconstrained, L∝D−0.095L \propto D^{-0.095}L∝D−0.095 with size unconstrained, and L∝C−0.050L \propto C^{-0.050}L∝C−0.050 along the efficient frontier — with the straight lines holding across seven orders of magnitude of compute. No plateaus, no phase transitions in the loss.

Second: shape barely matters, scale does. Within broad limits, depth versus width, number of attention heads, and other architectural choices moved the loss a few percent at most. Total parameter count dominated everything. This was a deflationary finding for architecture research: at fixed NNN, a mediocre transformer and a lovingly tuned one land close together; a 10× bigger mediocre one beats both.

Third — the influential mistake: big models are more sample-efficient, so grow NNN much faster than DDD. Kaplan’s fits implied that as compute grows, the optimal allocation is roughly N∝C0.73N \propto C^{0.73}N∝C0.73, D∝C0.27D \propto C^{0.27}D∝C0.27: pour almost all new compute into model size, and stop training well before convergence. Following this prescription, GPT-3 was built as 175B parameters trained on 300B tokens — about 1.7 tokens per parameter — and the era’s other giants (Gopher at 280B, MT-NLG at 530B, Jurassic-1, PanGu) all took similar shapes: enormous, and by later standards drastically undertrained.

⚠️Careful

The constants are not portable; the exponents barely are. The numbers kkk and α\alphaα in any fitted scaling law depend on the tokenizer, the data mixture, the context length, and the loss’s units (nats versus bits per token — see Chapter 7). A loss of 2.0 from one paper cannot be compared with a loss of 2.0 from another, and a law fitted on 2020 WebText will not accurately price a 2026 curated-and-synthetic mixture (Chapter 31). Labs refit their laws every time the data pipeline changes. What transfers is the functional form, not the fitted numbers.

Chinchilla: asking the question properly

In March 2022, Hoffmann et al. at DeepMind re-ran the allocation question with more care and got a dramatically different answer. The paper — universally known by the name of the model it trained, Chinchilla — used three independent estimation methods on over 400 training runs from 70M to 16B parameters.

  1. Fix models, vary training length. Train each model size on several data horizons; for each compute level, note which (model, tokens) pair achieved the lowest loss; fit how the winners move as compute grows.
  2. IsoFLOP profiles. Fix a total compute budget exactly, train a range of model sizes to spend precisely that budget (bigger model ⇒ fewer tokens, since D=C/6ND = C/6ND=C/6N), and plot loss against model size. Each budget traces a U-shaped valley — an “isoFLOP curve” — whose bottom is the optimal model size for that budget. Repeat across budgets and fit the drift of the minima.
  3. Fit a parametric loss surface to all runs at once (next box).

All three methods agreed, and disagreed with Kaplan: the optimal exponents are close to N∝C0.5N \propto C^{0.5}N∝C0.5 and D∝C0.5D \propto C^{0.5}D∝C0.5. Parameters and data should scale together, in equal proportion. Every doubling of budget should buy a 2×\sqrt{2}\times2​× bigger model and a 2×\sqrt{2}\times2​× longer training run.

∑The math

The Chinchilla loss surface. Approach 3 fits the functional form

  • EEE is the irreducible loss: the entropy of the text itself (Chapter 7). Language is inherently uncertain — no model, however large, predicts the next token of natural text perfectly. 1.691.691.69 nats ≈ 2.442.442.44 bits per token is the floor this data mixture leaves even for an infinite model trained on infinite data.
  • A/NαA/N^{\alpha}A/Nα is the finite-capacity penalty: the extra loss you pay because a model with only NNN parameters cannot represent the true distribution. It vanishes as N→∞N \to \inftyN→∞.

L(N)=E+AN−α+B(6NC)β.L(N) = E + A N^{-\alpha} + B\left(\frac{6N}{C}\right)^{\beta}.L(N)=E+AN−α+B(C6N​)β.

Nopt∝Cβα+β,Dopt∝Cαα+β.N_{\mathrm{opt}} \propto C^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}}, \qquad D_{\mathrm{opt}} \propto C^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}.Nopt​∝Cα+ββ​,Dopt​∝Cα+βα​.

Taking them as equal, the ratio D/ND/ND/N at the optimum is a constant — empirically about 20 tokens per parameter.

Chinchilla was the theory, deliberately instantiated.

The natural experiment landed decisively. Chinchilla (70B) beat Gopher (280B) nearly everywhere — on MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding, a broad exam-style benchmark) it scored about 67.5% to Gopher’s 60% — while being 4× cheaper to run at inference. The field’s reaction was immediate: “Chinchilla-optimal” entered the vocabulary, and the 100B+ giants of 2021 suddenly looked like monuments to a miscalibrated fit.

Why did Kaplan get it wrong? Two subtleties, both instructive. Kaplan’s analysis reused intermediate checkpoints of long runs with a fixed learning-rate schedule, but a cosine schedule (Chapter 34) only delivers a model’s best loss when its decay horizon matches the actual training length — so short-horizon points looked artificially bad, biasing the fit toward “training longer helps less than it does.” Kaplan also excluded embedding parameters from NNN where Hoffmann counted totals, which matters most at small scale, precisely where the fits are anchored. Later replications (notably Epoch AI’s “Chinchilla Scaling: A Replication Attempt”) found Hoffmann’s parametric fit slightly off in the constants but confirmed the headline allocation.

⚠️Careful

“20 tokens per parameter” is a fit, not a law of nature. The number came from one data distribution, one tokenizer, one architecture family, in 2022, and it answers one narrow question: how to minimize loss for a fixed training budget, ignoring everything that happens after training. Change the data quality, the architecture (mixture-of-experts especially), or — as the next section shows — the question itself, and the “optimal” ratio moves by orders of magnitude. Treat it as a landmark, not a commandment.

Beyond Chinchilla: when inference enters the ledger

Chinchilla optimizes the cost of making the model. But models are made in order to be used, and every token the model ever serves costs about 2N2N2N FLOPs of forward pass (Chapter 39). A model half the size is half price forever after. Once you expect serious usage, the question “cheapest way to reach a given loss during training” is the wrong question; the right one is “cheapest way to reach a given quality over the model’s whole life.”

Sardana et al. formalized this: minimize training FLOPs plus lifetime inference FLOPs,

Ctotal  =  6 N Dtrain⏟training  +  2 N Dinf⏟inference,C_{\mathrm{total}} \;=\; \underbrace{6\,N\,D_{\mathrm{train}}}_{\text{training}} \;+\; \underbrace{2\,N\,D_{\mathrm{inf}}}_{\text{inference}},Ctotal​=training6NDtrain​​​+inference2NDinf​​​,

subject to hitting a target loss, where DinfD_{\mathrm{inf}}Dinf​ is the number of tokens you expect to serve. Since both terms scale with NNN but only one scales with DtrainD_{\mathrm{train}}Dtrain​, high expected usage pushes the optimum toward smaller models trained far longer than Chinchilla prescribes — you deliberately overpay at training time to underpay at serving time. The loss surface’s flat valley makes the trade cheap: near the Chinchilla optimum, you can shrink NNN substantially and recover almost all the lost quality with extra tokens.

✏️Worked example

Inference cost equals training cost when 2NDinf=6NDtrain2 N D_{\mathrm{inf}} = 6 N D_{\mathrm{train}}2NDinf​=6NDtrain​, i.e. when the model has served Dinf=3DtrainD_{\mathrm{inf}} = 3 D_{\mathrm{train}}Dinf​=3Dtrain​ tokens. A widely deployed assistant generating (say) a few trillion tokens a month crosses that line within a year or two — after which every FLOP of the model’s life-to-date cost is mostly inference, and shaving parameters matters more than shaving training. (Usage figures are order-of-magnitude and fast-moving.)

The industry acted on this logic before the paper named it. Look at the trajectory of tokens-per-parameter:

Llama-2-7B (2023) trained on 2T tokens — roughly 290 per parameter, 14× “over-Chinchilla.” Llama-3-8B (2024) trained on 15T+, about 1,900 per parameter. By 2025, small open models like the Qwen3 family were reporting pretraining corpora around 36T tokens — thousands of tokens per parameter for the small variants — and the loss curves still had not flattened into uselessness, which is itself a quiet empirical finding: the returns to data at fixed model size, while power-law-diminishing, stay real much longer than the 2022 conversation assumed. This is the small-model renaissance: phone-sized models in 2026 outperform GPT-3.5 because they drank a hundred times the data Chinchilla would have prescribed.

Distillation amplifies the same economics. Several labs now train a large, Chinchilla-ish teacher for quality, then transfer its behavior into small serving models — training the student on the teacher’s outputs or soft token distributions rather than raw web text alone (Gemini’s Flash models and Llama-3.2’s 1B/3B are reported examples). Distillation effectively lets a small model buy loss with the teacher’s compute, bending its own scaling curve — one reason published “small model” quality since 2024 outruns naive extrapolation of small-model scaling laws.

⚙️In practice

A rule-of-thumb map for 2026 model shapes, all figures approximate: frontier teacher runs sit closest to compute-optimal reasoning (quality per training FLOP matters most, inference is amortized over distillation); mass-serving models are heavily overtrained and/or distilled (hundreds to thousands of tokens per parameter); mixture-of-experts (MoE) complicates the bookkeeping because NtotalN_{\text{total}}Ntotal​ and NactiveN_{\text{active}}Nactive​ diverge — DeepSeek-V3 has 671B total but 37B active parameters, and the 6ND6ND6ND formula prices its training near the active count (Chapter 29). Labs fit separate scaling laws for MoE configurations rather than reusing dense constants.

What scaling laws are actually for

It is tempting to file scaling laws as history — the argument that justified the money. Inside labs they are something more mundane and more important: the daily instrument panel of model development.

Forecasting the spend. Before a frontier run, labs fit their law on a ladder of small runs and extrapolate to the target. The GPT-4 technical report is the public landmark: OpenAI reports predicting GPT-4’s final loss accurately from models trained with 1,000–10,000× less compute — the prediction machinery working exactly as advertised, at a scale where a surprise would have cost nine figures.

Hyperparameter transfer. A scaling ladder only works if each rung is well-tuned, and tuning a big model directly is unaffordable. The maximal update parametrization (muP) of Yang et al. rescales initializations and per-layer learning rates with width so that the optimal learning rate stays fixed as the model grows — letting you tune hyperparameters on a 40M-parameter proxy and transfer them “zero-shot” to a model a hundred times larger (their demonstration outperformed the published GPT-3 6.7B using a tuning budget spent entirely at 40M). Open efforts including Cerebras-GPT and MiniCPM have used it end-to-end; several frontier labs are reported to use muP-like schemes. Without something in this family, every point on your scaling plot is contaminated by how well that particular size happened to be tuned.

Data-mixture and quality laws. The same machinery applies with the data recipe as the variable: fit loss (or downstream accuracy) as a function of the proportion of code, web, math, and synthetic data at small scale, then choose the mixture for the big run — a line of work running from DoReMi (scholar search) through the “data mixing laws” literature. Chapter 31 covers the recipes themselves.

Ablation-at-small-scale. Nearly every architecture and optimizer claim you read — RMSNorm placement, activation choice, a new attention variant — was validated by training small models at several sizes and checking whether the scaling trend favors the change, not whether one small model wins once. The method has a known failure mode: some interventions help at small scale and wash out (or invert) at large scale, so honest papers show trend lines, not points.

✎Note

There is a quiet epistemological caveat under all of this: scaling laws are empirical regularities, not derived theory. There are candidate explanations — notably that the exponents reflect the intrinsic dimensionality of the data manifold — but no consensus derivation predicts α≈0.34\alpha \approx 0.34α≈0.34 from first principles. We are engineers using a reliable pattern whose origin we cannot fully explain; the pattern has held over ~10 orders of magnitude, and each new frontier run is another data point rather than a guarantee.

The emergence debate

Everything so far concerned loss, which behaves predictably. The controversy begins when you ask about capabilities — the things users care about. Does a model gradually get better at three-digit arithmetic, or is there a scale below which it can’t and above which it can?

The case for emergence. Wei et al. (2022) catalogued dozens of tasks — modular arithmetic, multi-step reasoning, transliteration, MMLU among them — where performance sits at chance across several orders of magnitude of compute, then climbs steeply within roughly one order of magnitude. They defined an emergent ability as one “not present in smaller models but is present in larger models,” and noted the uncomfortable implication: if capabilities appear unpredictably, then forecasting loss does not mean forecasting what a model can do — a problem for both product planning and safety.

The case for the mirage. Schaeffer, Miranda & Koyejo (2023) replied that the sharpness lives in the metric, not the model. Most “emergent” results use discontinuous, all-or-nothing metrics — exact-match on a whole answer, multiple-choice accuracy — which mathematically manufacture cliffs out of smooth underlying progress. Score the same models with a smooth metric (per-token accuracy, edit distance, Brier score) and most of the cliffs dissolve into orderly curves. They showed the effect is so mechanical you can induce “emergence” in ordinary image classifiers by choosing a harsh enough metric, and noted that tiny evaluation sets add threshold noise on top.

✏️Worked example

How a smooth model produces a sharp metric. Suppose per-token accuracy on arithmetic rises smoothly with scale: p=0.60→0.80→0.95p = 0.60 \to 0.80 \to 0.95p=0.60→0.80→0.95 across three model sizes. A correct answer requires all 8 answer tokens right, so exact-match ≈ p8p^8p8 (treating tokens as independent):

0.608≈0.017,0.808≈0.168,0.958≈0.66.0.60^8 \approx 0.017, \qquad 0.80^8 \approx 0.168, \qquad 0.95^8 \approx 0.66.0.608≈0.017,0.808≈0.168,0.958≈0.66.

The underlying competence improved by modest, even increments; the benchmark went “broken, broken, works” — from 2% to 66%, with the last step of smooth progress delivering almost all the visible jump. Exponentiating a smooth curve through a conjunction of requirements is a cliff factory.

The honest synthesis. Both camps hold a genuine piece of the truth. Schaeffer is right that most published cliffs are metric artifacts, and that “the model suddenly learned X” claims deserve a smooth-metric audit before belief. But the mirage argument does not make the phenomenon unimportant: users, products, and risks live in exact-match world. A model that gets 95% of arithmetic tokens right is useless as a calculator; the capability, as a capability, really does arrive suddenly even if the competence underneath grew smoothly. And a few observed jumps are not obviously metric artifacts — training dynamics show real phase-change-like events, such as the abrupt formation of induction heads (Chapter 52). The working 2026 consensus: loss scales smoothly and is forecastable; downstream capability curves can be steep in ways that are metric-dependent; predicting which specific capability crosses usefulness at which scale remains an unsolved problem — GPT-4’s report could forecast its loss to high precision, and extrapolate some benchmark scores, but nobody’s spreadsheet predicted what GPT-4 would feel like to use.

Is scaling dead?

Late 2024 produced a wave of “scaling is over” discourse: reports of disappointing frontier training runs, the visible exhaustion of easily harvested web text (Chapter 31), and Ilya Sutskever’s NeurIPS 2024 line that “pretraining as we know it will end” — because compute keeps growing but “we have but one internet.” As of 2026, that narrative is best scored as one-third right and rhetorically overdrawn.

What happened is that scaling diversified. The single axis (“pretraining FLOPs in”) became several:

Test-time compute became a scaling law of its own. OpenAI’s o1 announcement (September 2024) showed two new straight lines on log axes: accuracy improving with reinforcement-learning (RL) training compute, and — the novel one — with thinking time at inference. DeepSeek-R1 reproduced the recipe openly months later. Spending 100× more FLOPs at inference on a hard problem turned out to buy capability that would have cost far more than 100× at pretraining time — for the subset of domains (math, code, verifiable reasoning) where an answer can be checked. This is a genuine third currency alongside NNN and DDD, and it further scrambles Chinchilla-style optimality: a smaller model that reasons longer can beat a bigger model that answers instantly, at equal total FLOPs. Chapter 37 is devoted to how these models are trained.

Pretraining scale quietly continued anyway. The clusters did not stop growing: xAI’s Colossus went from roughly 100,000 to 200,000 GPUs across 2024–2025 for the Grok series, Google’s Gemini runs on TPU (Tensor Processing Unit) pods of comparable ambition, and GPT-5-class and Claude-class training runs are credibly reported in the 102610^{26}1026-FLOP regime (all figures approximate — companies stopped publishing exact numbers around 2023). The returns per FLOP are what the power law always said they would be: positive, smooth, and increasingly expensive. What ended was not scaling but the era when pretraining scale alone was a strategy, since data quality, synthetic data, distillation, post-training, and test-time compute now compound with it.

The data wall is being negotiated, not hit. Estimates that high-quality public text runs out around the mid-2020s (Epoch AI’s “Will we run out of data?” analyses) motivated the pivot to synthetic and curated data — which changes the constants in the scaling law (better data lowers EEE‘s practical floor and the effective BBB) rather than repealing the law. Where the frontier stands on all of this is the subject of Chapter 40.

⚙️In practice

How this cashes out in 2026 model lines, approximately: frontier systems are trained as large (often MoE) base models near 102610^{26}1026 FLOPs, post-trained with reasoning RL, and served in “fast” and “thinking” modes that expose the test-time-compute dial to users; the mass-market tiers are overtrained or distilled small models; and every lab’s planning deck still contains the same artifact it contained in 2020 — a log-log plot with a straight line on it, now with more lines.

Where this leaves us

  • You can read any scaling plot: log-log axes, slope =−α= -\alpha=−α, scale-free returns — every ×10 of scale buys the same fraction off the loss.
  • You can derive C≈6NDC \approx 6NDC≈6ND by counting multiply-adds (2 forward, 4 backward per parameter per token) and turn any (model, tokens) pair into FLOPs, GPU-hours, and dollars.
  • You can state what Kaplan found (smooth power laws over seven orders; architecture second-order; grow NNN fast) and why its allocation was wrong (learning-rate-schedule mismatch, parameter accounting).
  • You can write the Chinchilla surface L=E+A/Nα+B/DβL = E + A/N^{\alpha} + B/D^{\beta}L=E+A/Nα+B/Dβ, interpret all three terms, derive N∝C0.5N \propto C^{0.5}N∝C0.5, D∝C0.5D \propto C^{0.5}D∝C0.5, and reproduce the ~20 tokens-per-parameter rule — and explain the Gopher-versus-Chinchilla experiment that confirmed it.
  • You can explain why 2024–2026 models are deliberately “over-trained” by 10–100× (inference cost, Sardana’s total-cost framing, distillation) and why that is rational, not sloppy.
  • You know what labs use scaling laws for daily: spend forecasting, muP hyperparameter transfer, data-mixture selection, and trend-based ablations.
  • You can argue both sides of the emergence debate — Wei’s discontinuities, Schaeffer’s metric critique — and state the synthesis: loss is forecastable, specific capabilities largely are not.
  • You know the 2024–2026 pivot: test-time compute as a new scaling axis, with pretraining scale continuing underneath it.

Scaling laws tell you what a budget buys; they say nothing about how to physically spend 102610^{26}1026 FLOPs without your cluster melting. Next: Compute — GPUs, Precision, and Parallelism.
