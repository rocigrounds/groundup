The Chain Rule, Jacobians, and Curvature

How derivatives compose: the chain rule as rates multiplying, Jacobians as the derivative of vector functions, why backprop uses vector-Jacobian products instead of full Jacobians, and how curvature shapes every optimizer.

In this chapter
  1. Rates multiply: the chain rule in one dimension
  2. Many paths: the multivariable chain rule
  3. The Jacobian: the derivative of a vector function
  4. Why backprop never builds the Jacobian
  5. Curvature: the second derivative and the Hessian
  6. Exponentials and logarithms: the survival kit
  7. Where this leaves us

In the previous chapter you learned what a derivative is: the best linear approximation to a function near a point, the answer to “if I nudge the input, how does the output move?” That chapter treated functions as single, self-contained objects. But a neural network is not a single function. It is a pipeline — dozens or hundreds of functions applied one after another, each feeding its output to the next. A 100-layer network is a composition of at least a hundred functions, and the loss at the very end depends on a weight buried at layer 3 only through everything stacked on top of it.

So the question that matters for training is: if I know how each stage responds to its own input, what can I say about how the whole pipeline responds? The answer is the chain rule, and it is not one tool among many — it is the tool. Backpropagation, the algorithm that trains every neural network on Earth, is nothing more than the chain rule organized so a computer can execute it without wasted work. When we reach Chapter 10, there will be no new math to learn; there will only be bookkeeping. Everything mathematical happens here.

This chapter builds the chain rule three times, each time more general: first for one number flowing into one number (rates multiply), then for values that flow along several paths at once (rates multiply along each path, and paths add), then for vectors flowing into vectors (Jacobian matrices multiply). Along the way we will see why practical systems never build those matrices — a memory argument with numbers that should permanently change how you picture backprop. Then we go one derivative deeper: the second derivative measures curvature, and curvature explains why plain gradient descent struggles, why optimizers like Adam exist, and even why a mysterious dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ appears in the attention formula you will meet in Chapter 23. We close with a short survival kit on exponentials and logarithms — the two functions the rest of the book leans on constantly.

Rates multiply: the chain rule in one dimension

Start with the smallest possible pipeline: a number xxx goes into a function that produces uuu, and uuu goes into a second function that produces zzz:

x  ⟶  u=g(x)  ⟶  z=f(u).x \;\longrightarrow\; u = g(x) \;\longrightarrow\; z = f(u).x⟶u=g(x)⟶z=f(u).

We know the local behavior of each stage: ggg has derivative g′(x)g'(x)g′(x) (how fast uuu moves when xxx moves) and fff has derivative f′(u)f'(u)f′(u) (how fast zzz moves when uuu moves). What is the derivative of the whole pipeline — how fast does zzz move when xxx moves?

💡Intuition

Think of currency conversion. Suppose one euro is worth 1.10 dollars, and one dollar is worth 150 yen. How many yen is one euro worth? You do not add the rates or average them — you multiply: 1.10×150=1651.10 \times 150 = 1651.10×150=165 yen per euro. Each conversion is a rate, and rates through a pipeline compound multiplicatively.

Or think of gears. If gear A turns gear B at a 3:1 ratio, and gear B turns gear C at a 2:1 ratio, then A drives C at 3×2=63 \times 2 = 63×2=6:1. The chain rule says derivatives behave exactly like gear ratios: the sensitivity of the end of the chain to the start of the chain is the product of the local sensitivities of every link. That is the entire idea. Everything else in this chapter is the same sentence with more indices.

∑The math

The chain rule (one dimension). If z=f(u)z = f(u)z=f(u) and u=g(x)u = g(x)u=g(x), and both functions are differentiable, then

dzdx  =  dzdu⋅dudx,\frac{dz}{dx} \;=\; \frac{dz}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx},dxdz​=dudz​⋅dxdu​,

where dzdu\frac{dz}{du}dudz​ means f′(u)f'(u)f′(u) evaluated at the current value of uuu, and dudx\frac{du}{dx}dxdu​ means g′(x)g'(x)g′(x) evaluated at the current xxx.

Why is this true? The derivative from Chapter 4 is a local linear approximation: a small nudge Δx\Delta xΔx produces

Δu  ≈  dudx Δx.\Delta u \;\approx\; \frac{du}{dx}\,\Delta x .Δu≈dxdu​Δx.

That nudge in uuu then propagates through the second stage:

Δz  ≈  dzdu Δu  ≈  dzdu⋅dudx Δx.\Delta z \;\approx\; \frac{dz}{du}\,\Delta u \;\approx\; \frac{dz}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}\,\Delta x .Δz≈dudz​Δu≈dudz​⋅dxdu​Δx.

Divide both sides by Δx\Delta xΔx and let the nudge shrink to zero, and the product of rates is the rate of the product pipeline. (A fully rigorous proof has to handle the case where Δu=0\Delta u = 0Δu=0, but the logic is exactly this.)

In Leibniz notation the rule looks like the dududu‘s cancel, as if derivatives were fractions. They are not literally fractions, but for single-variable chains this mnemonic never leads you astray — and we will see below exactly where it breaks (the moment more than one path exists).

Let us make this concrete with the example we will keep reusing.

✏️Worked example

Differentiate z=(3x+1)2z = (3x + 1)^2z=(3x+1)2 at x=2x = 2x=2, the long way.

Break the pipeline into named stages: the inner function u=3x+1u = 3x + 1u=3x+1 and the outer function z=u2z = u^2z=u2.

Step 1 — forward pass (compute the values). At x=2x = 2x=2: u=3⋅2+1=7u = 3\cdot 2 + 1 = 7u=3⋅2+1=7, and z=72=49z = 7^2 = 49z=72=49.

Step 2 — local rates. The inner stage has dudx=3\frac{du}{dx} = 3dxdu​=3 everywhere. The outer stage has dzdu=2u\frac{dz}{du} = 2ududz​=2u, which at the current value u=7u = 7u=7 is 2⋅7=142 \cdot 7 = 142⋅7=14.

Step 3 — multiply.

dzdx  =  dzdu⋅dudx  =  14×3  =  42.\frac{dz}{dx} \;=\; \frac{dz}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx} \;=\; 14 \times 3 \;=\; 42 .dxdz​=dudz​⋅dxdu​=14×3=42.

Step 4 — check it numerically, the way you always can. Nudge xxx from 222 to 2.012.012.01. Then u=7.03u = 7.03u=7.03 and z=7.032=49.4209z = 7.03^2 = 49.4209z=7.032=49.4209. The output moved by 0.42090.42090.4209 for an input move of 0.010.010.01, a ratio of 42.0942.0942.09 — matching our 424242 up to the size of the nudge.

Two things to notice. First, the local rate of the outer function had to be evaluated at the value flowing through the pipe (u=7u=7u=7), not at xxx. This is why every backprop implementation stores the intermediate activations from the forward pass: you cannot compute 2u2u2u without knowing uuu. Second, the general formula dzdx=2(3x+1)⋅3=6(3x+1)\frac{dz}{dx} = 2(3x+1)\cdot 3 = 6(3x+1)dxdz​=2(3x+1)⋅3=6(3x+1) agrees: at x=2x=2x=2, 6×7=426 \times 7 = 426×7=42.

The chain rule extends to any number of stages by applying it repeatedly: for x→u→v→zx \to u \to v \to zx→u→v→z, the rate is dzdx=dzdvdvdududx\frac{dz}{dx} = \frac{dz}{dv}\frac{dv}{du}\frac{du}{dx}dxdz​=dvdz​dudv​dxdu​ — one factor per link. A 100-layer network is a 100-factor product. Hold onto that image; it returns with a vengeance in Chapter 12, because a product of 100 numbers explodes if the factors average even slightly above 1 and vanishes if they average slightly below. Exploding and vanishing gradients are not exotic phenomena — they are the arithmetic of long products.

Many paths: the multivariable chain rule

The single chain covers a pipeline where each stage has exactly one input and one output. Real networks are not chains; they are graphs. A single activation feeds into many downstream neurons. A weight matrix touches every neuron in the next layer. In modern transformers the residual stream (see Chapter 16) lets every layer’s output reach the final loss along many routes simultaneously. So we need to know: what happens when xxx influences zzz along more than one path?

💡Intuition

Suppose your monthly spending depends on the price of oil in two ways: directly (you buy gasoline) and indirectly (shipping costs raise grocery prices). If oil rises $1, the total effect on your budget is the effect through gasoline plus the effect through groceries. Influences along independent paths do not interfere or multiply with each other — they simply add. That is the whole multivariable chain rule: multiply rates along each path, then add the paths.

Here is the smallest graph that is not a chain — a diamond. One input xxx fans out into two intermediates, which merge into one output:

∑The math

The multivariable chain rule. If z=f(u,v)z = f(u, v)z=f(u,v) where u=g(x)u = g(x)u=g(x) and v=h(x)v = h(x)v=h(x), then

dzdx  =  ∂z∂u dudx  +  ∂z∂v dvdx.\frac{dz}{dx} \;=\; \frac{\partial z}{\partial u}\,\frac{du}{dx} \;+\; \frac{\partial z}{\partial v}\,\frac{dv}{dx}.dxdz​=∂u∂z​dxdu​+∂v∂z​dxdv​.

Here ∂z∂u\frac{\partial z}{\partial u}∂u∂z​ (the partial derivative, from Chapter 4) means: the rate of change of zzz when uuu alone moves and vvv is held frozen. Each term is one path’s gear-ratio product; the sum ranges over all paths from xxx to zzz. With kkk intermediates the sum has kkk terms: dzdx=∑i=1k∂z∂uiduidx\frac{dz}{dx} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{\partial z}{\partial u_i}\frac{du_i}{dx}dxdz​=∑i=1k​∂ui​∂z​dxdui​​.

The justification is the same nudge argument as before. A nudge Δx\Delta xΔx moves both intermediates: Δu≈dudxΔx\Delta u \approx \frac{du}{dx}\Delta xΔu≈dxdu​Δx and Δv≈dvdxΔx\Delta v \approx \frac{dv}{dx}\Delta xΔv≈dxdv​Δx. Because the linear approximation of fff treats its inputs independently — that is what the plane-approximation of Chapter 4 means — the output moves by the sum Δz≈∂z∂uΔu+∂z∂vΔv\Delta z \approx \frac{\partial z}{\partial u}\Delta u + \frac{\partial z}{\partial v}\Delta vΔz≈∂u∂z​Δu+∂v∂z​Δv. Substitute and divide by Δx\Delta xΔx.

✏️Worked example

The diamond, with numbers. Take u=x2u = x^2u=x2, v=x+1v = x + 1v=x+1, z=u⋅vz = u \cdot vz=u⋅v, at x=2x = 2x=2.

Forward pass: u=4u = 4u=4, v=3v = 3v=3, z=12z = 12z=12.

Path through uuu (teal): ∂z∂u⋅dudx=v⋅2x=3×4=12\frac{\partial z}{\partial u} \cdot \frac{du}{dx} = v \cdot 2x = 3 \times 4 = 12∂u∂z​⋅dxdu​=v⋅2x=3×4=12.

Path through vvv (rose): ∂z∂v⋅dvdx=u⋅1=4\frac{\partial z}{\partial v} \cdot \frac{dv}{dx} = u \cdot 1 = 4∂v∂z​⋅dxdv​=u⋅1=4.

Total: dzdx=12+4=16\frac{dz}{dx} = 12 + 4 = 16dxdz​=12+4=16.

Check by collapsing the graph algebraically: z=x2(x+1)=x3+x2z = x^2(x+1) = x^3 + x^2z=x2(x+1)=x3+x2, so dzdx=3x2+2x=12+4=16\frac{dz}{dx} = 3x^2 + 2x = 12 + 4 = 16dxdz​=3x2+2x=12+4=16. Same answer — and notice the algebra itself split into the same two terms. The paths were always there.

This “sum over paths” view is worth internalizing now, because it is exactly what Chapter 10 will execute: backprop walks the computation graph from the loss backwards, and whenever a node’s output was used in several places, the gradient signals arriving from those places are added. It also shows up in a practical corner of language models: when the input embedding matrix and the output unembedding matrix are tied (the same parameter used in two places, common from GPT-2 through many modern models), that parameter’s gradient is the sum of the two paths’ contributions — no special machinery, just the multivariable chain rule.

Total versus partial: two questions that sound alike

There is a notational trap here that has confused generations of students, so let us name it explicitly.

⚠️Careful

∂f∂x\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}∂x∂f​ and dfdx\frac{df}{dx}dxdf​ answer different questions. The partial derivative ∂f∂x\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}∂x∂f​ asks: how does fff respond if I move xxx while holding every other input frozen — even inputs that, in reality, would move when xxx moves? The total derivative dfdx\frac{df}{dx}dxdf​ asks: how does fff respond when I move xxx and let all its downstream consequences play out?

Concretely, let f(x,y)=x2+xyf(x, y) = x^2 + xyf(x,y)=x2+xy, where yyy happens to depend on xxx as y=2xy = 2xy=2x. At x=1x = 1x=1 (so y=2y = 2y=2):

  • Partial: ∂f∂x=2x+y=4\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x + y = 4∂x∂f​=2x+y=4. (Freeze yyy; wiggle only the explicit xxx‘s.)
  • Total: dfdx=∂f∂x+∂f∂ydydx=(2x+y)+x⋅2=4+2=6\frac{df}{dx} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dx} = (2x + y) + x \cdot 2 = 4 + 2 = 6dxdf​=∂x∂f​+∂y∂f​dxdy​=(2x+y)+x⋅2=4+2=6.

Check: substituting y=2xy=2xy=2x gives f=3x2f = 3x^2f=3x2, whose derivative is 6x=66x = 66x=6 at x=1x=1x=1. The partial (444) is one path’s contribution; the total (666) sums all paths.

When a deep-learning paper writes ∂L∂W\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}∂W∂L​ for the gradient of the loss with respect to a weight matrix, it almost always means the total derivative through the whole network — the notation is loose, and everyone tolerates it. Keep the distinction in your head even when the symbols on the page blur it.

The Jacobian: the derivative of a vector function

So far the values flowing through our pipelines were single numbers. But a neural network layer takes in a vector of activations and emits a vector of activations — a function f:Rn→Rmf: \R^n \to \R^mf:Rn→Rm. What is “the derivative” of such a thing?

The answer follows from the same principle that defined the derivative in the first place: the derivative is the best linear approximation. In Chapter 2 you learned that a linear map from Rn\R^nRn to Rm\R^mRm is an m×nm \times nm×n matrix. So the derivative of a vector-to-vector function at a point must be a matrix: the matrix of the linear map that best mimics the function near that point. This matrix is called the Jacobian.

∑The math

Definition. For f:Rn→Rmf: \R^n \to \R^mf:Rn→Rm with components f1,…,fmf_1, \dots, f_mf1​,…,fm​ (each component a scalar function of all nnn inputs), the Jacobian at a point x\mathbf{x}x is the m×nm \times nm×n matrix

J  =  [∂f1∂x1⋯∂f1∂xn⋮⋱⋮∂fm∂x1⋯∂fm∂xn].J \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix}.J=​∂x1​∂f1​​⋮∂x1​∂fm​​​⋯⋱⋯​∂xn​∂f1​​⋮∂xn​∂fm​​​​.

Row iii is the gradient of output iii; column jjj is “how every output responds to input jjj.” The defining property is the linear approximation

f(x+δ)  ≈  f(x)+J δf(\mathbf{x} + \boldsymbol{\delta}) \;\approx\; f(\mathbf{x}) + J\,\boldsymbol{\delta}f(x+δ)≈f(x)+Jδ

for small displacement vectors δ∈Rn\boldsymbol{\delta} \in \R^nδ∈Rn — a matrix-vector product, exactly the machinery of Chapter 2, now serving as local physics for a curved map. Two familiar objects are special cases: when m=1m = 1m=1 the Jacobian is a single row, the transpose of the gradient vector from Chapter 4; when n=m=1n = m = 1n=m=1 it is a 1×11\times 11×1 matrix, an ordinary derivative.

✏️Worked example

A 2×2 Jacobian, computed and tested. Let f(x1,x2)=( x12+x2,    3x1x2 )f(x_1, x_2) = (\,x_1^2 + x_2,\;\; 3x_1x_2\,)f(x1​,x2​)=(x12​+x2​,3x1​x2​), a map from R2\R^2R2 to R2\R^2R2. The four partials:

At the point (2,1)(2, 1)(2,1): f(2,1)=(4+1,  6)=(5,6)f(2,1) = (4 + 1,\; 6) = (5, 6)f(2,1)=(4+1,6)=(5,6) and

J=[4136].J = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1\\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix}.J=[43​16​].

Now test the “best linear map” claim. Nudge the input by δ=(0.1,−0.1)\boldsymbol{\delta} = (0.1, -0.1)δ=(0.1,−0.1). The Jacobian predicts the output moves by

Jδ=[4136][0.1−0.1]=[0.4−0.10.3−0.6]=[0.30−0.30].J\boldsymbol{\delta} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1\\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0.1\\ -0.1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.4 - 0.1\\ 0.3 - 0.6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.30\\ -0.30 \end{bmatrix}.Jδ=[43​16​][0.1−0.1​]=[0.4−0.10.3−0.6​]=[0.30−0.30​].

The truth: f(2.1,0.9)=(4.41+0.9,  5.67)=(5.31,5.67)f(2.1, 0.9) = (4.41 + 0.9,\; 5.67) = (5.31, 5.67)f(2.1,0.9)=(4.41+0.9,5.67)=(5.31,5.67), an actual move of (0.31,−0.33)(0.31, -0.33)(0.31,−0.33). Prediction (0.30,−0.30)(0.30, -0.30)(0.30,−0.30) — off by a few hundredths, which is the quadratic error a linear approximation is allowed. Shrink the nudge tenfold and the error shrinks a hundredfold.

Now the payoff. What is the chain rule for vector functions? Compose f:Rn→Rmf: \R^n \to \R^mf:Rn→Rm with g:Rm→Rpg: \R^m \to \R^pg:Rm→Rp. Near a point, fff acts like its Jacobian JfJ_fJf​, and ggg acts like its Jacobian JgJ_gJg​. The composition of two linear maps is, by Chapter 2, the product of their matrices. So:

∑The math

The chain rule, fully general. If h(x)=g(f(x))\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x}) = g(f(\mathbf{x}))h(x)=g(f(x)), then

Jh  =  Jg Jf,J_h \;=\; J_g \, J_f,Jh​=Jg​Jf​,

where JgJ_gJg​ is evaluated at f(x)f(\mathbf{x})f(x) and JfJ_fJf​ at x\mathbf{x}x. The dimensions compose correctly: (p×m)(m×n)=p×n(p \times m)(m \times n) = p \times n(p×m)(m×n)=p×n. Every version of the chain rule in this chapter is this equation in costume. Scalar chain: 1×11\times 11×1 matrices multiplying — plain numbers. Sum-over-paths: write out one entry of the matrix product,

(JgJf)ij  =  ∑k=1m∂gi∂uk ∂fk∂xj,(J_g J_f)_{ij} \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{m} \frac{\partial g_i}{\partial u_k}\,\frac{\partial f_k}{\partial x_j},(Jg​Jf​)ij​=k=1∑m​∂uk​∂gi​​∂xj​∂fk​​,

and there it is — the sum over the mmm intermediate nodes uku_kuk​, each term a product of edge rates. Matrix multiplication is bulk bookkeeping for sum-over-paths. A deep network’s overall Jacobian is a product of one Jacobian per layer: J=JL JL−1⋯J1J = J_L \, J_{L-1} \cdots J_1J=JL​JL−1​⋯J1​.

✎Note

Because a Jacobian is a genuine linear map, all the geometric vocabulary of Chapter 2 applies locally to any smooth function: near a point it rotates, stretches, and shears space, and the absolute value of its determinant (for n=mn = mn=m) measures how it locally scales volume. That determinant has a starring role much later, in normalizing-flow-style arguments and diffusion models — here we only need the multiplication rule.

Why backprop never builds the Jacobian

At this point you might reasonably picture training as: compute each layer’s Jacobian matrix, multiply them all together, read off the gradients. That picture is conceptually right and computationally catastrophic, and the gap between the two is the single most important practical insight of this chapter.

Count the cost for a real model. A typical 7-billion-parameter transformer has hidden width d=4096d = 4096d=4096: each layer maps a 4096-dimensional activation vector to a 4096-dimensional one. Its Jacobian at one token position is a 4096×40964096 \times 40964096×4096 matrix — about 16.8 million numbers, roughly 67 MB in 32-bit floats. Per token. Per layer. Process a modest batch of 8 sequences of 2,048 tokens (16,384 positions) and the Jacobians of one layer occupy about 1.1 terabytes; across 32 layers, roughly 35 TB — hundreds of times the 80 GB memory of the H100 graphics processing units (GPUs) that trained this class of model. And multiplying two 4096×40964096\times40964096×4096 matrices costs about 1.4×10111.4 \times 10^{11}1.4×1011 arithmetic operations, again per token per layer. Materializing Jacobians is impossible at scale, not just wasteful.

The escape hatch is an observation about what we want. Training does not need the full Jacobian of the network — it needs the gradient of one scalar loss LLL with respect to the inputs and parameters. A scalar output means the final “Jacobian” in the chain, ∂L∂hL\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{h}_L}∂hL​∂L​, is a single row vector (1×d1 \times d1×d). And matrix multiplication is associative, so we get to choose the order:

∂L∂hL⏟1×d  JL JL−1⋯J1  =  ((∂L∂hL JL)JL−1)⋯J1.\underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{h}_L}}_{1 \times d}\; J_L\, J_{L-1} \cdots J_1 \;=\; \Big(\big(\tfrac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{h}_L}\, J_L\big) J_{L-1}\Big)\cdots J_1 .1×d∂hL​∂L​​​JL​JL−1​⋯J1​=((∂hL​∂L​JL​)JL−1​)⋯J1​.

Group from the left, and every intermediate result is a row vector times a matrix — output: another row vector. We never hold a d×dd \times dd×d product; we sweep one ddd-vector backwards through the network. This operation — row vector in, row vector out — is called a vector-Jacobian product (VJP), and it is the atomic operation of backpropagation.

Better still, each layer can compute its VJP without forming its Jacobian at all. For a linear layer h=Wx\mathbf{h} = W\mathbf{x}h=Wx, the Jacobian with respect to x\mathbf{x}x is exactly WWW, so the VJP is v⊤W\mathbf{v}\T Wv⊤W — a matrix-vector product with a matrix that already exists in memory. For an elementwise nonlinearity, the Jacobian is diagonal, and the VJP is an elementwise multiply. Every primitive operation in PyTorch or JAX ships with a hand-written VJP rule of this kind; “autograd” is the machinery that chains them in reverse order through the recorded computation graph.

✏️Worked example

A VJP with the numbers we already have. Notice what the multiplication did: each input’s gradient collected contributions from both outputs, weighted by how much that input influences each output. Sum-over-paths again — the matrix product does it wholesale.

✎Note

There is a mirror-image operation: the Jacobian-vector product (JVP), JuJ\mathbf{u}Ju for a column vector u\mathbf{u}u, which pushes an input perturbation forward through the network (“forward-mode” differentiation). Forward mode is efficient when a function has few inputs and many outputs; reverse mode (VJPs) when it has many inputs and few outputs. A neural network loss has billions of inputs (the parameters) and exactly one output — the most lopsided case imaginable — which is why deep learning is built on reverse mode. The price of reverse mode is memory: the backward sweep needs the forward activations (remember evaluating 2u2u2u at u=7u=7u=7), so they must be stored — or recomputed, a trade called activation checkpointing that returns in Chapter 33. The survey by Baydin et al. (2018) covers both modes properly.

🕰History

The chain rule itself is as old as calculus — Leibniz was using versions of it in the 1670s. Its reverse-mode reorganization is much younger: Seppo Linnainmaa described reverse-mode automatic differentiation in his 1970 master’s thesis, Paul Werbos proposed applying it to neural networks in his 1974 dissertation, and Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams (1986) made it famous by showing it lets multi-layer networks learn useful internal representations. The 1986 paper’s math fits on one page — the ideas you have just worked through are the whole of it.

⚙️In practice

In 2026 you rarely write a derivative by hand. In PyTorch, loss.backward() runs exactly the VJP sweep in the figure; torch.autograd.grad exposes it with a custom seed vector, and torch.func.vjp / torch.func.jvp (mirroring JAX’s jax.vjp / jax.jvp) give you the raw operations. What remains a human’s job is shaping computations so their VJPs are cheap and numerically stable — that is much of what architecture design (FlashAttention’s recomputation strategy, RMSNorm’s simple backward) is. The skill this chapter teaches is not computing gradients; it is predicting what autograd will do and why it might misbehave.

Curvature: the second derivative and the Hessian

The gradient answers “which way is downhill, right here?” It says nothing about the question an optimizer faces: how far can I trust that direction? Walk downhill on a gently curved valley floor and a big stride is safe. Walk downhill inside a steep, narrow ravine and the same stride overshoots the bottom and lands you on the opposite wall, higher than you started. The missing information — how quickly the slope itself changes — is the second derivative: curvature.

The precise statement is the second-order Taylor expansion, which upgrades our “best linear approximation” to a best quadratic one.

∑The math

Taylor expansion to second order. For a smooth scalar function of one variable,

f(x+δ)  ≈  f(x)  +  f′(x) δ  +  12f′′(x) δ2,f(x + \delta) \;\approx\; f(x) \;+\; f'(x)\,\delta \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}f''(x)\,\delta^2,f(x+δ)≈f(x)+f′(x)δ+21​f′′(x)δ2,

where f′′f''f′′ is the derivative of the derivative. For a scalar function of a vector, L:Rn→RL: \R^n \to \RL:Rn→R,

L(x+δ)  ≈  L(x)  +  ∇L(x)⊤δ  +  12 δ⊤H δ,L(\mathbf{x} + \boldsymbol{\delta}) \;\approx\; L(\mathbf{x}) \;+\; \nabla L(\mathbf{x})\T \boldsymbol{\delta} \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{\delta}\T H \,\boldsymbol{\delta},L(x+δ)≈L(x)+∇L(x)⊤δ+21​δ⊤Hδ,

where ∇L\nabla L∇L is the gradient and HHH is the Hessian, the n×nn \times nn×n matrix of second partials Hij=∂2L∂xi∂xjH_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}Hij​=∂xi​∂xj​∂2L​ (symmetric for smooth functions). The Hessian is to curvature what the Jacobian is to slope: it is a symmetric matrix, so — by the eigenvector story of Chapter 2 — it has orthogonal eigendirections, and its eigenvalues are the curvatures along them. A large eigenvalue means a sharply curved direction; a near-zero eigenvalue means a flat one; a negative one means the surface bends downward (a saddle direction).

Why does curvature matter so much for training? Because it sets the largest safe step size — and different directions can demand wildly different steps.

∑The math

The step-size limit, derived on a parabola. Take the simplest curved loss, f(x)=12ax2f(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}ax^2f(x)=21​ax2 with curvature f′′=a>0f'' = a > 0f′′=a>0, and run gradient descent with learning rate η\etaη (eta — the step-size knob):

xt+1  =  xt−ηf′(xt)  =  xt−η a xt  =  (1−ηa) xt.x_{t+1} \;=\; x_t - \eta f'(x_t) \;=\; x_t - \eta\, a\, x_t \;=\; (1 - \eta a)\,x_t .xt+1​=xt​−ηf′(xt​)=xt​−ηaxt​=(1−ηa)xt​.

Each step multiplies the distance from the minimum by the fixed factor (1−ηa)(1 - \eta a)(1−ηa). The process converges exactly when ∣1−ηa∣<1|1 - \eta a| < 1∣1−ηa∣<1, i.e. when η<2/a\eta < 2/aη<2/a: steeper curvature forces smaller steps. At η=1/a\eta = 1/aη=1/a you land on the minimum in one step; past 2/a2/a2/a every step makes things worse.

Now put two directions together: curvature amax⁡=10a_{\max} = 10amax​=10 in one direction and amin⁡=0.1a_{\min} = 0.1amin​=0.1 in another, one shared η\etaη. Safety in the sharp direction demands η<0.2\eta < 0.2η<0.2. Pick η=0.19\eta = 0.19η=0.19: the sharp direction multiplies by 1−1.9=−0.91 - 1.9 = -0.91−1.9=−0.9 per step — it overshoots and oscillates, flipping sign each time, shrinking only slowly — while the flat direction multiplies by 1−0.019=0.9811 - 0.019 = 0.9811−0.019=0.981, needing about 36 steps just to halve. The ratio κ=amax⁡/amin⁡=100\kappa = a_{\max}/a_{\min} = 100κ=amax​/amin​=100 is the condition number of the Hessian, and the number of gradient-descent steps needed scales with it. Ill-conditioned loss surfaces are slow to descend not because gradients are wrong but because one step size cannot fit all directions.

This picture is the origin story of most of Chapter 11. Momentum accumulates a running average of gradients, so the oscillating contributions from sharp directions cancel while the consistent contributions from flat directions build up speed — Goh’s interactive Distill article shows the condition-number dependence improving from κ\kappaκ to roughly κ\sqrt{\kappa}κ​. Adaptive optimizers like Adam keep a per-parameter estimate of gradient magnitude and divide by it, giving each coordinate its own effective step size — a cheap, diagonal stand-in for the Hessian information we cannot afford. Why can’t we afford it? A model with 7 billion parameters has a Hessian with (7×109)2≈5×1019(7 \times 10^9)^2 \approx 5 \times 10^{19}(7×109)2≈5×1019 entries — about 2×10202 \times 10^{20}2×1020 bytes in fp32 (32-bit floating point), roughly 200 exabytes, millions of large hard drives for one matrix. Second-order optimization in deep learning is a field of clever approximations (Hessian-vector products via double backprop, K-FAC, Shampoo — whose descendant Muon appears in Chapter 11) precisely because the exact object is unthinkably large.

✎Note

Curvature also underlies a live research debate: “flat minima generalize better than sharp minima” is folklore with real evidence (Li et al., 2018 is the classic visualization) but also real counterarguments — flatness is not invariant to reparameterizing the network, so a minimum can be made to look arbitrarily sharp without changing the function it computes. Chapter 13 on regularization and generalization takes up the dispute honestly.

Keeping compositions well-scaled: the dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ preview

Curvature explains one more thing, one you will otherwise trip over in Chapter 23: the mysterious division by dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ in the attention formula softmax⁡ ⁣(QK⊤/dk)V\softmax\!\big(QK\T/\sqrt{d_k}\big)Vsoftmax(QK⊤/dk​​)V.

The chain-of-rates picture says a deep composition behaves well only if each stage neither blows up nor flattens the signal. There is a statistical version of this discipline: keep the variance of each stage’s outputs around 1. Here is the failure it prevents. A dot product q⋅k\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{k}q⋅k between two dkd_kdk​-dimensional vectors is a sum of dkd_kdk​ terms; if the entries are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, the sum has variance dkd_kdk​ (variances of independent terms add — Chapter 6 proves this). At a typical head size dk=64d_k = 64dk​=64, the dot products have standard deviation 64=8\sqrt{64} = 864​=8: scores routinely land at ±16\pm 16±16 or more. Feed scores that large into a softmax and it saturates — the output is nearly a one-hot vector, sitting in a region where the function is nearly flat, so its derivatives are nearly zero, so (chain rule!) every gradient flowing through it is crushed toward zero and the attention layer stops learning. Dividing the scores by dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ restores variance 1 and keeps the softmax in its curved, trainable regime. That is the entire content of the famous footnote in Vaswani et al. (2017), and the same variance-bookkeeping logic drives weight initialization schemes in Chapter 12. One habit of mind — track how scale propagates through a composition — pays for itself across the whole book.

Exponentials and logarithms: the survival kit

Two scalar functions appear in almost every remaining chapter — in softmax, in log-likelihoods, in entropy, in learning-rate schedules — and both have derivative properties too convenient to be coincidence. This section is a compact refresher, with the one numerical trick you must know.

∑The math

The exponential exe^xex (with e≈2.71828e \approx 2.71828e≈2.71828) is the unique function with value 111 at x=0x = 0x=0 that is its own derivative: ddxex=ex\frac{d}{dx}e^x = e^xdxd​ex=ex — its growth rate at any point equals its current value. It converts sums into products, ea+b=eaebe^{a+b} = e^a e^bea+b=eaeb, and is always positive, which is why softmax uses it to manufacture legal probabilities from arbitrary scores.

The natural logarithm log⁡x\log xlogx (base eee throughout this book) is its inverse: log⁡ex=x\log e^x = xlogex=x. Its derivative is ddxlog⁡x=1x\frac{d}{dx}\log x = \frac{1}{x}dxd​logx=x1​, and it converts products into sums: log⁡(ab)=log⁡a+log⁡b\log(ab) = \log a + \log blog(ab)=loga+logb, hence log⁡(an)=nlog⁡a\log(a^n) = n \log alog(an)=nloga. Combined with the chain rule you get the workhorse identity of statistics:

ddxlog⁡f(x)  =  f′(x)f(x),\frac{d}{dx}\log f(x) \;=\; \frac{f'(x)}{f(x)},dxd​logf(x)=f(x)f′(x)​,

the relative rate of change of fff — which is why maximizing log⁡\loglog likelihood gives the same answer as maximizing likelihood (log is monotonic) with far friendlier derivatives.

Why does every loss in deep learning involve a log? Numerical survival. A language model assigns a probability to a document as a product of one probability per token (Chapter 18). Products of many numbers below 1 die fast:

✏️Worked example

Underflow, and the log-sum-exp rescue.

Products underflow. A 1,000-token document where the model gives each token probability around 0.050.050.05 has total probability about 0.051000=10−13010.05^{1000} = 10^{-1301}0.051000=10−1301. The smallest positive number a 64-bit float can normally represent is about 10−30810^{-308}10−308 — the true probability is over 990 orders of magnitude below representable, so the computed product is exactly 000, and every document looks equally, uselessly impossible. In log space there is no drama: 1000×log⁡(0.05)≈1000×(−2.996)=−29961000 \times \log(0.05) \approx 1000 \times (-2.996) = -29961000×log(0.05)≈1000×(−2.996)=−2996, a perfectly ordinary number. This is why models live entirely in log-probabilities — the “logprobs” exposed by every model provider’s application programming interface (API).

Exponentials overflow. Softmax has the dual problem. Suppose the logits are z=[1000,1001,1002]z = [1000, 1001, 1002]z=[1000,1001,1002] (large logits do occur). Computing e1002e^{1002}e1002 overflows — 64-bit floats give up near e710e^{710}e710 — and the naive softmax returns garbage (∞/∞\infty/\infty∞/∞). The fix exploits a symmetry: softmax only cares about differences between logits, because multiplying top and bottom by e−me^{-m}e−m cancels. So subtract the maximum m=1002m = 1002m=1002 first: shifted logits [−2,−1,0][-2, -1, 0][−2,−1,0], exponentials [0.1353,  0.3679,  1][0.1353,\; 0.3679,\; 1][0.1353,0.3679,1], sum 1.50321.50321.5032, softmax ≈[0.090,  0.245,  0.665]\approx [0.090,\; 0.245,\; 0.665]≈[0.090,0.245,0.665] — identical to the softmax of [0,1,2][0,1,2][0,1,2], computed without ever exceeding 111. And when you need the log of the softmax denominator, the same shift gives the log-sum-exp (LSE) trick:

log⁡∑iezi  =  m+log⁡∑iezi−m  =  1002+log⁡(1.5032)  =  1002.408.\log \sum_i e^{z_i} \;=\; m + \log \sum_i e^{z_i - m} \;=\; 1002 + \log(1.5032) \;=\; 1002.408 .logi∑​ezi​=m+logi∑​ezi​−m=1002+log(1.5032)=1002.408.

One more fact ties this section back to the rest of the chapter with a bow on top: differentiate the log-sum-exp function and, by the chain rule,

∂∂zjlog⁡∑iezi  =  ezj∑iezi  =  softmax⁡(z)j.\frac{\partial}{\partial z_j} \log \sum_i e^{z_i} \;=\; \frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_i e^{z_i}} \;=\; \softmax(z)_j .∂zj​∂​logi∑​ezi​=∑i​ezi​ezj​​=softmax(z)j​.

The gradient of log-sum-exp is the softmax. This innocent identity is why the gradient of cross-entropy loss through a softmax collapses to the simple “predicted probabilities minus true answer” — a derivation Chapter 9 walks through, and which makes the last VJP of every language-model training step nearly free.

⚙️In practice

You never implement these guards yourself: torch.logsumexp, torch.nn.functional.log_softmax, and every framework’s cross_entropy fuse the max-subtraction in. The practical rule in 2026 is stay in log space until the last possible moment — training losses, sampling filters, and evaluation perplexities all operate on logprobs, and the numerically naive path (log(softmax(z)) as two separate ops) is a classic source of silent NaNs (not-a-number results), especially in bf16/fp8 training — 16- and 8-bit floating-point formats — where the representable range is narrower still (Chapter 33 covers those formats).

Where this leaves us

You now hold the mathematical core of deep learning — everything after this is assembly. Concretely, you can:

  • Differentiate any composition by multiplying local rates along the chain, and check yourself numerically with a small nudge (z=(3x+1)2z = (3x+1)^2z=(3x+1)2: rates 333 and 141414, product 424242).
  • Handle branching influence with sum-over-paths on a computation graph, and keep total derivatives (dfdx\frac{df}{dx}dxdf​, all paths) distinct from partials (∂f∂x\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}∂x∂f​, one path, everything else frozen).
  • Read a Jacobian as the local linear map of a vector function, verify its prediction against a real nudge, and state the general chain rule as Jacobian multiplication Jh=JgJfJ_h = J_g J_fJh​=Jg​Jf​.
  • Explain, with a memory calculation (d=4096⇒d = 4096 \Rightarrowd=4096⇒ 67 MB per token per layer), why autograd sweeps a row vector backwards through vector-Jacobian products instead of ever materializing a Jacobian.
  • Use second-order Taylor expansion to see the Hessian as curvature, derive the step-size ceiling η<2/a\eta < 2/aη<2/a, and explain how condition number motivates momentum and Adam — and why the full Hessian (102010^{20}1020 bytes for a 7B model) is forever out of reach.
  • Track variance through a composition well enough to predict the dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ scaling in attention before you ever see the formula.
  • Compute safely in log space: logprobs against underflow, max-subtraction and log-sum-exp against overflow, and the identity ∇LSE=softmax⁡\nabla \mathrm{LSE} = \softmax∇LSE=softmax.

Backpropagation, when you meet it properly in Chapter 10, will be a rerun: the diamond-graph picture, executed backwards at scale. But first the book turns from geometry and rates to uncertainty — the language a model uses to say what it believes. Next: Probability for Deep Learning.
