CNNs and RNNs: The World Before Transformers

Convolutions and recurrence rebuilt honestly — a worked 3×3 filter, vanishing gradients over time, LSTM gates on a memory highway, the seq2seq bottleneck, and the concrete three-part case for why transformers took over.

If you learned deep learning around 2017, this chapter is the world you learned. Convolutional networks had owned computer vision for five years; recurrent networks — usually long short-term memory networks (LSTMs) — were how anyone serious did language, speech, and translation. Then, within about three years, nearly every frontier system dropped both in favor of a single architecture. This book spends its next three parts on that architecture. But skipping the world it replaced would be a mistake, for three reasons.

First, the ideas did not die. Convolutions are alive inside 2026 systems in places you might not expect — the first layer of most vision transformers is a convolution, and diffusion models leaned on convolutional U-Nets for years. The LSTM’s central trick — an additive path that lets gradients flow untouched — is the same trick as the residual connections in every transformer, discovered independently. And recurrence itself is currently staging a comeback in state-space models.

Second, you cannot really understand why attention won without understanding precisely what it beat. “Transformers are better” is a slogan. The real argument has three concrete parts — a hardware part, a gradient part, and an information part — and each one is a measurable property of the recurrent architectures in this chapter. By the end you will be able to make that argument with numbers.

Third, this material is where two of this book’s recurring themes — weight sharing as a prior, and the product-of-Jacobians view of gradient flow from the initialization chapter — get their most vivid workout. Same math, new axis: instead of gradients dying across depth, we will watch them die across time.

Convolution: a filter that slides

Start with images, because that is where the story starts. An image is a grid of numbers — say a 5×5 grayscale patch, one brightness value per pixel. The multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) you already know would flatten those 25 numbers into a vector and hit them with a dense weight matrix, treating pixel (1,1) and pixel (5,5) as unrelated inputs that merely happen to arrive on adjacent wires.

A convolutional layer refuses to flatten. It keeps the grid a grid and learns a small filter — a kernel — that slides across it.

💡Intuition

A kernel is a tiny template, and convolution is the act of asking “how much does this patch of the image look like my template?” at every location. A 3×3 kernel holds 9 numbers. You lay it over the top-left 3×3 window of the image, multiply each kernel number by the pixel underneath it, sum the 9 products into a single output number, then slide the window one pixel over and do it again. The grid of answers — one per window position — is called a feature map: a map of where in the image the template matched.

∑The math

Let XXX be an input grid and KKK a 3×33\times 33×3 kernel. The feature map YYY has entries

Yi,j  =  ∑a=02∑b=02Ka,b Xi+a,  j+b  +  c,Y_{i,j} \;=\; \sum_{a=0}^{2} \sum_{b=0}^{2} K_{a,b}\, X_{i+a,\; j+b} \;+\; c,Yi,j​=a=0∑2​b=0∑2​Ka,b​Xi+a,j+b​+c,

where ccc is a single learned bias shared across all positions. Each output is a dot product between the kernel (viewed as a 9-vector) and one 3×3 window of the input (viewed the same way) — so a convolution is a whole family of identical dot products, one per window position. Sliding a 3×3 window across a 5×5 input yields 3×3=93 \times 3 = 93×3=9 valid positions, so YYY is 3×3: convolution without padding shrinks the grid by (kernel size − 1) in each direction.

Two knobs you will see everywhere: padding (add a border of zeros so the output stays the same size — “same” padding), and stride (move the window sss pixels at a time instead of 1, shrinking the output by a factor of sss; keep stride in mind, because it returns at the end of this chapter as the way vision transformers cut images into patches).

Here is the whole operation with real numbers — a vertical-edge detector applied to an image containing one bright vertical stripe.

✏️Worked example

Work the top-left output by hand. The highlighted window is the input’s first three columns of its first three rows: [001001001]\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}​000​000​111​​, and the kernel is [10−110−110−1]\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}​111​000​−1−1−1​​. Multiply elementwise and sum: each row contributes 0⋅1+0⋅0+1⋅(−1)=−10\cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 + 1\cdot(-1) = -10⋅1+0⋅0+1⋅(−1)=−1, and three rows give −3-3−3. Slide one column right: now the stripe lands under the kernel’s zero column, so every product is 0 and the output is 0. Slide once more: the stripe lands under the +1+1+1 column, giving +3+3+3. The full feature map has −3-3−3 down its left column, 000 down its middle, +3+3+3 down its right — the filter has found both edges of the stripe and reported which side is which by sign. Nobody designed this particular kernel by hand in a real network; gradient descent learns kernels, and edge detectors like this one are reliably what the first layer learns.

One kernel produces one feature map, and one feature is not much of a description. Real convolutional layers learn many kernels in parallel — 64 or 256 of them — each producing its own map, stacked into a 3-dimensional block of activations (height × width × channels). The next layer’s kernels then extend through all input channels: a “3×3” kernel on a 64-channel input is really a 3×3×64 block of weights, mixing information across channels while staying local in space. Color images start this off naturally: a red-green-blue (RGB) input has 3 channels, so first-layer kernels are 3×3×3.

Two priors and a parameter miracle

Why does this beat a dense layer? Because convolution hard-codes two facts about images that a dense layer would otherwise have to burn data learning.

Locality. A pixel’s meaning depends most on its neighbors. Edges, corners, textures — the primitives of vision — are local patterns. A 3×3 kernel can only ever look at a 3×3 neighborhood, so the architecture cannot waste capacity on spurious long-range pixel correlations. Depth restores the long range: stack two 3×3 layers and the second layer’s outputs each depend on a 5×5 window of the original image; stack LLL layers and the receptive field grows to (2L+1)(2L+1)(2L+1) pixels wide. Global structure gets assembled gradually, local piece by local piece.

Translation equivariance, via weight sharing. The same 9 kernel weights are used at every window position. An edge is an edge whether it appears top-left or bottom-right, so the detector for it should be the same everywhere — and with shared weights, it is. Formally: shift the input two pixels right, and the feature map shifts two pixels right, unchanged otherwise. That property is called equivariance (the output moves with the input; “invariance” — output unchanged — comes later, from pooling). One learned example of a cat’s ear teaches the ear detector for every position at once.

∑The math

The parameter arithmetic is the argument in its sharpest form. Take a standard 224×224 RGB image: 224×224×3=150,528224 \times 224 \times 3 = 150{,}528224×224×3=150,528 input numbers.

A dense layer mapping this to 4,096 hidden units — a modest MLP by Chapter 8’s standards — needs 150,528×4,096≈6.17×108150{,}528 \times 4{,}096 \approx 6.17 \times 10^{8}150,528×4,096≈6.17×108 weights: 617 million parameters for one layer, before the network has done anything.

A convolutional layer with 64 kernels of size 3×3×3 needs 64×(3⋅3⋅3)=1,72864 \times (3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3) = 1{,}72864×(3⋅3⋅3)=1,728 weights plus 64 biases: 1,792 parameters — about 340,000× fewer — while producing an entire spatial map of 64 features at every one of ~50,000 positions. The output is bigger than the dense layer’s, and the parameter count is five orders of magnitude smaller. The catch, and it is the theme of this chapter: this economy is only a win when the assumptions baked into it (locality, translation equivariance) are true of your data. For images they are. Hold that thought for language.

Pooling finishes the classic recipe. A 2×2 max-pool slides a window like a convolution but computes max⁡\maxmax of each window instead of a dot product, halving the height and width. It has no parameters. Its job is to buy a little translation invariance — if the strongest edge response moves one pixel, the max over the 2×2 window often does not change at all — and to shrink the grid so deeper layers see a wider effective context at lower cost. The classic convolutional neural network (CNN) is exactly this loop: convolve, apply a nonlinearity like the rectified linear unit (ReLU), pool; repeat while channels grow and the grid shrinks; finish with a small dense head that reads the final features into class scores.

⚠️Careful

A common misreading: “CNNs are good because they have few parameters.” Fewer parameters is not the point in itself — modern networks are gigantic. The point is the prior. Weight sharing constrains the hypothesis space to functions that treat all image locations alike, which is a correct assumption for images, so every gradient update generalizes across the whole image. The transformer’s bet, which the rest of this book explores, is the opposite one: bake in less structure, and let scale plus data recover it. Both bets can be right — at different data scales.

From AlexNet to ResNet — and where convolutions live in 2026

🕰History

The convolutional idea is old — Fukushima’s Neocognitron (1980) and LeCun’s LeNet (1989–1998, reading handwritten digits on real US mail) both predate the deep learning era. The modern story runs through three ImageNet results. AlexNet (Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton, 2012) won the 2012 ImageNet competition with 15.3% top-5 error against the best non-neural runner-up’s 26.2% — a gap so large it converted the field overnight. Its ingredients read like a checklist of this book’s Part II: ReLU activations, dropout, and training on graphics processing units (GPUs), two GTX 580s, which is the detail that foreshadows everything. VGG (Simonyan & Zisserman, 2014) showed that architectural cleverness could be replaced by uniform depth: 16–19 layers of nothing but 3×3 convolutions, arguing that two stacked 3×3 layers see a 5×5 window with fewer parameters and more nonlinearity than one 5×5 layer. ResNet (He et al., 2015) broke the depth barrier — plain stacks degraded past ~20 layers — with the skip connection, training 152 layers to 3.57% top-5 error. You have already met this idea properly in the residual connections chapter; here, note only that it was discovered as a CNN fix, and that (as we will see shortly) the LSTM had found the same additive-path trick for the time axis eighteen years earlier.

⚙️In practice

Convolutions did not disappear in 2026; they moved to the edges of the architectures that replaced them. The Vision Transformer, or ViT (Dosovitskiy et al., 2020), begins by “patchifying” the image — cutting it into 16×16 patches and linearly projecting each to an embedding — and that operation is a convolution: a 16×16 kernel applied with stride 16, so the windows tile the image without overlapping. Every ViT-style vision encoder in today’s multimodal models starts with that strided-conv stem. Speech models keep small convolutional front-ends to downsample raw audio features before attention (Whisper’s encoder opens with two conv layers). And image generation ran on convolutions well into the diffusion era: Stable Diffusion’s denoiser was a convolutional U-Net, and even now that diffusion transformers have largely taken over the denoiser, the variational autoencoder (VAE) that maps between pixels and latents remains fully convolutional. Plus the unglamorous truth: away from the frontier, on phones, cameras, and embedded hardware, plain CNNs still do an enormous share of the world’s actual vision inference.

Recurrence: networks with memory

Now for sequences. Language, audio, and sensor streams share a problem images do not have: variable length. A dense network has a fixed input size; a sentence does not. The 2014-era answer: process the sequence one step at a time, and carry a running summary.

A recurrent neural network (RNN) maintains a hidden state hth_tht​ — a vector, say 512 numbers — that is its memory. At each time step it reads the next input xtx_txt​, combines it with the previous state, and produces a new state:

ht  =  f(ht−1, xt).h_t \;=\; f(h_{t-1},\, x_t).ht​=f(ht−1​,xt​).

That one equation is the whole architecture. Everything the network knows about the sequence so far must live in hth_tht​.

∑The math

The vanilla (“Elman”) RNN makes fff a single dense layer applied to both inputs:

ht  =  tanh⁡ ⁣(Wh ht−1  +  Wx xt  +  b),h_t \;=\; \tanh\!\big(W_h\, h_{t-1} \;+\; W_x\, x_t \;+\; b\big),ht​=tanh(Wh​ht−1​+Wx​xt​+b),

where WhW_hWh​ (state-to-state) and WxW_xWx​ (input-to-state) are weight matrices and bbb a bias, all shared across every time step — one set of weights, no matter how long the sequence. An output head yt=Wyhty_t = W_y h_tyt​=Wy​ht​ can read a prediction from the state at each step (for language modeling: a distribution over the next word). h0h_0h0​ is initialized to zeros.

Notice the symmetry with convolution. A CNN shares weights across space and gains translation equivariance: a pattern detector works at every position. An RNN shares weights across time and gains the analogous prior: the rule for updating your understanding given a new word should be the same at word 3 and word 300. Weight sharing is the same idea both times — pick the axis along which your data’s rules are uniform, and reuse parameters along it.

To train an RNN, we unroll it: draw one copy of the cell per time step, wired left to right. Unrolled, an RNN processing a 40-word sentence is a 40-layer-deep feedforward network in which every layer has identical weights — and backpropagation applies exactly as you learned it. Applied here it gets a grander name, backpropagation through time (BPTT), but it is the ordinary chain rule walking right-to-left through the unrolled graph, summing each weight’s gradient contributions across all the steps where it was used.

Backprop through time and the vanishing gradient

You met the product-of-Jacobians argument in the chapter on initialization and gradient pathologies: in a deep network, the gradient reaching layer 1 is a product of one Jacobian per layer, so it shrinks or blows up exponentially in depth unless each factor is delicately near 1. The unrolled RNN is that same deep network — with one extra cruelty. In an ordinary deep net, each layer has its own weights, so some factors can be slightly big and others slightly small, partially cancelling. In an RNN every factor contains the same WhW_hWh​. The product does not get the mercy of cancellation; it compounds one matrix’s behavior, TTT times.

∑The math

How does the loss at step TTT depend on the state way back at step ttt? Chain rule through every intermediate step:

∂hT∂ht  =  ∏k=t+1T∂hk∂hk−1  =  ∏k=t+1Tdiag⁡ ⁣(tanh⁡′(ak)) Wh,\frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_t} \;=\; \prod_{k=t+1}^{T} \frac{\partial h_k}{\partial h_{k-1}} \;=\; \prod_{k=t+1}^{T} \operatorname{diag}\!\big(\tanh'(a_k)\big)\, W_h,∂ht​∂hT​​=k=t+1∏T​∂hk−1​∂hk​​=k=t+1∏T​diag(tanh′(ak​))Wh​,

where ak=Whhk−1+Wxxk+ba_k = W_h h_{k-1} + W_x x_k + bak​=Wh​hk−1​+Wx​xk​+b is step kkk‘s pre-activation and diag⁡(tanh⁡′(ak))\operatorname{diag}(\tanh'(a_k))diag(tanh′(ak​)) is the diagonal matrix of elementwise derivatives. Take norms. Since tanh⁡′\tanh'tanh′ lies in (0,1](0, 1](0,1], each factor’s norm is at most σmax⁡(Wh)\sigma_{\max}(W_h)σmax​(Wh​), the largest singular value of WhW_hWh​ (the most a matrix can stretch any vector — see matrices as transformations):

∥∂hT∂ht∥  ≤  (σmax⁡(Wh)) T−t.\left\lVert \frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_t} \right\rVert \;\le\; \big(\sigma_{\max}(W_h)\big)^{\,T-t}.​∂ht​∂hT​​​≤(σmax​(Wh​))T−t.

If σmax⁡(Wh)<1\sigma_{\max}(W_h) < 1σmax​(Wh​)<1, the gradient from step TTT back to step ttt vanishes exponentially in the gap T−tT-tT−t; and Pascanu, Mikolov & Bengio (2013) showed the converse side too: when relevant singular values exceed 1, gradients can explode exponentially instead. It is the depth argument from Chapter 12, transplanted to the time axis — with sequence length playing the role of depth, and no per-layer freedom to soften the product.

✏️Worked example

Shrink everything to one dimension so you can watch it happen. Let ht=tanh⁡(w ht−1+xt)h_t = \tanh(w\, h_{t-1} + x_t)ht​=tanh(wht−1​+xt​) with a single scalar weight www. Then ∂hT/∂ht=∏ktanh⁡′(ak) w\partial h_T / \partial h_t = \prod_k \tanh'(a_k)\, w∂hT​/∂ht​=∏k​tanh′(ak​)w, and since ∣tanh⁡′∣≤1\lvert\tanh'\rvert \le 1∣tanh′∣≤1, the product is at most ∣w∣T−t\lvert w \rvert^{T-t}∣w∣T−t.

With w=0.5w = 0.5w=0.5, twenty steps back: 0.520≈9.5×10−70.5^{20} \approx 9.5 \times 10^{-7}0.520≈9.5×10−7 — under one millionth of the gradient survives. Whatever word 1 should have taught the network about word 21, the learning signal arrives a million times fainter than the signal from word 20. With w=1.2w = 1.2w=1.2 and activations in tanh⁡\tanhtanh‘s near-linear zone, the same twenty steps give 1.220≈381.2^{20} \approx 381.220≈38 — and forty steps give 1.240≈1,4701.2^{40} \approx 1{,}4701.240≈1,470. There is no comfortable middle: you need the effective factor pinned almost exactly to 1, at every step, across training. In practice, a vanilla RNN reliably learns dependencies over roughly 5–10 steps and effectively nothing beyond tens of steps.

⚠️Careful

Vanishing and exploding gradients feel like one disease but get different medicine, and mixing them up wastes weeks. Exploding gradients are the easy one: they announce themselves (loss spikes, NaNs — not-a-number values) and are treated with gradient clipping — if the gradient norm exceeds a threshold, rescale it down to the threshold — a blunt fix from Pascanu et al. that still ships in every 2026 LLM training run. Vanishing gradients are silent: training proceeds, loss falls, and the model quietly never learns long-range structure. No rescaling trick fixes a signal that has already rounded to zero; you need a different architecture. Hence the LSTM.

The LSTM: gates on a memory highway

The Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997) attacks the product head-on. The vanishing argument shows the killer is multiplication: every step multiplies the gradient by another factor. The LSTM’s fix: give the network a second state that is updated additively, so that gradient flow along it is multiplication-free by default — then let the network learn when to write to it, when to erase it, and when to read from it.

💡Intuition

Picture the LSTM’s cell state ctc_tct​ as a conveyor belt running through time. By default, whatever is on the belt stays on the belt — no transformation, no squashing, no repeated multiplication by WhW_hWh​. Three learned gates, each a sigmoid producing values between 0 and 1 (soft switches: 0 means closed, 1 means open), control all traffic: the forget gate decides what fraction of each belt slot to erase, the input gate decides how much new information to place on the belt, and the output gate decides how much of the belt to reveal as this step’s working output hth_tht​. “Remember the subject of this sentence until the verb arrives, then move on” becomes: input gate opens at the subject, forget gate holds near 1 across the intervening clause, output gate reads it out at the verb, forget gate drops toward 0 afterward.

∑The math

All three gates, and the candidate content, are computed the same way — a dense layer over the concatenation [ht−1,xt][h_{t-1}, x_t][ht−1​,xt​], each with its own weights:

ft=σ(Wf [ht−1,xt]+bf)forget gate: what to keep of the old cell stateit=σ(Wi [ht−1,xt]+bi)input gate: how much new content to admitc~t=tanh⁡(Wc [ht−1,xt]+bc)candidate: the new content itselfot=σ(Wo [ht−1,xt]+bo)output gate: how much of the cell to expose\begin{aligned} f_t &= \sigma\big(W_f\,[h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_f\big) &&\text{forget gate: what to keep of the old cell state}\\ i_t &= \sigma\big(W_i\,[h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_i\big) &&\text{input gate: how much new content to admit}\\ \tilde{c}_t &= \tanh\big(W_c\,[h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_c\big) &&\text{candidate: the new content itself}\\ o_t &= \sigma\big(W_o\,[h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_o\big) &&\text{output gate: how much of the cell to expose} \end{aligned}ft​it​c~t​ot​​=σ(Wf​[ht−1​,xt​]+bf​)=σ(Wi​[ht−1​,xt​]+bi​)=tanh(Wc​[ht−1​,xt​]+bc​)=σ(Wo​[ht−1​,xt​]+bo​)​​forget gate: what to keep of the old cell stateinput gate: how much new content to admitcandidate: the new content itselfoutput gate: how much of the cell to expose​

with ⊙\odot⊙ denoting elementwise multiplication. Now differentiate the cell path:

∂ct∂ct−1  =  diag⁡(ft).\frac{\partial c_t}{\partial c_{t-1}} \;=\; \operatorname{diag}(f_t).∂ct−1​∂ct​​=diag(ft​).

No WhW_hWh​. No tanh⁡′\tanh'tanh′. The Jacobian along the cell path is whatever the forget gate says it is — and when the network sets ft≈1f_t \approx 1ft​≈1, that factor is ≈1\approx 1≈1 and the gradient product across a long stretch of time stays ≈1\approx 1≈1 instead of decaying as 0.5200.5^{20}0.520. The vanishing problem is not solved by clever initialization of a multiplicative path; it is solved by building an additive path and handing the network the keys. Hochreiter and Schmidhuber called this the constant error carousel. Eighteen years later, ResNets rediscovered the same principle for depth: xl+1=xl+F(xl)x_{l+1} = x_l + F(x_l)xl+1​=xl​+F(xl​) has ∂xl+1/∂xl=I+…\partial x_{l+1}/\partial x_l = I + \ldots∂xl+1​/∂xl​=I+… — identity plus correction. One trick, two axes, arguably the most important single design idea in deep learning.

And the gradient factor through the cell path for this step is exactly ft=0.900f_t = 0.900ft​=0.900 — after 20 such steps, 0.920≈0.120.9^{20} \approx 0.120.920≈0.12 of the signal survives, versus the 10−610^{-6}10−6 of the vanilla example. A trained LSTM pushes forget gates far closer to 1 than this on dimensions that matter; a standard practical trick is to initialize bfb_fbf​ to 1 or 2 so the highway starts open.

The gated recurrent unit (GRU) (Cho et al., 2014) is the LSTM’s popular simplification: it merges cell state and hidden state into one vector and uses two gates instead of three — an update gate that interpolates between keeping the old state and adopting the new candidate (playing forget and input as one lever, so the highway is built into the interpolation), and a reset gate controlling how much old state enters the candidate. Roughly three-quarters of the LSTM’s parameters, and in most 2015-era head-to-heads the two were within noise of each other. When you wanted a slightly cheaper LSTM, you used a GRU; nobody ever fully settled which was “better”, and then the question stopped mattering.

seq2seq and the fixed-vector bottleneck

By 2014 LSTMs could read sequences. The breakthrough application was making them write sequences too: machine translation, where input and output are both sentences and their lengths differ. The sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) architecture (Sutskever, Vinyals & Le, 2014; concurrently Cho et al., 2014) is two RNNs in a row. An encoder LSTM reads the source sentence one token at a time and — this is the key design decision — its final hidden state, one fixed-size vector, is taken as the representation of the entire sentence. A decoder LSTM is initialized with that vector and generates the target sentence token by token, each step feeding on its own previous output. Sutskever et al. trained this for English→French and landed within reach of the best hand-engineered statistical systems — a shocking result for a model that knew nothing about grammar, alignment, or phrase tables.

Look at the rose box and quantify what it is being asked to do. A typical setup carried a hidden state of around 512 dimensions. A 40-word sentence, compressed into 512 floating-point numbers, from which a fluent, faithful, 40-word translation must be reconstructed — every name, number, negation, and nested clause. And the vector does not grow with the sentence: a 10-word sentence and a 200-word legal clause pass through the same 512 slots. The researcher Ray Mooney’s famous (and famously profane) complaint from this era was that you cannot cram the meaning of a whole sentence into a single vector — and empirically he was right: translation quality in these systems degraded measurably as source sentences grew longer, precisely the signature of a representation running out of room.

✎Note

Two honest footnotes to the classic story. First, Sutskever et al.’s actual model was bigger than the caricature — 4 stacked LSTM layers of 1,000 units, so the full handoff was several thousand numbers — and its most famous practical trick was reversing the source sentence, which shortens the distance between the start of the source and the start of the target, easing exactly the long-range gradient problem you now understand. Second, the bottleneck is a design flaw, not a law of nature: within a year, Bahdanau, Cho & Bengio (2014) fixed it by letting the decoder look back at all encoder states, choosing dynamically which to consult at each output step. That mechanism is attention, and it is where the next part of this book begins — attention was born as a patch for the RNN bottleneck, and only later did anyone ask whether the RNN under it was needed at all.

Why transformers displaced RNNs

You now have every ingredient needed to state, concretely, why the architecture in this chapter lost. The argument has three parts. None of them is “attention is smarter”.

1. Recurrence cannot be parallelized across time — and GPUs are parallel machines. The recurrence ht=f(ht−1,xt)h_t = f(h_{t-1}, x_t)ht​=f(ht−1​,xt​) makes step ttt‘s computation depend on step t−1t-1t−1‘s result. Training on a 1,000-token sequence therefore requires 1,000 strictly sequential cell evaluations — 1,000 small matrix-vector products, each waiting on the last — no matter how many thousands of parallel cores your GPU has idling. A transformer processes the same 1,000 tokens during training as a handful of large matrix-matrix multiplications over all positions at once: its sequential depth is the number of layers (dozens), not the number of tokens (thousands to millions). Same total floating-point operations, radically different hardware utilization. When your budget is measured in GPU-months, an architecture that keeps the silicon busy wins the scaling race almost regardless of its other virtues.

2. Long-range gradient paths of length nnn versus length 1. This chapter’s central math: information (and gradient) connecting token 1 to token nnn in an RNN traverses nnn steps, and even the LSTM’s highway only defends the signal against decay — 0.972000.97^{200}0.97200 is still 0.0020.0020.002; the highway is a good road, not teleportation. In a transformer, attention connects every pair of positions directly: the path from token 1 to token 1,000 is one attention hop, within a single layer. Learning a dependency at range 500 no longer requires a gradient to survive 500 chained Jacobians; it requires one attention weight to grow.

3. The bottleneck versus random access. The seq2seq handoff forced a whole sentence through one fixed vector; even a plain RNN language model forces everything relevant about the entire past through the fixed-size hth_tht​. The model must decide what to keep before knowing what will matter later — memory management under uncertainty, learned with noisy gradients. A transformer keeps every position’s representation around and lets each new token query all of them after seeing what it needs (at a cost — the KV cache — that Part IV treats in detail). Attention is random-access memory where recurrence is a stream you sip once.

⚙️In practice

The receipts, briefly. LSTM language models were state of the art as late as 2016–2017 — Google’s “Exploring the Limits of Language Modeling” work and the first production neural translation systems were large LSTMs. Then “Attention Is All You Need” (2017) showed a pure-attention model beating the best recurrent translation systems while training in a fraction of the time — the training-efficiency claim was front and center in the paper, not a footnote. What happened next was the deeper verdict: transformers kept improving smoothly as parameters, data, and compute grew — the scaling laws story — while recurrent models’ sequential training made the same scale-up experiments painfully slow even to attempt. By GPT-2 (2019) the question was closed; no frontier language model since has been a pure RNN.

And yet — the honest coda. Every property has a price, and the transformer pays for random access at inference time: generating token nnn means attending over all n−1n-1n−1 previous positions, with a memory footprint that grows linearly in context length. An RNN’s inference is the mirror image: constant memory, constant cost per token — the entire past is, for better and worse, those 512 numbers. As contexts stretched to hundreds of thousands of tokens, that trade-off stopped looking one-sided, and recurrence returned in modern dress: state-space models like Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2023) are, at heart, recurrences engineered (via linearity in the state) to also train in parallel — an attempt to keep this chapter’s inference economics and the transformer’s training economics at once. Several 2025–2026 production models hybridize the two. That story gets its own chapter: state-space models. The world before transformers is not entirely behind us.

Where this leaves us

  • You can execute a convolution by hand — slide, dot, map — and you computed a vertical-edge detector’s full 3×3 feature map from a 5×5 input.
  • You can name the two priors a CNN hard-codes (locality; translation equivariance via weight sharing) and put numbers on the payoff: ~1.8k conv parameters where a dense layer needs ~617M.
  • You can trace the arc AlexNet → VGG → ResNet, and point to where convolutions still live in 2026: ViT patchify stems (a strided conv), audio front-ends, and the convolutional VAEs of latent diffusion.
  • You can unroll ht=f(ht−1,xt)h_t = f(h_{t-1}, x_t)ht​=f(ht−1​,xt​) into a deep network sharing one weight matrix over time, and derive why ∂hT/∂ht\partial h_T/\partial h_t∂hT​/∂ht​ decays or explodes like σmax⁡(Wh)T−t\sigma_{\max}(W_h)^{T-t}σmax​(Wh​)T−t — the Chapter 12 product argument, transplanted from depth to time.
  • You can explain each LSTM gate’s job, run one cell update numerically, and state the deep point: ∂ct/∂ct−1=ft\partial c_t / \partial c_{t-1} = f_t∂ct​/∂ct−1​=ft​ — an additive highway is the same trick as a residual connection, found first.
  • You can quantify the seq2seq bottleneck — an entire sentence through ~512 numbers, regardless of length — and say why attention was invented to fix it.
  • You can make the three-part case for the transformer takeover — parallel training, path length 1 versus nnn, random access versus the bottleneck — and its inference-cost fine print, which is why recurrence is quietly returning.
