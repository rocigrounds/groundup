Matrices as Transformations

A matrix may seem like a grid of numbers, but it is more importantly a function that moves space.

In this chapter
  1. From grids of numbers to functions that move space
  2. The matrix–vector product, two ways

  5. Rank: how much of space survives
  6. Eigenvectors: the directions that only stretch

  8. Matrices are what neural networks are made of

A neural network is mostly matrices. If you learned matrices in school, you probably learned them as grids of numbers with an arbitrary-seeming multiplication rule. That framing is an obstacle to understanding deep learning.

Consider instead that a matrix is a function. It takes a vector in and produces a vector out. If you feed it every vector in space at once, it moves space — rotating it, stretching it, squashing it, shearing it, sometimes flattening it. The grid of numbers is a compact recipe for that motion. Once you see matrices this way, the multiplication rule stops being a ritual and becomes the only rule it could possibly be, and the core operations of a transformer — attention’s queries and keys, every layer’s weights, embedding lookups — become geometric actions you can picture.

This chapter builds that picture from the ground up. We take the vectors of the previous chapter and learn what it means to transform them: the matrix–vector product from two complementary directions, matrix–matrix multiplication as function composition, projections (the “shadow” operation that attention performs constantly), and then the deeper structure — rank, eigenvectors, and the singular value decomposition — that explains why a technique like LoRA (low-rank adaptation) can fine-tune a billion-parameter model by touching a fraction of a percent of it.

From grids of numbers to functions that move space

Start with the smallest interesting case: a 2×22 \times 22×2 matrix acting on 2-dimensional vectors,

A=[2101].A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.A=[20​11​].

We will define the multiplication rule in a moment, but first, the punchline it serves. A matrix is a linear transformation: a function fff from vectors to vectors that respects the two operations vectors have. Scaling before or after the function makes no difference, f(c x)=c f(x)f(c\,\mathbf{x}) = c\,f(\mathbf{x})f(cx)=cf(x), and the same for addition, f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)f(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}) = f(\mathbf{x}) + f(\mathbf{y})f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y). Geometrically: the function may rotate, stretch, shear, or flatten space, but it keeps grid lines straight, parallel, and evenly spaced, and it leaves the origin where it is. No bending, no tearing.

That constraint has a startling consequence. Every 2D vector can be written in terms of the two standard basis vectors e1=(1,0)\mathbf{e}_1 = (1, 0)e1​=(1,0) and e2=(0,1)\mathbf{e}_2 = (0, 1)e2​=(0,1): the vector (3,1)(3, 1)(3,1) is 3e1+1e23\mathbf{e}_1 + 1\mathbf{e}_23e1​+1e2​, by definition of coordinates. Apply a linear fff and use both rules:

f((3,1))=f(3e1+1e2)=3 f(e1)+1 f(e2).f\big((3,1)\big) = f(3\mathbf{e}_1 + 1\mathbf{e}_2) = 3\,f(\mathbf{e}_1) + 1\,f(\mathbf{e}_2).f((3,1))=f(3e1​+1e2​)=3f(e1​)+1f(e2​).

The output is completely determined by where e1\mathbf{e}_1e1​ and e2\mathbf{e}_2e2​ land. Know those two landing spots and you know the entire function — its effect on all infinitely many vectors in the plane. So a linear transformation of 2D space can be recorded with exactly four numbers: two coordinates for where e1\mathbf{e}_1e1​ goes, two for where e2\mathbf{e}_2e2​ goes.

That record is the matrix. The columns of a matrix are the landing spots of the basis vectors. Our matrix AAA above says: send e1\mathbf{e}_1e1​ to (2,0)(2, 0)(2,0) — first column — and send e2\mathbf{e}_2e2​ to (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1) — second column. Everything else follows.

💡Intuition

Read a matrix column by column and it stops being a grid of numbers. Each column answers one question: “where does this basis direction end up?” A 2×22 \times 22×2 matrix is two answers; a 1000×10001000 \times 10001000×1000 matrix is a thousand answers, one per input direction. The multiplication rule we define next is nothing but the bookkeeping that turns those answers into the output for any input vector.

One more piece of vocabulary before the mechanics. Matrices need not be square. An m×nm \times nm×n matrix (that is mmm rows, nnn columns — rows first, always) has nnn columns, so it records the landing spots of nnn basis vectors, but each landing spot has mmm coordinates. It is therefore a function from nnn-dimensional space to mmm-dimensional space: it takes vectors in Rn\R^nRn and produces vectors in Rm\R^mRm. A 3×23 \times 23×2 matrix lifts the plane into 3D space; a 2×32 \times 32×3 matrix flattens 3D space onto the plane. Deep learning uses rectangular matrices constantly — a layer that maps a 4,096-dimensional representation to a 14,336-dimensional one is a 14,336×4,09614{,}336 \times 4{,}09614,336×4,096 matrix.

The matrix–vector product, two ways

Now the rule. There are two ways to compute AxA\mathbf{x}Ax, and they give the same answer while teaching different lessons. You need both. The rows view is how you compute by hand and how hardware computes; the columns view is how you should think.

∑The math

Let AAA be an m×nm \times nm×n matrix with entries AijA_{ij}Aij​ (row iii, column jjj), and let x=(x1,…,xn)\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_n)x=(x1​,…,xn​) be a vector in Rn\R^nRn. The product y=Ax\mathbf{y} = A\mathbf{x}y=Ax is a vector in Rm\R^mRm whose iii-th entry is

yi  =  ∑j=1nAij xj.y_i \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{n} A_{ij}\, x_j .yi​=j=1∑n​Aij​xj​.

Rows view. Fix iii and look at the sum: it multiplies the entries of row iii pairwise against the entries of x\mathbf{x}x and adds them up. That is a dot product — the similarity-measuring operation from Chapter 1. So each output entry is the dot product of one row of AAA with the input:

Ax=[row1⋅x⋮rowm⋅x].A\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} \text{row}_1 \cdot \mathbf{x} \\ \vdots \\ \text{row}_m \cdot \mathbf{x} \end{bmatrix}.Ax=​row1​⋅x⋮rowm​⋅x​​.

Columns view. Now regroup the same sum by jjj instead of iii. Each xjx_jxj​ multiplies the entire jjj-th column aj\mathbf{a}_jaj​ of AAA:

Ax  =  x1a1+x2a2+⋯+xnan.A\mathbf{x} \;=\; x_1 \mathbf{a}_1 + x_2 \mathbf{a}_2 + \cdots + x_n \mathbf{a}_n .Ax=x1​a1​+x2​a2​+⋯+xn​an​.

The output is a linear combination of the columns of AAA, weighted by the entries of x\mathbf{x}x — exactly the formula f(x)=x1f(e1)+⋯+xnf(en)f(\mathbf{x}) = x_1 f(\mathbf{e}_1) + \cdots + x_n f(\mathbf{e}_n)f(x)=x1​f(e1​)+⋯+xn​f(en​) we derived from linearity, with the columns playing the role of the landing spots f(ej)f(\mathbf{e}_j)f(ej​).

✏️Worked example

Take A=[2101]A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}A=[20​11​] and x=(3,1)\mathbf{x} = (3, 1)x=(3,1).

Rows view. Row 1 is (2,1)(2, 1)(2,1), so y1=2⋅3+1⋅1=7y_1 = 2 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 1 = 7y1​=2⋅3+1⋅1=7. Row 2 is (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1), so y2=0⋅3+1⋅1=1y_2 = 0 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 1 = 1y2​=0⋅3+1⋅1=1. Result: Ax=(7,1)A\mathbf{x} = (7, 1)Ax=(7,1).

Columns view. Take 3 copies of column one plus 1 copy of column two:

3[20]+1[11]=[60]+[11]=[71].3 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + 1 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.3[20​]+1[11​]=[60​]+[11​]=[71​].

Same answer, different story. The rows view says “the output’s first coordinate measures how much x\mathbf{x}x aligns with (2,1)(2,1)(2,1).” The columns view says ”x\mathbf{x}x was 3 parts e1\mathbf{e}_1e1​ and 1 part e2\mathbf{e}_2e2​, so the output is 3 parts wherever-e1\mathbf{e}_1e1​-landed and 1 part wherever-e2\mathbf{e}_2e2​-landed.”

Why insist on both? Because each is the right lens for a different half of deep learning. When a network computes attention scores, the rows view is the truth: each row of a matrix is a probe, and each output number is a similarity measurement. When a network builds a representation — say, mixing token embeddings — the columns view is the truth: the output is a blend of learned direction vectors, weighted by the input. Being able to flip between “bank of dot-product detectors” and “recipe for combining columns” without recomputing anything is the core skill this chapter exists to install.

⚠️Careful

Matrix–vector multiplication is not entrywise. AxA\mathbf{x}Ax does not multiply AijA_{ij}Aij​ by xjx_jxj​ and leave a grid; it produces a vector, and every output entry generally depends on every input entry. Deep learning frameworks do also offer entrywise (Hadamard) products — written ⊙\odot⊙ — and confusing the two is a classic source of silently wrong code, since broadcasting rules will often let the wrong one run without error.

The best way to make this geometric picture permanent is to push matrices around yourself. In the demo below, drag the landing spots of e1\mathbf{e}_1e1​ and e2\mathbf{e}_2e2​ — that is, edit the columns — and watch the whole plane follow. Try making the two columns nearly parallel and watch the plane squash toward a line; that degenerate case is where rank, later in this chapter, becomes real.

Composition: why matrix multiplication looks the way it does

If a matrix is a function, an inevitable question follows: what happens when you apply one after another? First rotate, then stretch. First embed, then project. Applying transformation BBB and then transformation AAA to a vector x\mathbf{x}x computes A(Bx)A(B\mathbf{x})A(Bx). Composition of linear functions is itself linear (it still keeps grids straight and the origin fixed), so there must exist a single matrix that does in one step what AAA-after-BBB does in two. That matrix is what we define the product ABABAB to be.

The bizarre school rule falls straight out of this definition. To find column jjj of ABABAB, ask where ej\mathbf{e}_jej​ lands: first BBB sends it to bj\mathbf{b}_jbj​ (the jjj-th column of BBB), then AAA sends that to AbjA\mathbf{b}_jAbj​. So:

AB=[Ab1Ab2⋯Abn]— each column of AB is A applied to a column of B,AB = \begin{bmatrix} A\mathbf{b}_1 & A\mathbf{b}_2 & \cdots & A\mathbf{b}_n \end{bmatrix} \qquad\text{— each column of } AB \text{ is } A \text{ applied to a column of } B,AB=[Ab1​​Ab2​​⋯​Abn​​]— each column of AB is A applied to a column of B,

and each of those matrix–vector products resolves, by the rows view, into “row of AAA dot column of BBB” — the familiar recipe, now with a reason:

(AB)ij=∑kAikBkj=rowi(A)⋅colj(B).(AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k} A_{ik} B_{kj} = \text{row}_i(A) \cdot \text{col}_j(B).(AB)ij​=k∑​Aik​Bkj​=rowi​(A)⋅colj​(B).

The shape rule also stops being arbitrary. If BBB maps Rn→Rk\R^n \to \R^kRn→Rk (a k×nk \times nk×n matrix) and AAA maps Rk→Rm\R^k \to \R^mRk→Rm (an m×km \times km×k matrix), the composite maps Rn→Rm\R^n \to \R^mRn→Rm. The inner dimensions — BBB‘s output size and AAA‘s input size, both kkk — must match for the same reason a garden hose must match the tap: the output of the first function has to be a legal input for the second. When your deep-learning code throws a shape-mismatch error, it is telling you that you tried to compose two functions whose middle dimension disagrees.

✏️Worked example

Let S=[2001]S = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}S=[20​01​] (stretch the xxx-direction by 2) and R=[0−110]R = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}R=[01​−10​] (rotate 90° counterclockwise — check the columns: e1→(0,1)\mathbf{e}_1 \to (0,1)e1​→(0,1), e2→(−1,0)\mathbf{e}_2 \to (-1,0)e2​→(−1,0)).

Stretch, then rotate is RSRSRS (read right-to-left, like function notation f(g(x))f(g(x))f(g(x))):

RS=[0−110][2001]=[0−120].RS = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.RS=[01​−10​][20​01​]=[02​−10​].

Rotate, then stretch is SRSRSR:

SR=[2001][0−110]=[0−210].SR = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.SR=[20​01​][01​−10​]=[01​−20​].

Different matrices — and you can see why with e1\mathbf{e}_1e1​. Stretch first: e1→(2,0)→(0,2)\mathbf{e}_1 \to (2,0) \to (0,2)e1​→(2,0)→(0,2), a vector of length 2 pointing up. Rotate first: e1→(0,1)→(0,1)\mathbf{e}_1 \to (0,1) \to (0,1)e1​→(0,1)→(0,1), length 1, because the stretch acts on the xxx-direction and the rotation had already moved the vector out of it.

Matrix multiplication is therefore not commutative: AB≠BAAB \neq BAAB=BA in general. This is not a defect but a faithful report about the world — order of operations matters for functions (“put on socks, then shoes” is not “put on shoes, then socks”), so it must matter for the matrices that represent them. It is associative, (AB)C=A(BC)(AB)C = A(BC)(AB)C=A(BC), because both sides describe the same three-step pipeline; only the grouping of the bookkeeping differs. That freedom to regroup is quietly load-bearing in practice: computing A(Bx)A(B\mathbf{x})A(Bx) as two matrix–vector products is vastly cheaper than forming the full matrix ABABAB first, and choosing the cheapest grouping is a real optimization in every deep learning framework.

Two special cases complete the toolkit. The identity matrix III — ones on the diagonal, zeros elsewhere — is the do-nothing function: its columns say ”e1\mathbf{e}_1e1​ lands on e1\mathbf{e}_1e1​, e2\mathbf{e}_2e2​ lands on e2\mathbf{e}_2e2​,” so Ix=xI\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}Ix=x for every x\mathbf{x}x. And the inverse A−1A^{-1}A−1, when it exists, is the undo function: the unique matrix with A−1A=AA−1=IA^{-1}A = AA^{-1} = IA−1A=AA−1=I. Rotations are undone by rotating back; a stretch by 2 is undone by a stretch by 12\tfrac1221​.

The operative phrase is when it exists. A transformation can be undone only if it never merges two different inputs into the same output. A rotation loses nothing, so it is invertible. But consider a matrix that flattens the whole plane onto a line: infinitely many input vectors pile onto each output point, and no function can look at the output and recover which input produced it. The information is gone. Such matrices are called singular, and the next two sections are about understanding exactly how and where a matrix destroys information — starting with the most important information-destroying operation in deep learning.

Projections: the shadow of a vector

Here is an expression you will meet dozens of times in this book, usually introduced in passing as if it were obvious:

(x⋅u) u— "the projection of x onto u."(\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u})\,\mathbf{u} \quad\text{— "the projection of } \mathbf{x} \text{ onto } \mathbf{u}\text{."}(x⋅u)u— "the projection of x onto u."

If your honest reaction is what? — a vector dotted with a vector, times the second vector again, why? — you are in good company, and this section exists for you. We are going to build it slowly, because this one expression, fully understood, unlocks attention heads, LoRA, and most of interpretability.

The physical picture first. Stand a stick in the ground at an angle, at noon, with the sun directly overhead. The stick casts a shadow on the ground. The shadow is what remains of the stick as seen by the ground: all the stick’s height is discarded, all its along-the-ground extent is kept. Projection is exactly this. Given a vector x\mathbf{x}x and a direction of interest u\mathbf{u}u, the projection of x\mathbf{x}x onto u\mathbf{u}u is the shadow x\mathbf{x}x casts on the line through u\mathbf{u}u, with the light shining perpendicular to that line. It answers: how much of x\mathbf{x}x lies along the u\mathbf{u}u direction, and nothing else?

Now let’s earn the formula, in two steps. Throughout, u\mathbf{u}u is a unit vector — length exactly 1. That is not decoration; the formula is wrong without it, for reasons about to become clear.

Step 1: x⋅u\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u}x⋅u is the length of the shadow. Recall from Chapter 1 the geometric identity for the dot product: x⋅u=∥x∥ ∥u∥cos⁡θ\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u} = \|\mathbf{x}\|\,\|\mathbf{u}\|\cos\thetax⋅u=∥x∥∥u∥cosθ, where θ\thetaθ is the angle between the vectors and ∥⋅∥\|\cdot\|∥⋅∥ denotes length. Basic trigonometry says the shadow of a stick of length ∥x∥\|\mathbf{x}\|∥x∥, leaning at angle θ\thetaθ from the ground, has length ∥x∥cos⁡θ\|\mathbf{x}\|\cos\theta∥x∥cosθ — the adjacent side of the right triangle formed by the stick, the ground, and the vertical light ray. And since u\mathbf{u}u has length 1, the identity collapses to

x⋅u=∥x∥cos⁡θ=length of the shadow.\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u} = \|\mathbf{x}\| \cos\theta = \text{length of the shadow.}x⋅u=∥x∥cosθ=length of the shadow.

So the dot product with a unit vector is a ruler reading: a single number telling you how far along the u\mathbf{u}u direction the vector x\mathbf{x}x reaches. (If θ\thetaθ is obtuse, the reading is negative: the shadow points the other way. That is a feature — “how much of u\mathbf{u}u-ness does x\mathbf{x}x have” can be negative.)

Step 2: multiplying by u\mathbf{u}u turns the reading back into a vector. A length is not a shadow; the shadow is an arrow lying on the ground. We know its length (x⋅u\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u}x⋅u, from step 1) and its direction (along u\mathbf{u}u). The vector with a given length in a given unit direction is length-times-direction. Hence:

shadow  =  (x⋅u)⏟how far (a number)  u⏟which way (a unit vector).\text{shadow} \;=\; \underbrace{(\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u})}_{\text{how far (a number)}} \; \underbrace{\mathbf{u}}_{\text{which way (a unit vector)}}.shadow=how far (a number)(x⋅u)​​which way (a unit vector)u​​.

That is the whole formula. Dot to measure, multiply to rebuild. The first u\mathbf{u}u is used as a measuring device; the second u\mathbf{u}u is used as a direction to lay the measurement along. The same vector, playing two roles — which is exactly why the expression looks cryptic until someone says so.

✏️Worked example

Let x=(2,4)\mathbf{x} = (2, 4)x=(2,4) and u=(0.8,0.6)\mathbf{u} = (0.8, 0.6)u=(0.8,0.6) — a legitimate unit vector, since 0.82+0.62=0.64+0.36=10.8^2 + 0.6^2 = 0.64 + 0.36 = 10.82+0.62=0.64+0.36=1.

Measure: x⋅u=2(0.8)+4(0.6)=1.6+2.4=4\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u} = 2(0.8) + 4(0.6) = 1.6 + 2.4 = 4x⋅u=2(0.8)+4(0.6)=1.6+2.4=4. The shadow is 4 units long.

Rebuild: (x⋅u) u=4×(0.8,0.6)=(3.2,2.4)(\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u})\,\mathbf{u} = 4 \times (0.8, 0.6) = (3.2, 2.4)(x⋅u)u=4×(0.8,0.6)=(3.2,2.4).

Check the leftover: the discarded part is x−(3.2,2.4)=(−1.2,1.6)\mathbf{x} - (3.2, 2.4) = (-1.2, 1.6)x−(3.2,2.4)=(−1.2,1.6). Is it really perpendicular to u\mathbf{u}u? Its dot product with u\mathbf{u}u is −1.2(0.8)+1.6(0.6)=−0.96+0.96=0-1.2(0.8) + 1.6(0.6) = -0.96 + 0.96 = 0−1.2(0.8)+1.6(0.6)=−0.96+0.96=0. Yes — the shadow plus a perpendicular remainder reassemble x\mathbf{x}x exactly, and the Pythagorean theorem holds: 42+22=20=∥x∥2=22+424^2 + 2^2 = 20 = \|\mathbf{x}\|^2 = 2^2 + 4^242+22=20=∥x∥2=22+42.

⚠️Careful

The formula (x⋅u) u(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{u})\,\mathbf{u}(x⋅u)u requires ∥u∥=1\|\mathbf{u}\| = 1∥u∥=1. For a general nonzero vector v\mathbf{v}v, the projection is x⋅vv⋅v v\frac{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}}\,\mathbf{v}v⋅vx⋅v​v — the extra division normalizes away v\mathbf{v}v‘s length, which otherwise gets counted twice (once in the measurement, once in the rebuild). Forgetting this is the most common projection bug, in homework and in code alike.

Projection is a linear operation — shadows of sums are sums of shadows — so by this chapter’s opening logic it must be a matrix. It is: the outer product P=uu⊤P = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}\TP=uu⊤, an n×nn \times nn×n matrix built from a single direction. For our u=(0.8,0.6)\mathbf{u} = (0.8, 0.6)u=(0.8,0.6):

P=uu⊤=[0.640.480.480.36],P[24]=[0.64(2)+0.48(4)0.48(2)+0.36(4)]=[3.22.4].✓P = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}\T = \begin{bmatrix} 0.64 & 0.48 \\ 0.48 & 0.36 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad P\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.64(2) + 0.48(4) \\ 0.48(2) + 0.36(4) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3.2 \\ 2.4 \end{bmatrix}. \checkmarkP=uu⊤=[0.640.48​0.480.36​],P[24​]=[0.64(2)+0.48(4)0.48(2)+0.36(4)​]=[3.22.4​].✓

Notice two things about PPP. First, P2=PP^2 = PP2=P: a shadow’s shadow is itself, because the shadow already lies on the line. Second, PPP is emphatically not invertible — it flattens the entire plane onto a line, merging every vector with the same u\mathbf{u}u-component into one output. You cannot un-cast a shadow. Which brings us to the natural question: how much does a given matrix flatten?

💡Intuition

Deep learning is projection all the way down. An attention head does not look at a token’s full 8,192-dimensional representation; it projects it onto a small learned subspace (“the part of you that is relevant to my question”) and works with the shadow. A linear probe in interpretability is a learned direction u\mathbf{u}u such that x⋅u\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{u}x⋅u — the shadow’s length — reads out some property (“how much does this activation encode Paris?”). When this book says a model “reads a direction” or “writes to a subspace,” it means exactly the measure-and-rebuild operation you just learned.

Rank: how much of space survives

Feed every vector in Rn\R^nRn through a matrix AAA and collect the outputs. The collection is called the column space of AAA — by the columns view it is precisely the set of all linear combinations of AAA‘s columns — and its dimension is the rank of AAA. Rank answers a blunt question: after AAA does its work, how many dimensions are left?

A 2×22 \times 22×2 matrix whose columns point in different directions has rank 2: outputs fill the whole plane, nothing is lost, and the matrix is invertible. But consider

B=[1224].B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}.B=[12​24​].

Its second column is exactly twice its first. Both basis vectors land on the same line through (1,2)(1, 2)(1,2), so every linear combination of the columns lies on that line too. BBB crushes the entire plane onto a single line: rank 1. The output looks two-dimensional on paper — two coordinates! — but the coordinates are shackled together (the second is always double the first), and one number, the position along the line, describes any output completely. Rank measures true dimensionality, as opposed to the number of coordinates used to write things down; that distinction returns with force in high-dimensional geometry.

Three facts about rank you will use constantly:

  • An m×nm \times nm×n matrix has rank at most min⁡(m,n)\min(m, n)min(m,n): you cannot keep more dimensions than you started with or than you have room to output.
  • Composition can only destroy: the rank of ABABAB is at most the smaller of the ranks of AAA and BBB. Once a pipeline squeezes information through a rank-8 bottleneck, no later matrix can resurrect the other dimensions.
  • A square matrix is invertible exactly when it has full rank — no flattening anywhere. Rank deficiency and information loss are the same thing wearing different clothes.

An outer product uv⊤\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}\Tuv⊤ always has rank 1 (every column is a multiple of u\mathbf{u}u), which is why the projection matrix uu⊤\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}\Tuu⊤ of the last section flattened the plane to a line. Rank-1 matrices are the atoms of matrix-land, and the idea that a big matrix might be well described by a few such atoms — low rank — is about to become the punchline of the chapter. First, though, we need one more concept: the directions a matrix treats specially.

Eigenvectors: the directions that only stretch

Apply a matrix to a typical vector and the output points somewhere new — the matrix rotates it while rescaling it. But most matrices have a few special input directions where nothing so complicated happens: the output points the same way as the input, merely stretched or shrunk (or flipped). These are the eigenvectors, and the stretch factors are their eigenvalues — from German eigen, “own”: the matrix’s own directions.

∑The math

A nonzero vector v\mathbf{v}v is an eigenvector of the square matrix AAA, with eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ (a number), when

Av=λv.A\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}.Av=λv.

Along the direction of v\mathbf{v}v, the whole elaborate transformation collapses to multiplication by one number. To find these directions, rewrite the condition as (A−λI)v=0(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}(A−λI)v=0: the matrix A−λIA - \lambda IA−λI must flatten the nonzero vector v\mathbf{v}v to zero, so it must be rank-deficient — singular. For a 2×22 \times 22×2 matrix, singularity means its determinant (the area-scaling factor from Figure 1) is zero, and setting det⁡(A−λI)=0\det(A - \lambda I) = 0det(A−λI)=0 gives a quadratic equation in λ\lambdaλ: the characteristic polynomial. Its roots are the eigenvalues.

Take A=[3102]A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}A=[30​12​]. Then

det⁡(A−λI)=det⁡[3−λ102−λ]=(3−λ)(2−λ)=0,\det(A - \lambda I) = \det\begin{bmatrix} 3-\lambda & 1 \\ 0 & 2-\lambda \end{bmatrix} = (3-\lambda)(2-\lambda) = 0,det(A−λI)=det[3−λ0​12−λ​]=(3−λ)(2−λ)=0,

so λ1=3\lambda_1 = 3λ1​=3 and λ2=2\lambda_2 = 2λ2​=2.

For λ1=3\lambda_1 = 3λ1​=3: we need (A−3I)v=0(A - 3I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}(A−3I)v=0, i.e. [010−1]v=0\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}[00​1−1​]v=0, which forces v2=0v_2 = 0v2​=0. So this matrix, which shears and stretches most vectors in a hard-to-picture way, is secretly simple: it stretches the (1,0)(1,0)(1,0) direction by 3, stretches the (1,−1)(1,-1)(1,−1) direction by 2, and every other vector’s fate is a blend of those two behaviors. Apply AAA ten times to any vector and the (1,0)(1,0)(1,0)-component grows like 310=59,0493^{10} = 59{,}049310=59,049 while the other grows like 210=1,0242^{10} = 1{,}024210=1,024 — after enough applications, everything aligns with the dominant eigenvector.

That last observation — repeated multiplication is governed by the largest eigenvalue — is why eigenvalues matter to deep learning even though you will rarely compute a characteristic polynomial. A deep network multiplies by many matrices in sequence, and whether signals explode or die across layers is an eigenvalue story (Chapter 12 makes this precise for gradients). The curvature of a loss surface is described by the eigenvalues of a certain matrix of second derivatives (Chapter 5). And Google’s original PageRank was one giant eigenvector computation over the web’s link matrix.

✎Note

Not every real matrix has real eigenvectors. A 90° rotation moves every direction — no vector comes out parallel to itself — so its eigenvalues are complex numbers, a fact we can happily wave at and walk past. And non-symmetric matrices can have eigenvectors that are far from perpendicular to each other, which makes them awkward tools for rectangular, messy, real-world matrices. What we want is a decomposition with all of the insight of eigenvectors and none of the caveats. There is exactly one, and it works for every matrix.

The SVD: every matrix is rotate, stretch, rotate

Every matrix — square or rectangular, invertible or singular, no exceptions — is a sequence of three elementary motions: a rotation, then a pure axis-aligned stretch, then another rotation. This is the singular value decomposition (SVD):

A=UΣV⊤,A = U \Sigma V\T,A=UΣV⊤,

where V⊤V\TV⊤ and UUU are rotations (more precisely orthogonal matrices — rotations possibly combined with reflections, which preserve all lengths and angles) and Σ\SigmaΣ is diagonal: zero everywhere except non-negative entries σ1≥σ2≥⋯≥0\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge 0σ1​≥σ2​≥⋯≥0 on its diagonal, the singular values, each one a stretch factor along one axis.

Reading right to left, as a vector experiences it: V⊤V\TV⊤ rotates the input so that a special set of perpendicular input directions v1,v2,…\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dotsv1​,v2​,… (the right singular vectors) line up with the coordinate axes. Σ\SigmaΣ then stretches axis iii by σi\sigma_iσi​ — the only “distorting” step, and it is as tame as distortion gets. Finally UUU rotates the result to point the stretched axes along a set of perpendicular output directions u1,u2,…\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2, \dotsu1​,u2​,… (the left singular vectors). All of a matrix’s complexity is an illusion of viewpoint: pick the right perpendicular axes for input and output, and every matrix is a plain stretch.

✏️Worked example

For our running matrix A=[2101]A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}A=[20​11​], the singular values come out to σ1≈2.29\sigma_1 \approx 2.29σ1​≈2.29 and σ2≈0.87\sigma_2 \approx 0.87σ2​≈0.87 (they are the square roots of the eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix A⊤AA\T AA⊤A — a fact we state without proof). Sanity checks that need no computation: AAA maps the unit circle to a tilted ellipse (as in the figure above), whose half-axes are exactly σ1\sigma_1σ1​ and σ2\sigma_2σ2​; and the area scale factor σ1σ2≈2.0\sigma_1 \sigma_2 \approx 2.0σ1​σ2​≈2.0 matches the determinant of AAA, the parallelogram area from Figure 1. A singular value of zero would mean the ellipse has collapsed flat — which is precisely a rank-deficient matrix. The number of nonzero singular values is the rank.

🕰History

The decomposition was discovered independently by Eugenio Beltrami (1873) and Camille Jordan (1874) for square matrices, and extended to rectangular matrices by Carl Eckart and Gale Young in 1936, who also proved the approximation theorem below. It became practical in 1965 when Gene Golub and William Kahan published a stable algorithm for computing it — the same Kahan who later designed the floating-point arithmetic your graphics processing unit (GPU) uses.

Low-rank approximation: keeping the biggest stretches

The SVD’s superpower is that it ranks a matrix’s behavior by importance. An equivalent way to write A=UΣV⊤A = U\Sigma V\TA=UΣV⊤ is as a sum of rank-1 atoms:

A  =  σ1u1v1⊤+σ2u2v2⊤+⋯+σrurvr⊤,A \;=\; \sigma_1 \mathbf{u}_1 \mathbf{v}_1\T + \sigma_2 \mathbf{u}_2 \mathbf{v}_2\T + \cdots + \sigma_r \mathbf{u}_r \mathbf{v}_r\T,A=σ1​u1​v1⊤​+σ2​u2​v2⊤​+⋯+σr​ur​vr⊤​,

each term a measure-and-rebuild operation of exactly the projection kind: “measure the input along vi\mathbf{v}_ivi​, scale the reading by σi\sigma_iσi​, lay it along output direction ui\mathbf{u}_iui​.” The terms with big σi\sigma_iσi​ do big things; the terms with small σi\sigma_iσi​ barely matter. So to compress a matrix, keep only the first kkk terms. The Eckart–Young theorem says this truncation is provably optimal: among all rank-kkk matrices, the truncated SVD is the closest possible approximation to AAA. If a matrix’s singular values decay quickly — and empirically, the singular values of many matrices arising in trained networks do — a few atoms capture almost everything, at a fraction of the storage and compute.

Count the savings. A full 4,096×4,0964{,}096 \times 4{,}0964,096×4,096 matrix holds about 16.8 million numbers. A rank-8 version — eight u\mathbf{u}u vectors, eight v\mathbf{v}v vectors, eight scales — needs roughly 4,096×8×2=65,5364{,}096 \times 8 \times 2 = 65{,}5364,096×8×2=65,536: about 0.4% of the original.

⚙️In practice

Low-rank structure is one of the most cashed-in ideas in modern deep learning. LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation, Hu et al., 2021) fine-tunes a frozen model by learning only an update ΔW=BA\Delta W = BAΔW=BA where BBB and AAA are thin matrices of rank 4–64, betting — correctly — that the change needed for a new task is low-rank even when WWW itself is not; Chapter 38 is devoted to it. Multi-head latent attention (MLA), introduced in DeepSeek-V2 and carried into DeepSeek-V3 and R1, compresses attention’s keys and values through a low-rank bottleneck, shrinking the memory-hungry key–value (KV) cache by an order of magnitude (Chapter 28). Quantization-aware factorizations, weight compression for serving, and classic recommender systems all run on the same theorem.

PCA in two paragraphs

Principal component analysis (PCA) is the SVD applied to data. Take a cloud of data points — say, the activation vectors a model produced for a thousand sentences — subtract the mean so the cloud is centered at the origin, and stack the points as the rows of a matrix XXX. The right singular vectors of XXX are the principal components: perpendicular directions ordered by how much the cloud spreads along them, with the singular values measuring the spread. The top component is the single direction of maximum variance; the top two span the best-fitting plane; and truncating, by Eckart–Young, gives the most faithful possible kkk-dimensional flattening of the cloud.

PCA is deep learning’s workhorse microscope. Those 2D scatter plots of embeddings where similar words cluster together are almost always “project onto the top two principal components.” Interpretability work uses PCA to find candidate feature directions in activations before bringing in heavier tools like sparse autoencoders. Its limits matter too: PCA only finds orthogonal directions of large variance, and Chapter 50 turns on the discovery that models pack meaningful features into non-orthogonal, individually small directions that PCA is structurally blind to. A readable deeper treatment is Shlens’s tutorial.

Matrices are what neural networks are made of

Every thread of this chapter now converges on one claim, which the rest of the book will spend its time cashing out: a neural network is mostly a pile of learned matrices, and each one is doing the geometry you can now picture.

The basic layer of a network computes Wx+bW\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}Wx+b — a matrix WWW applied to the incoming representation x\mathbf{x}x, plus an offset vector b\mathbf{b}b called the bias (the one non-linear-algebra touch: it shifts the origin, letting the transformation slide space as well as reshape it). In the rows view, WWW is a bank of learned detectors, each row dotting against x\mathbf{x}x to measure one learned pattern. In the columns view, WWW re-expresses x\mathbf{x}x in a new coordinate system of learned directions — each layer literally changes what the axes mean, turning pixel-coordinates into edge-coordinates into texture-coordinates, or token-coordinates into syntax-coordinates into meaning-coordinates. Stacking layers composes these re-expressions, and the whole reason networks need the nonlinearities of Chapter 8 between layers is this chapter’s composition rule: a stack of pure matrices would collapse, by ABABAB-is-one-matrix, into a single matrix — no deeper than one layer, no matter how tall.

Attention, the engine of transformers, is three projections in a trench coat. Each attention head owns learned matrices WQW_QWQ​, WKW_KWK​, WVW_VWV​ (queries, keys, values — Vaswani et al., 2017) that project every token’s representation into a small subspace: three learned viewpoints on the same vector. WQxW_Q\mathbf{x}WQ​x is the shadow of the token that expresses what it is looking for, WKxW_K\mathbf{x}WK​x the shadow that expresses what it can offer, and the attention scores are dot products between these shadows — rows-view similarity measurements between columns-view projections. When Chapter 23 derives attention, every operation in it will be one you have already met here.

⚙️In practice

Concrete 2026-scale numbers, to calibrate intuition. In Llama 3 70B, each token is a vector of dimension 8,192, and the embedding matrix — vocabulary size 128,256 by 8,192 — is a single matrix of about 1.05 billion parameters whose columns-view job is “turn a token ID into its learned direction.” GPT-3 used width 12,288; frontier models are wider still (exact widths of closed models are unpublished). Each attention head’s WQW_QWQ​ projects 8,192 dimensions down to 128 — a 64-fold flattening, rank at most 128 by this chapter’s min⁡(m,n)\min(m, n)min(m,n) bound. And nearly all of a large model’s compute, at training and inference alike, is matrix multiplication; GPUs are, to first approximation, machines for the ∑kAikBkj\sum_k A_{ik}B_{kj}∑k​Aik​Bkj​ rule executed trillions of times per second (Chapter 33).

One forward pointer to keep in your pocket. Matrices are linear functions, and real networks are nonlinear — yet linear algebra still governs them, because calculus is the art of approximating anything smooth by something linear near a point. The derivative of a many-dimensional function is a matrix (the Jacobian), and Chapter 5 will show that backpropagation is nothing but this chapter’s composition rule applied to those matrices. Learn to see matrices as motions of space and you have learned to see both what networks compute and how they are trained.

Where this leaves us

  • A matrix is a linear function that moves space; its columns are the landing spots of the basis vectors, and that alone determines its action on every vector.
  • You can compute AxA\mathbf{x}Ax two ways — dot products with rows (a bank of detectors) or a weighted combination of columns (a recipe of learned directions) — and flip between the views at will.
  • Matrix multiplication is function composition: inner dimensions must match because outputs must be legal inputs, order matters because it matters for functions, and the identity and inverse are “do nothing” and “undo.”
  • (x⋅u) u(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{u})\,\mathbf{u}(x⋅u)u is a noon shadow: dot with the unit vector u\mathbf{u}u to measure how far x\mathbf{x}x reaches along it, multiply by u\mathbf{u}u to rebuild that measurement as a vector.
  • Rank counts how many dimensions survive a transformation; rank deficiency, singularity, and information loss are the same fact.
  • Eigenvectors are the directions a matrix only stretches; repeated application is dominated by the largest eigenvalue.
  • Every matrix is rotate–stretch–rotate (A=UΣV⊤A = U\Sigma V\TA=UΣV⊤), truncating the small stretches gives the best low-rank approximation, and that single theorem underlies PCA, LoRA, and MLA.
  • Every network layer Wx+bW\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}Wx+b re-expresses its input in learned coordinates, and attention’s WQW_QWQ​, WKW_KWK​, WVW_VWV​ are learned projection viewpoints.
