Architecture Families: BERT to Decoder-Only

The three ways to wire a transformer — encoder-only, encoder-decoder, decoder-only — what each objective trains, and why next-token prediction won.

  2. Encoder-only: BERT and the art of filling in blanks
  3. Encoder-decoder: the original recipe, and where it survives
  4. Decoder-only: why it won
  5. Prefix-LM: the middle ground
  6. The three objectives, side by side
  7. What “decoder-only” hides

The previous chapters assembled a transformer out of attention, feed-forward blocks, normalization, and residual connections. What they quietly postponed is a decision every transformer-based system has to make before training begins: which tokens are allowed to see which other tokens, and what is the model asked to predict? Those two choices — the attention mask and the training objective — split the transformer world into three families. The 2017 original was an encoder-decoder, built for translation. BERT, the model that dominated 2018–2020, kept only the encoder. And nearly every model you interact with today — GPT-5, Claude, Gemini, Llama, DeepSeek — keeps only the decoder.

This is worth a chapter for two reasons. First, the losing families are not dead: encoder-only models still run billions of times a day inside search engines, rerankers, and embedding pipelines, and encoder-decoders still power Whisper and most production translation. If you build with AI, you will touch all three. Second, why decoder-only won is one of the most instructive stories in the field — it is not that bidirectional attention is worse at understanding language (it is arguably better), but that next-token prediction turned out to be denser as a training signal, simpler to scale, cheaper to serve, and, almost by accident, a universal interface for every task.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to look at any transformer’s attention mask and objective and predict what it will be good at, follow the arithmetic of why a causal model extracts roughly 6.7×6.7\times6.7× more supervised predictions per token of data than BERT does, and understand exactly what the rest of this book means when it says — as it will, from here on — “the model” and silently assumes decoder-only.

Three ways to wire the same blocks

All three families use the identical ingredients from Chapter 25: embeddings in, a stack of attention + multi-layer perceptron (MLP) blocks, unembedding out. What differs is the attention mask — the pattern of which positions may attend to which — and, downstream of that, what objective the model can be trained on.

💡Intuition

Think of the mask as a policy about cheating. An encoder is allowed to look at the whole sentence at once, so you can never ask it to predict the next word — it has already read it. Instead you must damage the input (hide words) and ask it to repair the damage. A decoder is forbidden from looking ahead, so you can ask it to predict every next word, honestly, at every position simultaneously during training. Almost everything else in this chapter — what each family is good at, what it costs to train, why one won — falls out of this single difference.

Each mask pairs naturally with an objective:

  • Encoder-only → masked language modeling (MLM): hide some tokens, predict them from both sides.
  • Decoder-only → causal language modeling (CLM): predict token t+1t{+}1t+1 from tokens 1…t1 \dots t1…t, at every position. This is exactly the chain-rule factorization of sequence probability from Chapter 18.
  • Encoder-decoder → sequence-to-sequence objectives: read a full input bidirectionally, generate an output causally, with cross-attention as the bridge.

There is also a fourth pattern, the prefix language model (prefix-LM), which splits a single stack down the middle: bidirectional attention over a prefix, causal generation after it. We will come back to it — it is the mask that quietly runs inside many multimodal models today.

Encoder-only: BERT and the art of filling in blanks

BERT — Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers, Devlin et al., 2018 — took the encoder half of the original transformer and asked a question with a frustrating circularity to it: language modeling means predicting words, but if every token can attend to every other token, the model can see the answer. Predicting the next word is off the table. BERT’s solution was to corrupt the input and train the model to undo the corruption.

∑The math

Given a token sequence x=(x1,…,xT)x = (x_1, \dots, x_T)x=(x1​,…,xT​), choose a random subset M⊂{1,…,T}M \subset \{1, \dots, T\}M⊂{1,…,T} of positions (about 15% of them) and build a corrupted sequence x~\tilde{x}x~ by replacing the chosen tokens. The masked language modeling loss is the average cross-entropy (from Chapter 9) over only the chosen positions:

LMLM  =  −1∣M∣∑i∈Mlog⁡pθ ⁣(xi∣x~),\mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}} \;=\; -\frac{1}{|M|} \sum_{i \in M} \log p_\theta\!\left(x_i \mid \tilde{x}\right),LMLM​=−∣M∣1​i∈M∑​logpθ​(xi​∣x~),

where pθ(xi∣x~)p_\theta(x_i \mid \tilde{x})pθ​(xi​∣x~) is the model’s softmax distribution over the vocabulary at position iii, computed with full bidirectional attention over the corrupted sequence, and θ\thetaθ is the model’s parameters. Note what is not here: no ordering, no chain rule, no probability assigned to the whole sequence — each masked position is predicted independently given the same corrupted context.

The corruption itself uses BERT’s famous 15% / 80-10-10 recipe. Fifteen percent of token positions are selected for prediction. Of those selected positions: 80% are replaced by a special [MASK] token, 10% are replaced by a random vocabulary token, and 10% are left unchanged. The loss is computed at all selected positions, whatever happened to them.

Why the 10/10 complications? Because [MASK] is an artificial token that will never appear when the trained model is used on real text. If the model only ever made predictions at [MASK] positions, it could learn to do serious work only there and treat every real token as trustworthy. The 10% random replacement teaches it that any token might be wrong, so it must read context everywhere; the 10% unchanged slice teaches it that a token might also be right, keeping its per-token representations honest.

✏️Worked example

Run the recipe on a full BERT training sequence of 512 tokens. Selected positions: 0.15×512=76.8≈770.15 \times 512 = 76.8 \approx 770.15×512=76.8≈77. Of those: 0.8×77≈610.8 \times 77 \approx 610.8×77≈61 become [MASK], 0.1×77≈80.1 \times 77 \approx 80.1×77≈8 become a random vocabulary token, and ≈8\approx 8≈8 stay unchanged. The model produces a vocabulary distribution at all 512 positions, but cross-entropy is computed at only those 77 — the other 435 positions supply context but contribute no loss terms of their own. Hold onto that number; it is the hinge of this chapter.

BERT also trained a second task, next sentence prediction (classifying whether two segments were adjacent in the source text), but RoBERTa showed a year later that dropping it and training longer on more data worked better — the MLM objective was doing nearly all the work.

🕰History

BERT did not appear from nowhere. ELMo (early 2018) had shown that pretraining bidirectional long short-term memory (LSTM) language models and reusing their internal states boosted many tasks, and OpenAI’s GPT-1 (mid-2018) had shown the same with a causal transformer. BERT combined the two insights — transformer + bidirectionality — and the effect was seismic: it swept the GLUE (General Language Understanding Evaluation) benchmark suite, and within a year “download a pretrained BERT and fine-tune it” replaced “design a task-specific architecture” as the default methodology of natural language processing (NLP). BERT-base had 110M parameters, BERT-large 340M, trained on roughly 3.3 billion words of books and Wikipedia — adorable numbers by current standards, and a useful reminder that the pretrain-then-adapt paradigm was proven at small scale before anyone scaled it.

What encoders are for — and what they cannot do

A bidirectional encoder produces, for each input token, a vector summarizing that token in the context of the entire input — past and future. That is the ideal shape for tasks where the whole text exists up front and you want to understand rather than continue it:

  • Classification: sentiment, topic, toxicity, intent — read the whole thing, output a label.
  • Token-level labeling: named-entity recognition, extracting spans.
  • Embeddings: pool the token vectors into one vector per text, and you get the semantic-search representations of Chapter 20 — the retrieval half of every retrieval-augmented generation system.
  • Reranking: feed a query and a candidate document together through the encoder (a “cross-encoder”) so attention can compare them word by word, and output a relevance score. This remains one of the highest-value uses of encoders in 2026.

What an encoder cannot do natively is generate.

⚠️Careful

“Why can’t BERT write?” is worth answering precisely, because the reason is structural, not a matter of scale. Generation requires a way to assign probability to a whole sequence and extend it one piece at a time — the chain-rule factorization p(x)=∏tp(xt∣x<t)p(x) = \prod_t p(x_t \mid x_{\lt t})p(x)=∏t​p(xt​∣x<t​) from Chapter 18. MLM never learns that factorization. It learns isolated conditionals “token given both sides,” and if you mask several positions it predicts them independently, which is wrong whenever the answers need to agree with each other (mask two tokens of “New York City” and the independent guesses may be “Mexico” and “Jersey”). You can force generation out of a masked model by unmasking one token at a time and re-running the encoder — but that costs a full forward pass per token with no key-value (KV) cache to reuse, and the results were never competitive. BERT is a reader, not a writer, by construction.

✎Note

One honest wrinkle from the frontier: the idea of generating by iterative unmasking has returned in modern form as text diffusion models — LLaDA, Inception Labs’ Mercury, and Google DeepMind’s experimental Gemini Diffusion (2025) generate whole blocks in parallel through repeated denoising steps, trading the left-to-right bottleneck for parallelism. As of 2026 these are a promising minority approach, not the mainstream — but they show the MLM-style objective was never inherently a dead end for generation, merely uncompetitive in its 2019 form. More in the diffusion chapter.

⚙️In practice

The encoder family’s 2024–2026 descendants are thriving in exactly the niches above. ModernBERT (Answer.AI/LightOn, December 2024) rebuilt BERT with everything this book’s Part IV covers — rotary (RoPE) positional encodings, gated activations, alternating global/local attention, an 8,192-token context, ~2T training tokens — at roughly 150M/400M parameters, and became a standard backbone for retrieval and classification where a 7B decoder would be absurd overkill. Embedding and reranker families (BGE, GTE, E5, Cohere and Voyage rerankers) still lean heavily on encoders, though the largest embedding models are now often adapted decoders (E5-Mistral, NV-Embed, Qwen3-Embedding) — even in BERT’s home territory, decoder-only checkpoints are encroaching because that is where the pretraining compute went. The practical rule in 2026: encoders where latency and cost dominate (running a reranker over 100 candidate documents per query), big decoders where quality dominates.

Encoder-decoder: the original recipe, and where it survives

The 2017 transformer was an encoder-decoder because machine translation demanded it: read a German sentence completely (encoder, bidirectional — word order differs across languages, so you want the whole source before committing to anything), then write English one token at a time (decoder, causal), consulting the source at every step through cross-attention — the decoder supplies queries, the encoder’s output supplies keys and values, exactly as covered in Chapter 22 and Chapter 25. The third panel of the mask figure above is this architecture as one picture: bidirectional square, causal triangle, cross-attention rectangle, and a blocked corner (the source is encoded once and never sees the target).

The definitive study of this family is T5 (“Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer,” Raffel et al., 2019), which contributed two things. First, a unifying framing: every NLP task can be cast as text in → text out. Translation: translate English to German: .... Classification: input the review, output the literal word positive. Summarization, question answering, even regression rendered as digits — one model, one interface, no task-specific heads. This framing looks obvious now precisely because it won; in 2019 it was a genuine reorganization of the field, and today’s “everything is a prompt” world is its direct descendant. Second, a massive controlled ablation of architectures and objectives, which landed on span corruption — an MLM-cousin where contiguous spans (average length 3, 15% of tokens) are each replaced by a single sentinel token, and the decoder learns to emit the missing spans in order. T5 scaled to 11B parameters and, with instruction-tuned descendants like Flan-T5, remained a workhorse for years.

Where does encoder-decoder survive in 2026? Precisely where its shape matches the task — a complete, fixed input that deserves bidirectional reading, and an output in a different “language”:

  • Translation. Meta’s NLLB-200 and most production translation systems remain encoder-decoders; the task is the architecture’s home turf.
  • Speech. OpenAI’s Whisper encodes audio spectrogram frames bidirectionally and decodes text causally — very likely the encoder-decoder you use most often without knowing it.
  • Multimodal bridges. Several vision-language designs graft a modality encoder onto a text decoder through cross-attention (Flamingo is the classic example), which is an encoder-decoder pattern even when the text side is described as decoder-only. The competing (and currently more common) design concatenates vision tokens directly into the decoder’s sequence — that is the prefix-LM pattern below.
✎Note

You will occasionally see claims that particular frontier systems are secretly hybrids — Gemini in particular attracted rumors of encoder-decoder ancestry, plausible-sounding given Google’s T5 and PaLM lineage. Google’s published Gemini reports describe transformer decoders (with mixture-of-experts sparsity in some versions), and the rumors remain unconfirmed. Treat architecture claims about closed models with care throughout this book: labs disclose little, and “it’s a transformer decoder” is often the entirety of the public record.

Decoder-only: why it won

A decoder-only model is the smallest possible answer to “how do I train a transformer on raw text”: one stack, a causal mask, and the objective from Chapter 18 —

LCLM  =  −1T∑t=1Tlog⁡pθ ⁣(xt∣x1,…,xt−1),\mathcal{L}_{\text{CLM}} \;=\; -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta\!\left(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1}\right),LCLM​=−T1​t=1∑T​logpθ​(xt​∣x1​,…,xt−1​),

next-token prediction at every position. GPT-1 committed to this shape in 2018 and was promptly outshone by BERT on understanding benchmarks; GPT-2 (2019) and GPT-3 (2020) kept committing, scaled it, and by 2021 the argument was over. Six reasons, roughly in order of importance:

1. Objective density: every token supervises. The causal mask makes the model’s prediction at position ttt honest — it cannot see xtx_txt​ — so a single forward pass over a TTT-token sequence yields TTT genuine prediction problems and TTT cross-entropy terms, each contributing gradient. MLM yields 0.15 T0.15\,T0.15T. Per token of data ingested, the causal objective extracts about 1/0.15≈6.7×1/0.15 \approx 6.7\times1/0.15≈6.7× more supervised predictions.

✏️Worked example

Concretely: a training run that streams 1 billion tokens through a causal model produces ≈109\approx 10^9≈109 supervised next-token predictions. The same billion tokens through BERT-style MLM produce ≈1.5×108\approx 1.5 \times 10^8≈1.5×108. To match the causal model’s count of prediction problems, the masked model must make ≈6.7\approx 6.7≈6.7 epochs over the data — paying full attention-and-MLP compute on every pass, including at the ~85% of positions that generate no gradient. This is not a decisive argument by itself (each MLM prediction sees richer, two-sided context, and empirically BERT was more sample-efficient than GPT-1 on understanding tasks at 2018 scale) — but when the bottleneck became “how much signal per dollar of compute at trillion-token scale,” density started to matter enormously.

2. Architectural simplicity. One stack, one mask, no cross-attention, no encoder-decoder length bookkeeping, no decisions about how to split parameters between two towers. Every engineering effort in Part V — parallelism schemes, fused kernels, scaling-law sweeps — gets cheaper and less error-prone when there is exactly one block type repeated NNN times. Simplicity compounds: the whole ecosystem’s tooling optimized one shape.

3. Clean scaling. The scaling-law literature — Kaplan et al., Chinchilla — was measured on decoder-only models, giving that family a trustworthy compute → loss → capability map that made $100M training runs plannable. Nobody built an equally confident map for the other families, partly cause, partly effect.

4. In-context learning fell out for free. GPT-3’s headline result was that a large causal model, shown a few examples inside its prompt, performs the task on the next example — no gradient updates. In hindsight the training objective makes this less magical: predicting token t+1t{+}1t+1 on internet text constantly requires inferring “what pattern is this document following?” and continuing it. Few-shot prompting is that same skill, invoked deliberately. An MLM is never trained to continue a pattern, and fine-tuned-BERT had no comparable trick. In-context learning turned one frozen model into a universal tool — arguably the commercial unlock for the entire field.

5. Serving economics. Causal attention means each new token’s representation depends only on earlier tokens, so generation can reuse all previous keys and values — the KV cache of the next chapter — paying one incremental forward pass per token. A bidirectional model would need to recompute everything at every step. And because one decoder handles every task, providers can batch thousands of heterogeneous requests through a single deployed model, which is the economic foundation of the API era.

6. Every task is a continuation. T5 needed a text-to-text framing; a decoder gets it for free. Question answering is continuing “Q: … A:”. Chat is continuing a transcript. Instruction-following, after the post-training of Part V, is continuing a conversation in which one participant happens to be the model. No heads, no task formats, no per-task checkpoints.

✎Note

The careful controlled comparison — Wang et al., 2022, “What Language Model Architecture and Pretraining Objective Work Best for Zero-Shot Generalization?” — is worth reading for its honesty. With pure self-supervised pretraining, the causal decoder was the clear zero-shot winner, but after multitask instruction finetuning, an encoder-decoder trained with masked-style objectives won their benchmarks. The field went decoder-only anyway: scale, in-context learning, serving economics, and ecosystem gravity outweighed a benchmark edge that itself faded as instruction tuning was applied to ever larger decoders. Architecture “winners” are decided by the whole system — training cost, inference cost, tooling, flexibility — not by accuracy tables alone.

Prefix-LM: the middle ground

Between “everything bidirectional” and “everything causal” sits a compromise so natural you may have already invented it while reading: when a task has a given input and a generated output but you want only one stack, let attention be bidirectional within the input (“prefix”) and causal after it, and compute the loss only on the continuation. The mask is the decoder triangle with its top-left corner filled into a square. That is a prefix language model.

The idea threads through the PaLM-era literature: UniLM (Dong et al., 2019) trained one transformer with interchangeable bidirectional/causal/prefix masks; GLM built its pretraining around autoregressive blank-filling; and UL2 (Tay et al., 2022) unified causal, prefix, and span-corruption denoising into a single “mixture-of-denoisers” recipe, arguing the objectives are points on one continuum rather than rival religions.

As a pure-text pretraining objective, prefix-LM never displaced plain CLM — you sacrifice supervision on the prefix half (the figure above: 512 of 1,024 positions) for a modest quality gain on input-grounded tasks, and at scale the density argument tends to win. Where the prefix pattern is alive in 2026 is multimodal models: a vision-language model that consumes image tokens plus a text prompt and generates an answer has exactly the prefix-LM shape, and several open designs (Google’s PaliGemma states this explicitly) apply full bidirectional attention across the image tokens and prompt while decoding the answer causally. The image is a given, not a prediction target — masking it causally would only handicap the encoder-like reading it deserves. When you reach multimodal models, recognize the mask.

What “decoder-only” hides

One warning before the book starts saying “decoder-only” as if it named a single design. The label fixes only the mask and the objective; within it, 2026’s models diverge enormously:

  • Dense vs sparse. A dense model (Llama-3-405B) runs every parameter for every token; a mixture-of-experts (MoE) model (DeepSeek-V3, Mixtral, and — per public reporting — several frontier closed models) routes each token through a small subset of expert MLPs, decoupling parameter count from per-token compute.
  • Attention internals. Full multi-head attention vs grouped-query attention vs DeepSeek’s multi-head latent attention; global attention in every layer vs sliding-window/local layers interleaved — the whole menu of Chapter 28.
  • Sequence mixing beyond attention. Some “decoder-only” models replace part of the attention stack with state-space layers (Mamba-style hybrids like Jamba). Same mask philosophy, different machinery.

These differences — not the encoder/decoder question — are where architecture work happens now. The family war is over; the intra-family engineering is Chapters 28–30.

And so, explicitly, the assumption this book makes from here forward: unless stated otherwise, “the model” means a decoder-only, causally masked transformer trained on next-token prediction. Pretraining (Part V), post-training, inference, and the entire interpretability part study that object. When a claim would change for encoders or encoder-decoders, the text will say so.

Where this leaves us

  • A transformer’s family is determined by its attention mask: full square (encoder-only), lower triangle (decoder-only), or square + triangle + cross-attention rectangle (encoder-decoder) — Figure 1 is the whole taxonomy.
  • Masked language modeling corrupts ~15% of tokens (80% [MASK], 10% random, 10% unchanged) and predicts them bidirectionally; it builds superb readers — classifiers, embedding models, rerankers — that structurally cannot generate, because they never learn a chain-rule factorization of sequence probability.
  • Encoder-decoders pair a bidirectional reader with a causal writer via cross-attention; they survive where the task shape matches — translation, Whisper, some multimodal bridges — and T5’s text-to-text framing is the ancestor of “everything is a prompt.”
  • Decoder-only won on objective density (every token supervises: ~6.7× more predictions per token than MLM), simplicity that let the entire tooling ecosystem optimize one shape, trustworthy scaling laws, emergent in-context learning, KV-cache-friendly serving, and any-task-as-continuation.
  • Prefix-LM — bidirectional prefix, causal continuation — is the quiet compromise living inside many multimodal models.
  • “Decoder-only” is a genus, not a species: dense vs MoE and the attention menu are where 2026’s real architectural diversity lives.

The first consequence of the causal mask is an inference-time gift: everything computed for past tokens can be cached and reused. How that cache works, what it costs at long context, and the attention variants invented to shrink it are next: The KV Cache and Efficient Attention.
