This chapter is the whole machine at minimum depth: every part of a modern frontier model — the math, the network, the transformer, the training run, the serving stack — defined once, with one formula and sometimes one picture each, in about half an hour of reading. Every entry ends with a link to the chapter that tells the full story, so nothing here is the last word on anything; this is the map you hold in your head while you read the rest of the book.

If you would rather build up slowly from zero, skip this chapter entirely. The book proper starts at vectors and assumes nothing. This chapter is for the reader who wants the entire shape of the thing now, and is willing to accept “that’s the idea, details later” fifty times in a row.

There are no derivations here, no history, no worked examples beyond a stray arithmetic check, and no accounting of where the field disagrees with itself. Those are the substance of the real chapters; this is scaffolding.

💡Intuition

The entire field in four sentences. A model is one enormous mathematical function with billions of adjustable numbers in it. You feed it text, it predicts what comes next, and a loss measures how wrong the prediction was. Calculus tells you which direction to nudge every one of those billions of numbers to make the loss slightly smaller, and you repeat that a few million times on a few trillion words. Everything else in this chapter is engineering to make that loop expressive, stable, and fast.

The math, in seven ideas

Deep learning uses a narrow slice of mathematics, and it uses it over and over. Linear algebra supplies the objects — the things a model stores and moves around — and calculus supplies the learning signal, the answer to “which way should this number move?”. Seven definitions cover the working vocabulary of the rest of the book. If you already know them, read them anyway for the notation, since every later formula reuses these symbols.

Vector

A vector is an ordered list of numbers, and throughout this book it is a point — or a direction — in a space with many dimensions. A token, an image patch, a sentence, a whole document all become vectors of a few hundred to a few thousand numbers, and everything a model knows is stored as positions and directions in that space. We write x=(x1,x2,…,xd)x = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_d)x=(x1​,x2​,…,xd​), where ddd is the dimension; in a frontier model ddd is typically between 2,048 and 16,384. The single idea to carry forward: related meanings sit in similar directions.

Vector operations

Three operations do most of the work. Addition, (a+b)i=ai+bi(a+b)_i = a_i + b_i(a+b)i​=ai​+bi​, superimposes two pieces of information in one vector — this is how a model accumulates meaning. Scaling, (αa)i=αai(\alpha a)_i = \alpha a_i(αa)i​=αai​, turns a direction up or down without changing which direction it is. And the dot product measures agreement between two directions:

a⋅b  =  ∑i=1daibi  =  ∥a∥ ∥b∥cos⁡θ,a \cdot b \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{d} a_i b_i \;=\; \|a\|\,\|b\|\cos\theta,a⋅b=i=1∑d​ai​bi​=∥a∥∥b∥cosθ,

where ∥a∥\|a\|∥a∥ is the length of aaa and θ\thetaθ is the angle between the two vectors. A large positive value means “these point the same way”, zero means “unrelated”, negative means “opposed”. Anchor: (1,2,3)⋅(4,0,−1)=4+0−3=1(1,2,3)\cdot(4,0,-1) = 4 + 0 - 3 = 1(1,2,3)⋅(4,0,−1)=4+0−3=1, which is small — a weak match. Every “how relevant is this thing to that thing” question inside a transformer is a dot product.

Matrix

A matrix is a rectangular grid of numbers, WWW with mmm rows and nnn columns. Read it two ways at once: as a stack of vectors (each row or column is one), and as a function that takes an nnn-dimensional vector in and gives an mmm-dimensional vector out. The second reading is the one that matters — a matrix is a linear map, a fixed rule for rotating, stretching, and projecting a space. Nearly all of a model’s learned parameters are matrices.

Matrix operations

Matrix–vector multiplication y=Wxy = Wxy=Wx produces an output whose iii-th entry is the dot product of row iii of WWW with xxx:

yi  =  ∑j=1nWij xj.y_i \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{n} W_{ij}\,x_j .yi​=j=1∑n​Wij​xj​.

So one matrix multiply is “score xxx against mmm different stored directions at once”. Matrix–matrix multiplication C=ABC = ABC=AB is the same operation applied to many vectors in parallel: Cik=∑jAijBjkC_{ik} = \sum_j A_{ij} B_{jk}Cik​=∑j​Aij​Bjk​. That batching is why GPUs matter — a graphics processor is a machine for doing enormous matrix–matrix products, and a transformer is written almost entirely in them.

Derivative

The derivative of a function tells you how fast its output changes when you wiggle its input: f′(x)f'(x)f′(x) is the slope of fff at the point xxx. If f′(x)=−3f'(x) = -3f′(x)=−3, then increasing xxx by a tiny amount ϵ\epsilonϵ decreases fff by about 3ϵ3\epsilon3ϵ. That is the whole reason calculus is in this book — the derivative is the answer to “which way should I move to make this number smaller?”

Gradient

When a function has many inputs, the derivative becomes a gradient: a vector holding the derivative with respect to each input separately.

∇θL  =  (∂L∂θ1,  ∂L∂θ2,  …,  ∂L∂θN),\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} \;=\; \left(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_1},\; \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_2},\; \dots,\; \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_N}\right),∇θ​L=(∂θ1​∂L​,∂θ2​∂L​,…,∂θN​∂L​),

where L\mathcal{L}L is the loss and θ1…θN\theta_1 \dots \theta_Nθ1​…θN​ are the model’s parameters. The gradient points in the direction of steepest increase, so training moves the opposite way. For a frontier model NNN is in the hundreds of billions, and the gradient is a vector with that many entries — one number per weight, saying “raise me” or “lower me”.

Full story: Derivatives and Gradients.

Chain rule

A network is functions stacked inside functions, and the chain rule is how derivatives pass through the stack. For z=g(f(x))z = g(f(x))z=g(f(x)),

dzdx  =  dzdy⋅dydx,y=f(x).\frac{dz}{dx} \;=\; \frac{dz}{dy}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx}, \qquad y = f(x).dxdz​=dydz​⋅dxdy​,y=f(x).

Sensitivities multiply along a path. With many inputs and outputs the same rule holds with matrices of partial derivatives (Jacobians) in place of single numbers. This one identity, applied mechanically layer by layer, is backpropagation.

The trainable machine

Now build something that learns. This section is the anatomy shared by every neural network since the 1980s, transformers included: the unit that computes, the way units are stacked, the number that says how wrong the stack is, the procedure that turns that number into a direction for every weight, and the four pieces of hygiene — initialization, regularization, normalization, residual connections — without which a deep stack does not train at all. None of this is specific to language.

Neuron

A neuron takes a vector in, scores it against one stored direction, adds an offset, and bends the result:

a  =  ϕ(w⋅x+b),a \;=\; \phi(w \cdot x + b),a=ϕ(w⋅x+b),

where www is the neuron’s weight vector, bbb its bias (a learned threshold), and ϕ\phiϕ a non-linear activation function. Without ϕ\phiϕ a neuron is a dot product and nothing more. With it, the neuron becomes a soft detector: quiet when the input does not match www, loud when it does.

Layer

Stack mmm neurons that all look at the same input and you have a layer, which is one matrix multiply plus one activation:

h  =  ϕ(Wx+b),h \;=\; \phi(Wx + b),h=ϕ(Wx+b),

with WWW holding one row per neuron. The layer converts a vector in one space into a vector in another, where the new coordinates are “how strongly did each of these mmm detectors fire”. Every learned transformation in this book is some variation on this line.

Multi-layer perceptron (MLP)

A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) is layers stacked so that each one’s output feeds the next. Two layers with a non-linearity between them can approximate any continuous function given enough width, which is the formal reason this shape is worth building on. Inside a transformer the MLP is a specific, unvarying sandwich — expand the vector to about four times its width, apply the activation, project back down:

MLP(x)  =  W2 ϕ(W1x),\mathrm{MLP}(x) \;=\; W_2\,\phi(W_1 x),MLP(x)=W2​ϕ(W1​x),

with W1W_1W1​ of shape 4d×d4d \times d4d×d and W2W_2W2​ of shape d×4dd \times 4dd×4d. Roughly two-thirds of a dense transformer’s parameters live in these two matrices, repeated once per layer.

Activation functions

The activation is the bend that stops a deep stack from collapsing into a single matrix. Three matter in practice. The rectified linear unit (ReLU), max⁡(0,x)\max(0, x)max(0,x), passes positives and zeroes negatives — fast, and the historical default. The Gaussian error linear unit (GELU), x Φ(x)x\,\Phi(x)xΦ(x) where Φ\PhiΦ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function, is a smoothed ReLU that keeps a small gradient near zero and trains slightly better. SwiGLU, W2(Swish(Wgx)⊙W1x)W_2\big(\mathrm{Swish}(W_g x) \odot W_1 x\big)W2​(Swish(Wg​x)⊙W1​x) with ⊙\odot⊙ meaning elementwise multiplication, adds a learned gate that multiplies one branch by another; it costs a third matrix and is what Llama-, DeepSeek-, and Qwen-family models use.

Full story: Activation Functions.

Loss

The loss is the single number that says how wrong the model was, and the only thing training tries to reduce. Language models use cross-entropy: the model turns its output scores (logits) zzz into probabilities with softmax,

pi=ezi∑jezj,L=−log⁡pcorrect,p_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}, \qquad \mathcal{L} = -\log p_{\text{correct}},pi​=∑j​ezj​ezi​​,L=−logpcorrect​,

so the loss is the negative log of the probability assigned to the token that actually came next. Assign 0.5 to the right token and pay 0.690.690.69; assign 0.010.010.01 and pay 4.64.64.6. Regression tasks use mean squared error instead, 1n∑i(yi−y^i)2\frac1n\sum_i (y_i - \hat y_i)^2n1​∑i​(yi​−y^​i​)2, but almost nothing in this book does.

Full story: Loss Functions and Information Theory.

Backpropagation

Backpropagation is the chain rule run backwards through the network, once, to get the derivative of the loss with respect to every weight. The forward pass computes and stores intermediate values; the backward pass starts from ∂L/∂L=1\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathcal{L} = 1∂L/∂L=1 at the output and walks toward the input, multiplying by each layer’s local derivative on the way. Its cost is about twice the forward pass, no matter how many parameters there are — that efficiency is why networks with hundreds of billions of weights are trainable at all.

Full story: Gradient Descent and Backprop.

Optimizers

Once you have the gradient, an optimizer decides how to move. Plain stochastic gradient descent (SGD) takes a step straight downhill:

θ  ←  θ−η ∇θL,\theta \;\leftarrow\; \theta - \eta\,\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L},θ←θ−η∇θ​L,

where η\etaη is the learning rate, the step size. Momentum accumulates a running average of past gradients so the step keeps rolling in a consistent direction and cancels noise. AdamW — the default for every large language model — keeps running averages of both the gradient and its square, and divides by the square root of the second, so each parameter gets its own effective learning rate; the “W” is decoupled weight decay, pulling weights toward zero separately from the gradient step. Muon, a newer optimizer that orthogonalizes the update for matrix-shaped parameters, has begun appearing in 2025–2026 training runs as a faster alternative.

Full story: Optimizers.

Initialization

Before training, every weight is a random number, and the scale of those random numbers decides whether the network trains at all. Too large and activations blow up through the depth of the stack; too small and they shrink to nothing and gradients vanish. The standard fix sets the variance of each weight from the layer’s fan-in ninn_{\text{in}}nin​ — the number of inputs it reads:

Var⁡(Wij)=2nin,\Var(W_{ij}) = \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}}},Var(Wij​)=nin​2​,

the He initialization used with ReLU-family activations. Deep transformers add a further downscaling of the layers that write into the residual stream, so that adding hundreds of layers does not inflate it.

Full story: Initialization and Stability.

Regularization

Regularization is anything that stops the model from memorizing its training set instead of learning patterns that transfer. Weight decay pulls every weight slightly toward zero on each step, the same pressure toward simpler solutions that adding λ∥θ∥2\lambda\|\theta\|^2λ∥θ∥2 to the objective would give, where λ\lambdaλ sets the strength; it is on in every frontier run, typically at λ=0.1\lambda = 0.1λ=0.1. Dropout randomly zeroes a fraction of activations during training so no single unit can be relied on; it was standard in the 2017 era but is often set to zero in large pretraining runs, because a model that sees each token roughly once has little opportunity to memorize anyway.

Full story: Regularization and Generalization.

Normalization

Normalization rescales a vector to a controlled size before it enters a sublayer, which keeps activations in a range where gradients behave. Modern transformers use RMSNorm, which divides by the root mean square of the vector’s entries and then applies a learned per-dimension gain ggg:

RMSNorm(x)  =  x1d∑i=1dxi2+ϵ⊙g,\mathrm{RMSNorm}(x) \;=\; \frac{x}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d} x_i^2 + \epsilon}} \odot g,RMSNorm(x)=d1​∑i=1d​xi2​+ϵ​x​⊙g,

where ϵ\epsilonϵ is a tiny constant preventing division by zero. Where it sits matters: in current models the norm goes before each sublayer, inside the residual branch (“pre-norm”), which is what makes 100-layer stacks trainable without warmup gymnastics.

Full story: Normalization.

Residual connections and the residual stream

A residual connection makes every sublayer additive rather than replacing:

x  ←  x+F(x),x \;\leftarrow\; x + F(x),x←x+F(x),

where FFF is the attention or MLP block. Gradients then have a clean path straight back to the input — the ”+ xxx” contributes a derivative of exactly 1 — so depth stops being a barrier. The mental model this buys you is the residual stream: one wide vector per token, running the full height of the network, which each layer reads from and writes into a little. Layers communicate by leaving things in that shared channel rather than by handing outputs down a chain.

Full story: Residual Connections.

From text to numbers

A network eats vectors and produces vectors. Text is neither, so two adapters sit at the ends of the model: one that converts characters into vectors on the way in, and one that converts a vector back into a distribution over possible next tokens on the way out. Both are learned, both are ordinary matrices, and the choices made here quietly shape everything downstream — how many tokens a document costs, how well arithmetic and rare languages work, how much of the parameter budget is spent before any computation happens.

Tokenization

A model cannot consume characters or words directly, so text is chopped into tokens from a fixed vocabulary of typically 32,000 to 256,000 entries. Byte pair encoding (BPE) builds that vocabulary by starting from raw bytes and repeatedly merging the most frequent adjacent pair into a new symbol, so common words end up as one token and rare words split into pieces. The result is a lookup table plus a merge list, and every string becomes a sequence of integer IDs. Anchor: English averages roughly 4 characters per token, so a 1,000-word essay is about 1,300 tokens.

Full story: Tokenization.

Embeddings

The embedding matrix EEE is a lookup table with one row per vocabulary entry, each row a learned ddd-dimensional vector. Turning token ID 4,721 into a vector is reading row 4,721 — nothing more. Those rows start random and are trained along with everything else, so by the end of pretraining, directions in this space carry meaning; the classic demonstration is analogies falling out of plain vector arithmetic, king − man + woman landing near queen. This is where “meaning lives in directions” becomes concrete.

Unembedding

At the other end, the model must convert its final ddd-dimensional vector back into a score for every possible next token. That is one more matrix multiply, by an unembedding matrix UUU of shape V×dV \times dV×d where VVV is the vocabulary size, producing VVV logits — one dot product between the model’s output direction and each token’s direction. Softmax then turns those logits into a probability distribution over the vocabulary. Many models tie the weights, reusing the embedding matrix itself as UUU so a token’s input vector and its output direction are the same, which saves parameters and is a reasonable default.

Full story: Embeddings and What Is a Language Model.

The transformer

The pieces so far process one vector at a time. Language is a sequence, and the meaning of any token depends on the others around it, so the architecture needs an operation that moves information between positions. Recurrent networks did that by passing a state along one step at a time, which is slow to train and forgets. The transformer’s answer — attention — lets every position look at every other position at once, in a single matrix multiply, which is both more expressive and far friendlier to a GPU. That trade is the whole reason everything since 2017 happened.

Attention: the retrieval idea

Every token needs information held by other tokens. In “the trophy did not fit in the suitcase because it was too big”, resolving “it” requires looking back. Attention is a differentiable lookup that does exactly that: each position emits a query describing what it needs, each position advertises a key describing what it offers, and each position holds a value it will contribute if selected. Compare every query against every key, turn the resulting scores into weights that sum to 1, and each position takes home a weighted average of the values. No position is chosen outright — everything is a soft blend, which is what makes the operation differentiable and therefore trainable.

Full story: Seq2Seq and Attention.

Scaled dot-product attention

The industrial version is one line:

Attention(Q,K,V)  =  softmax⁡ ⁣(QK⊤dk)V.\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) \;=\; \softmax\!\left(\frac{QK\T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V.Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(dk​​QK⊤​)V.

Every symbol: QQQ is the query matrix, n×dkn \times d_kn×dk​, one row per token, obtained as XWQXW_QXWQ​ from the layer’s input XXX; KKK is the key matrix, also n×dkn \times d_kn×dk​, from XWKXW_KXWK​; VVV is the value matrix, n×dvn \times d_vn×dv​, from XWVXW_VXWV​ (a different VVV from the vocabulary size a moment ago — both uses are standard); and WQ,WK,WVW_Q, W_K, W_VWQ​,WK​,WV​ are learned. The product QK⊤QK\TQK⊤ is an n×nn \times nn×n grid of dot products — entry (i,j)(i,j)(i,j) is how well token iii‘s query matches token jjj‘s key. Dividing by dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​, the square root of the head dimension, keeps those scores from growing with dimension and saturating the softmax. The softmax runs along each row, turning scores into weights summing to 1, and multiplying by VVV replaces each token’s vector with the weighted blend of values it asked for.

Multi-head attention

One attention operation computes one weighted average, so it can follow one relationship at a time. Multi-head attention splits the model dimension into several independent heads — a 4,096-dimensional model might run 32 heads of 128 dimensions each — runs the whole softmax⁡(QK⊤/dk)V\softmax(QK\T/\sqrt{d_k})Vsoftmax(QK⊤/dk​​)V computation separately in each, concatenates the results, and applies one more learned matrix WOW_OWO​. Different heads reliably specialize: some track syntax, some copy repeated patterns, some attend to the immediately previous token. The cost is the same as one big head, since the dimensions were split rather than duplicated.

Full story: Multi-Head Attention.

Positional information

Attention as defined is blind to order — permute the tokens and the weighted averages come out permuted the same way, unchanged in content. Something must tell the model that “dog bites man” differs from “man bites dog”. The current answer is rotary position embeddings (RoPE): before computing scores, each query and key vector is split into 2-D pairs and each pair is rotated by an angle proportional to the token’s position, with a different frequency per pair. Because the dot product between two rotated vectors depends on the difference of their angles, the attention score automatically encodes relative distance, and you can stretch the frequencies at inference time to extend the usable context window.

Full story: Positional Encodings and Long Context.

The transformer block and the decoder-only stack

A transformer block is two sublayers, each wrapped in a norm and a residual add:

x←x+Attn(RMSNorm(x)),x←x+MLP(RMSNorm(x)).x \leftarrow x + \mathrm{Attn}(\mathrm{RMSNorm}(x)), \qquad x \leftarrow x + \mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{RMSNorm}(x)).x←x+Attn(RMSNorm(x)),x←x+MLP(RMSNorm(x)).

Attention moves information between positions; the MLP transforms each position on its own. A model is NNN of these blocks stacked identically — 32 for a small open model, 60 to 120-plus at the frontier — with a final norm and the unembedding on top. Modern language models are decoder-only, meaning a causal mask sets the attention scores for all future positions to −∞-\infty−∞ before the softmax, so position iii can read positions 1…i1 \dots i1…i and no further. That mask is what lets a single forward pass train every position at once while keeping each prediction honest about what it was allowed to see.

Full story: Transformer Architecture and Architecture Families.

KV cache

When generating text, the model appends one token at a time, and recomputing attention over the whole prefix each step would be quadratic waste. The key–value (KV) cache stores the key and value vectors already computed for every earlier position, so each new token computes only its own qqq, kkk, vvv, appends the new kkk and vvv to the cache, and attends over the stored ones. Generation cost per token becomes linear in context length instead of quadratic — and the cache becomes the dominant memory consumer, which is why grouped-query attention (several query heads sharing one key/value head) and multi-head latent attention exist to shrink it.

Full story: The KV Cache and Efficient Attention.

Mixture of experts

A mixture-of-experts (MoE) model replaces each block’s single MLP with many parallel MLPs — the experts — plus a small learned router that picks the top kkk of them (usually 1 to 8 out of 64 to 256) for each token. Only the chosen experts run, so a model can hold a very large number of total parameters while spending a small fraction of them per token: DeepSeek-V3, for instance, carries roughly 671 billion parameters and activates about 37 billion on any given token. The trade is capacity for compute, at the price of a harder training problem (routers collapse onto favorite experts unless balanced) and a much larger memory footprint at serving time.

Full story: Mixture of Experts.

State-space models and hybrids

Attention’s cost grows with the square of sequence length, which is painful at 100,000-plus tokens. State-space models (SSMs) such as Mamba take the opposite approach — carry a fixed-size hidden state forward through the sequence, updating it with a linear recurrence that can still be computed in parallel during training. Cost per token is then constant in context length, but the fixed state cannot hold everything, so recall of specific distant details suffers. The 2025–2026 answer is hybrids: mostly SSM or linear-attention layers with a handful of full attention layers interleaved, which keeps most of the speed and most of the recall.

Full story: State-Space Models.

⚙️In practice

Concrete shapes, so the symbols have sizes attached. A mid-2020s open model in the 70-billion-parameter class runs about 80 blocks with d=8,192d = 8{,}192d=8,192, 64 attention heads of 128 dimensions each sharing 8 key/value heads, an MLP hidden width near 28,00028{,}00028,000, RMSNorm before each sublayer, SwiGLU activations, RoPE positions, and a vocabulary around 128,000. Frontier models in 2026 are mostly mixture-of-experts and mostly undisclosed, but the published ones — DeepSeek-V3, Qwen3, Kimi K2 — sit in the range of 300 billion to 1 trillion total parameters with 3% to 10% of them active per token. These numbers move every few months; the shape does not.

Training, start to finish

Everything above is a part. This section assembles them: one minibatch of text, followed from a hard drive full of scraped web pages to a slightly changed set of weights. Nothing new is introduced here — every name below has a definition above.

One minibatch, end to end

  1. The pile of text. Start with a corpus: web crawl, books, code, papers, filtered and deduplicated down to something in the range of 10 to 30 trillion tokens. Quality filtering at this stage moves final model quality more than most architecture choices do.

  2. Tokenize. Every document is run through the byte pair encoding tokenizer once, offline, turning it into a long stream of integer IDs. The corpus is now a very large array of numbers on disk.

  3. Pack into sequences. The stream is cut into fixed-length chunks — say L=8,192L = 8{,}192L=8,192 tokens each — with document boundaries marked by a special token. Nothing is padded and nothing is wasted; documents are concatenated end to end and sliced.

  4. Form the minibatch. Take BBB of those chunks and stack them into a tensor of shape B×LB \times LB×L — a grid of token IDs, BBB rows deep and LLL columns wide. A frontier run uses a global batch of a few million tokens, so B×LB \times LB×L might be 512×8,192512 \times 8{,}192512×8,192, split across thousands of GPUs.

  5. Embed. Each ID is replaced by its row of the embedding matrix, giving a tensor of shape B×L×dB \times L \times dB×L×d. The residual stream now exists: one ddd-dimensional vector per token, per sequence.

  6. Run the blocks. For each of the NNN blocks, in order: normalize a copy of the stream with RMSNorm; run multi-head attention on it, so every position gathers a weighted blend of what earlier positions hold (the causal mask forbids looking forward, and RoPE has already told the scores about relative distance); add the result back into the stream. Then normalize again, run the MLP — expand to 4d4d4d, apply SwiGLU, project back — and add that in too. The stream comes out the top of the block the same shape it went in, carrying more.

  7. Predict. After the last block, one final RMSNorm, then multiply by the unembedding matrix. Every one of the B×LB \times LB×L positions now has VVV logits: a raw score for every token in the vocabulary as the candidate for what comes next.

  8. Score. Softmax turns each position’s logits into a probability distribution, and cross-entropy compares it against the token that actually came next — which is sitting right there in the batch, one column over. This is the point that makes language model pretraining so efficient: every position is a training example, and all B×LB \times LB×L of them are scored from one forward pass. A batch of 4 million tokens gives 4 million supervised predictions, with no human labeling at any point. Average all those per-position losses and you have one number, the loss for this batch — perhaps 2.92.92.9 early in training, drifting toward 1.71.71.7 much later.

  9. Backpropagate. Run the chain rule backwards through everything that just happened: through the cross-entropy, the unembedding, every block’s MLP and attention and norms and residual adds, down to the embedding rows that were touched. The output is a gradient — one number for every parameter in the model, saying which way that parameter should move to make this batch’s loss smaller. Cost: about two forward passes’ worth of arithmetic.

  10. Update. Hand the gradient to AdamW. It updates its running averages of the gradient and the squared gradient, computes a per-parameter step, applies weight decay, and moves every weight by a small amount — the learning rate at this point in training, typically somewhere around 10−410^{-4}10−4 and shrinking on a schedule. Gradients are usually clipped first so that one strange batch cannot blow the model up.

  11. Repeat. Throw the batch away, take the next one, and do it again a few hundred thousand times. The forward pass is redone from scratch each step because the weights have changed. Over a full run this consumes a few trillion tokens, weeks of wall-clock time on tens of thousands of accelerators, and a training bill that for a frontier model runs into the hundreds of millions of dollars.

Two things are worth noticing about that loop. First, no step in it knows anything about language — the same eleven steps train an image model if you swap what the tokens are. Second, the model is never told how to solve any task; it is only ever told which token came next. Everything a frontier model can do is downstream of that one objective plus scale.

The same machine, running forward

Inference is the same stack with the loop turned inside out. You send a prompt; it is tokenized and embedded, and the whole prompt runs through the NNN blocks in one parallel pass — the prefill — filling the KV cache with the keys and values for every prompt position. The final position’s logits go through softmax and one token is sampled. That token is appended, embedded, and pushed through the blocks alone, attending over the cache rather than recomputing it, producing the next token; its key and value join the cache, which is now one column longer. That loop — embed one token, run the stack, sample, append — repeats until a stop token appears or a limit is hit. No gradients, no loss, no backward pass: the weights are frozen, and the only thing that changes between steps is the cache.

⚠️Careful

A confusion worth killing early: the model does not decide on an answer and then write it out. It produces a distribution over the next token, one token is drawn from it, and the model then reads its own output back as input. There is no plan held anywhere outside the text itself — which is exactly why making a model write out its reasoning before answering changes what it can solve, and why the reasoning training in the next section works at all.

From base model to assistant

The model coming out of pretraining is a base model: a fluent, knowledgeable document continuer with no notion that it is supposed to be helpful. Ask it a question and it might well produce five more questions, because that is what a list of questions on the internet looks like. Post-training turns it into an assistant, in a ladder of stages.

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is the same cross-entropy objective on a much smaller, much more curated dataset: tens of thousands to a few million example conversations, written or vetted by people, formatted with role markers so the model learns where a user’s turn ends and its own begins. Loss is usually computed only on the assistant’s tokens. This one step does most of the work of making a base model behave like a chatbot; what remains is preference, taste, and refusal behavior.

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)

Demonstrations cannot express “this answer is better than that one”, so the next stage learns from comparisons. People are shown two model responses to the same prompt and pick the better one; those pairs train a reward model, a copy of the network with a scalar output head, to predict which response a person would prefer. Then the language model is optimized by reinforcement learning — proximal policy optimization (PPO) in the original recipe — to produce responses the reward model scores highly, with a penalty for drifting too far from the SFT model so it does not collapse into reward-hacking gibberish.

Direct preference optimization (DPO)

Direct preference optimization (DPO) skips the reward model and the RL loop: an algebraic rearrangement shows that the same preference objective can be optimized by a single classification-style loss on the preferred/rejected pairs, raising the likelihood of the chosen response and lowering the rejected one relative to a frozen reference model. It is far simpler and cheaper to run, which is why most open-weight models use it, though full RLHF still edges it out in careful comparisons.

Reasoning training

The newest rung replaces the human judge with a checker. In reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR), the model is given problems whose answers can be checked automatically — math with a known result, code with unit tests — told to think at length before answering, and rewarded when the final answer is correct. Group relative policy optimization (GRPO), the algorithm DeepSeek-R1 made popular, samples a group of, say, 16 attempts per problem, scores them all, and pushes up the probability of the tokens in above-average attempts while pushing down the below-average ones — using the group’s mean score as the baseline, so no separate value network is needed. Given enough of this, models learn to produce long internal chains of reasoning, to check their own work, and to backtrack, none of which was demonstrated to them by a human. This is the single largest source of capability gain in 2025–2026, and it is why models now spend more compute at inference time than they used to.

Full story: Reasoning Models.

Running it

Training happens once; serving happens billions of times, and its economics are completely different. A training step is arithmetic-hungry and processes millions of tokens in parallel. A generation step processes one token and spends most of its time moving weights from memory into the chip. Everything strange about how models are served — enormous batches, aggressive caching, draft models, quantization — follows from that one asymmetry.

The model’s output is a probability distribution, not a token, so something has to choose. Temperature TTT rescales the logits before the softmax,

The pair prevents the long tail of nonsense tokens from ever being drawn while leaving room for variety.

Speculative decoding

A small draft model proposes several tokens ahead; the large model verifies them all in one parallel forward pass and accepts the longest correct prefix, producing multiple tokens per big-model pass with output identical in distribution to sampling from the big model alone.

Why inference is memory-bound

Generating one token requires reading every weight the model will use, and doing comparatively little arithmetic with each — one token times one weight matrix is a skinny matrix-vector product, which leaves the accelerator’s arithmetic units idle waiting on memory. The bottleneck is memory bandwidth, not floating-point throughput, which is why serving systems batch many users’ requests together (the weights get read once and used many times) and why the KV cache, which must also be read every step, dominates capacity planning.

Agents and tools

An agent is a loop around a model, not a different kind of model. The system prompt describes a set of tools — search, run this code, read this file, call this API — each with a name and a schema for its arguments. The model, having been trained on examples of tool use, emits a structured call instead of a normal reply; the surrounding program executes it, catches the result, and appends that result to the conversation as a new message. The model reads the extended conversation and decides what to do next, which may be another tool call or a final answer. That loop, run for tens or hundreds of turns with the whole transcript kept in context, is what “agentic” means, and its failure modes are compounding: a mistake at turn 3 is still in the context at turn 40.

Full story: Agents and Tools and Retrieval-Augmented Generation.

Multimodal basics

Images enter the same machine by becoming tokens. The picture is cut into a grid of fixed-size patches — 14×14 or 16×16 pixels — each patch is flattened and sent through one learned linear projection into a ddd-dimensional vector, and those vectors are inserted into the sequence alongside text embeddings, with their own position information. From the transformer’s point of view nothing has changed: it is still a sequence of vectors being mixed by attention. Audio works the same way, with short overlapping windows of a spectrogram in place of patches; image generation is a different story, usually a diffusion model that starts from noise and denoises toward an image.

Where this leaves us

That is the entire machine. To restate it as a list you can check yourself against:

  • Everything is vectors and matrices. A model is a long chain of matrix multiplies with non-linear bends between them, and every “is this relevant to that” question is a dot product.
  • Everything is trained by one loop: predict, measure the loss, get the gradient by backpropagation, take a small step with AdamW, repeat.
  • The transformer block is two moves — attention mixes information between positions, the MLP transforms each position on its own — each wrapped in a norm and added back into a residual stream, stacked NNN times.
  • The training objective is next-token prediction, and its efficiency comes from the causal mask: every position in every sequence is a labeled example, all scored in one pass, with no human annotation.
  • Post-training is a ladder of progressively weaker supervision: imitate demonstrations, then learn from comparisons, then learn from an automatic checker.
  • Inference is the same forward pass with a KV cache and a sampler bolted on, and it is limited by memory bandwidth rather than arithmetic.
  • Agents and multimodality are not new machinery — they are the same token stream with tool results or image patches spliced into it.

Every one of those bullets is a chapter, and most of them are three. The difference between what you have now and what the rest of the book gives you is the difference between recognizing a part and being able to derive it, predict how it fails, and say what the alternatives were.
