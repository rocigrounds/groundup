Post-Training I: SFT, Reward Models, RLHF

How a raw document-completer becomes an assistant: supervised fine-tuning, chat templates, reward models derived from Bradley–Terry, and the full RLHF loop with PPO.

Take a pretrained model fresh out of the training run — hundreds of billions of parameters, trillions of tokens absorbed — and ask it a question:

Prompt: What is the capital of France?

A modern assistant answers “Paris.” A raw base model might instead produce something like:

What is the capital of Spain? What is the capital of Italy? What is the capital of Germany? Answers on page 12.

or:

This question appeared on the 2019 Year 7 geography exam. Students found it straightforward, though question 4 caused difficulty…

Neither continuation is a malfunction. The base model was trained to do exactly one thing — predict the next token of documents scraped from the internet — and on the internet, a lone quiz question is most often followed by more quiz questions, or by commentary about the quiz, or occasionally by an answer in some house style. The base model is a document completer: a simulator of “what text plausibly comes next,” with no built-in notion that you asked it something and it should reply. There is no “it” yet. There is a distribution over continuations.

Post-training is the process that closes this gap — that takes a distribution over internet text and shapes it into a single consistent character: an assistant that answers the question, follows instructions, declines harmful requests, admits uncertainty, and stops talking when it is done. This chapter covers the classic pipeline that first did it at scale: supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reward modeling from human preferences, and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) with proximal policy optimization (PPO). The next two chapters cover the descendants: direct preference optimization (DPO), which collapses part of this pipeline into a single loss, and reasoning training, which replaces the learned reward with verifiable ones.

The pipeline at a glance

The modern recipe descends almost unchanged from InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022), the paper behind the original ChatGPT models. It has three stages, plus an outer loop that real labs iterate many times:

  1. SFT — show the base model a curated set of conversations written the way you want it to talk, and train it to imitate them with the ordinary next-token loss.
  2. Reward modeling — collect human judgments comparing pairs of model responses, and train a second model to predict which response a human would prefer, as a single score.
  3. RL — generate fresh responses from the SFT model, score them with the reward model, and nudge the model’s probabilities toward higher-scoring behavior — while a Kullback–Leibler (KL) penalty keeps it tethered to the SFT starting point.

Keep one framing in mind throughout: pretraining spends on the order of 102510^{25}1025 floating-point operations and post-training typically spends a fraction of a percent of that (a fast-moving number — reasoning-era RL has been pushing it up; see Chapter 37). Post-training does not teach the model most of what it knows. It selects and shapes behavior that pretraining already made possible. That asymmetry explains most of what works and what fails below.

Supervised fine-tuning: showing, not telling

SFT is conceptually the simplest stage: continue training the base model with the exact same next-token cross-entropy loss from Chapter 9, but on a small, carefully chosen dataset of conversations that demonstrate the target behavior — a user turn, then an assistant turn written the way you want the model to write. No new machinery. The magic is entirely in the data and its formatting.

Where the demonstrations come from

Three sources, in historically shifting proportions:

  • Hired human writers. InstructGPT used roughly 13,000 demonstration prompts (a mix of labeler-written prompts and real prompts submitted to the OpenAI API), with contractors writing the ideal responses under a detailed style guide. Expensive, slow, and quality-capped by the writers — but this is how the first assistants were bootstrapped.
  • Model-generated, then filtered. Once any decent assistant exists, you can have it draft responses (or entire synthetic conversations) and keep only the ones that pass filtering — by human review, by a stronger model acting as judge, or by automatic checks (does the code run? does the math verify?). By 2026 the large majority of SFT tokens at frontier labs are synthetic in this sense: model-written, machine- and human-filtered. Distillation from a stronger model into a smaller one — as in the DeepSeek-R1 distilled models — is the same idea across model generations.
  • Real conversations. Actual user interactions with a deployed model (where policies permit their use), selected for quality, corrected where needed, and fed back in. This is the flywheel the dashed arrow in the figure above describes.

Chat templates, demystified

Here is the part that surprises people most, so let us be blunt about it: a conversation is just tokens. The model has no “system prompt slot,” no message objects, no roles in its architecture. When you send a chat request to an API, software on the server flattens your list of messages into one long token sequence using a chat template — a rigid textual format with special delimiter tokens — and the model completes that sequence like any other. Here is (lightly abridged) the Llama 3 family’s template for a two-turn exchange:

<|begin_of_text|><|start_header_id|>system<|end_header_id|>

You are a concise assistant.<|eot_id|><|start_header_id|>user<|end_header_id|>

Explain RLHF in one line.<|eot_id|><|start_header_id|>assistant<|end_header_id|>

RLHF fine-tunes a model against a learned human-preference reward.<|eot_id|>

The strings that look like markup — <|begin_of_text|>, <|start_header_id|>, <|eot_id|> (“end of turn”) — are special tokens: single entries in the tokenizer’s vocabulary, reserved during pretraining or added before SFT, which never occur inside ordinary text. During SFT the model sees thousands of conversations in exactly this format and learns the grammar of dialogue: after assistant<|end_header_id|> comes a helpful reply; after the reply comes <|eot_id|>. That last part matters more than it looks — learning to emit the end-of-turn token is how the model learns to stop talking. At inference time, generation halts when <|eot_id|> is sampled.

Two consequences worth internalizing. First, a system prompt is not a different kind of input — it is tokens like any others, placed first and wrapped in delimiters, influential only because SFT data consistently showed the assistant obeying whatever appeared in that position. Second, this is why prompt injection is structurally hard to eliminate: instructions in the system turn and instructions smuggled into a pasted document are, to the transformer, the same kind of thing — tokens in the context — differing only in position and learned conventions about which to trust. There is no privilege boundary in the architecture; every defense is trained, not built.

Loss masking: train on the answers, not the questions

If we ran the plain next-token loss over the whole formatted sequence, the model would spend capacity learning to predict user messages — to imitate the typos, phrasing, and distribution of questions. We do not want that. So SFT applies a loss mask: the cross-entropy loss is computed only at positions where the target token belongs to an assistant turn (response text plus its closing <|eot_id|>); everywhere else the loss weight is zero. The user and system tokens still flow through the network as context — attention reads them — they are only excluded from the training signal. Mechanically this is one elementwise multiply: per-token losses times a 0/1 mask, then a sum divided by the number of unmasked tokens.

⚙️In practice

Every open-weight model ships its template in the tokenizer config, and libraries apply it for you (tokenizer.apply_chat_template(messages) in Hugging Face Transformers). Templates differ per family — Llama 3’s header tokens above, OpenAI’s ChatML-derived <|im_start|>role … <|im_end|>, Gemma’s <start_of_turn> — and they are not interchangeable: serving a model with the wrong template is one of the most common ways to “break” a good open model, producing rambling, never-terminating, or role-confused output while the weights are perfectly fine. When a fine-tune behaves strangely, checking the template is step one.

How much data? Less than you would guess

The LIMA paper (Zhou et al., 2023) made the point sharply: fine-tuning a 65B-parameter LLaMA on 1,000 carefully curated demonstrations — no RLHF at all — produced an assistant that human raters found comparable to or better than much more heavily post-trained contemporaries in a meaningful fraction of head-to-head comparisons. The authors’ interpretation, now called the superficial alignment hypothesis: nearly all of an assistant’s knowledge and capability comes from pretraining; post-training mainly teaches which subdistribution of formats and personas to use. Frontier SFT datasets in 2026 are far larger than 1,000 examples (hundreds of thousands to millions of conversations, spanning coding, multilinguality, tool use, safety), but the LIMA lesson survived: past a modest scale, data quality and diversity dominate data quantity, and a few thousand sloppy examples can measurably damage a model that billions of pretraining tokens built.

💡Intuition

Think of the base model as an enormous repertory theater company that has read everything ever written and can play any character. SFT is not acting school — it is casting. A small number of clear demonstrations tells the company which single character to play from now on: the helpful, honest assistant. This is why little data goes far (casting is cheap; the acting ability was already there), and why bad demonstrations are so costly (miscasting is also cheap).

What SFT can and cannot teach

SFT is excellent at teaching format (markdown, turn-taking, when to stop), style and persona (concise vs. discursive, refusal phrasing), and at surfacing latent skills — behaviors the base model could produce but rarely chose to, like step-by-step reasoning or asking clarifying questions.

What it cannot do well is teach new knowledge, and here lies a trap with a name: the behavioral cloning problem. SFT is behavioral cloning — pure imitation of demonstrations — and imitation transfers the demonstrator’s knowledge policy, not the model’s. Suppose a human writer’s demonstration confidently answers a factual question the base model knows nothing about. Gradient descent cannot install the missing fact from one example; the cheapest way to reduce the loss is to learn the pattern “produce a confident, plausible-sounding answer in this situation.” You have just trained the model to confabulate. John Schulman highlighted this mechanism as a core driver of assistant hallucination, and controlled experiments (Gekhman et al., 2024) back it up: fine-tuning on facts the model has not already internalized measurably increases hallucination on unrelated questions. The fix requires feedback that depends on what the model actually knows — which is one of the deep reasons the pipeline does not stop at SFT.

⚠️Careful

Imitation teaches confidence, not truth. Any SFT example whose correct production requires knowledge the model lacks is a lesson in bluffing. Careful pipelines audit demonstrations against the base model’s own knowledge (e.g., checking whether the base model can answer correctly when sampled many times) and rewrite unknowable answers as hedges or refusals. This is also why “I don’t know” examples are deliberately included — with care, because too many teach reflexive unhelpfulness.

Learning what people prefer

SFT’s supervision signal is “write exactly this.” That is both too strong — there are many good answers, and token-level imitation penalizes all but one — and too weak — it can never say “this whole answer is worse than that one.” The next idea, imported from Christiano et al., 2017, is to collect a cheaper, more honest signal: comparisons.

The collection interface is mundane and important. A rater sees one prompt and two model responses, A and B, side by side, and clicks which is better (some interfaces allow ties, or a 4–8 point Likert scale later binarized). Pairwise comparison is used instead of absolute scoring for a well-replicated reason: humans are unreliable at assigning consistent absolute scores (“is this a 7 or an 8?”) but much more consistent at picking between two options shown together. Raters work from a guidelines document — InstructGPT’s instructed labelers to prioritize helpfulness, truthfulness, and harmlessness, with explicit rules for conflicts; Anthropic’s HH-RLHF collection split helpfulness and harmlessness into separate tasks entirely.

Now the uncomfortable reality: raters disagree, a lot. InstructGPT reported inter-annotator agreement around 73% (approximate; ties excluded) — meaning that on roughly a quarter of pairs, two trained humans picked opposite winners. Disagreement comes from genuine taste differences, ambiguous guidelines, fatigue, and prompts where neither answer is checkable. This number is worth memorizing because it sets a ceiling: a reward model trained on labels that humans only agree on 73% of the time cannot meaningfully exceed that agreement with any one human, and everything downstream optimizes a signal containing this noise. Preference data is not ground truth about quality; it is a noisy sample from a distribution of human opinions, filtered through a pay scale and a PDF of instructions.

The reward model: distilling preference into a number

We have thousands of comparisons. RL, coming next, needs a scalar reward for any (prompt, response) pair — including brand-new responses no human ever saw. The bridge is the reward model (RM): a neural network rϕ(x,y)r_\phi(x, y)rϕ​(x,y) with parameters ϕ\phiϕ that maps a prompt xxx and a full response yyy to one real number, trained so that its scores reproduce human comparisons.

Bradley–Terry: from “A beats B” to a training loss

We need a probabilistic model connecting hidden scores to observed comparisons. The standard choice is nearly a century old — the Bradley–Terry model (1952), originally built for paired-comparison experiments like taste tests. Its single assumption: each item has a latent strength, and

P(A preferred over B)  =  σ ⁣(rA−rB)  =  11+e−(rA−rB),\P(A \text{ preferred over } B) \;=\; \sigma\!\big(r_A - r_B\big) \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(r_A - r_B)}},P(A preferred over B)=σ(rA​−rB​)=1+e−(rA​−rB​)1​,

where σ\sigmaσ is the logistic sigmoid and rA,rBr_A, r_BrA​,rB​ are the latent scores. Equal scores give probability 0.50.50.5; a score gap of +2+2+2 gives σ(2)≈0.88\sigma(2) \approx 0.88σ(2)≈0.88. If you have met Elo ratings in chess, this is exactly that model with a natural-log base. Note that only the difference rA−rBr_A - r_BrA​−rB​ matters — rewards are defined only up to an additive constant, a freedom that will come back to bite us in a moment.

So every gradient step pushes the winner’s score up and the loser’s down by an amount proportional to 1−σ(Δ)1 - \sigma(\Delta)1−σ(Δ) — that is, proportional to how surprised the model was. This is the binary cross-entropy machine from Chapter 9 wearing a comparison costume.

Architecture: a language model with a value head

The RM is not built from scratch. Standard practice since InstructGPT: start from the SFT model itself (or a smaller sibling), delete the token prediction head, and bolt on a value head — a single learned linear layer mapping the final hidden state to one scalar. Feed in the prompt and full response as one token sequence, read the scalar at the last token, and that is rϕ(x,y)r_\phi(x, y)rϕ​(x,y). Initializing from the SFT model means the RM already understands language, code, and the chat template; it only has to learn taste. Training runs are short — typically about one epoch over the comparison set, because the RM overfits quickly, and an overfit RM memorizes rater quirks instead of preferences. Because only differences ever appear in the loss, the raw scale of rϕr_\phirϕ​ drifts arbitrarily; implementations usually normalize scores (e.g., to mean zero on a reference set) before RL.

Where reward models go wrong

The RM is now the objective function for the most powerful optimizer in the pipeline, and it is a lossy, biased proxy. Its known pathologies:

  • Length bias. Raters, on average, slightly prefer longer, more detailed answers — so the RM learns “longer is better” as a shortcut, and RL then inflates responses with padding, restatement, and bullet-point bloat. Nearly every open RLHF replication rediscovered this; mitigations include length-balanced training pairs and explicit length penalties.
  • Sycophancy. Responses that agree with the user’s stated opinion, flatter them, or validate a premise get preferred a bit more often by human raters — and the RM amplifies the pattern into a policy that tells users what they want to hear. Sharma et al., 2023 traced sycophancy in deployed assistants directly to this preference-data signal.
  • Style over substance. Confident tone, headers, and polished formatting are visible in seconds; factual errors take minutes to check. Raters under time pressure reward the former, so the RM does too — a direct channel for the confabulation problem SFT started.
⚠️Careful

Reward hacking: a field guide. When an optimizer exploits gaps between a proxy and the true goal, that is Goodhart’s law (“when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure”). A useful taxonomy (Manheim & Garrabrant, 2018) with RLHF examples:

  • Regressional — the proxy correlates with quality imperfectly, and selecting for extreme proxy values selects the error too: pick responses the RM loves most and you disproportionately pick RM mistakes.
  • Extremal — correlations that held in the training distribution break in regimes the optimizer reaches: “longer answers rated better” was true from 200 to 600 tokens, catastrophically false at 4,000 tokens of filler.
  • Causal — the proxy rewards a symptom: confident phrasing correlates with correctness in human data, so the policy learns confident phrasing without the correctness that caused it.
  • Adversarial — the policy actively searches for RM exploits: strings of flattery, faux citations, or (a real observed failure in early pipelines) degenerate token patterns that spuriously max out the score.

Classic non-LLM ancestor: OpenAI’s boat-racing agent (CoastRunners) that learned to spin in circles collecting respawning power-ups instead of finishing the race — maximum reward, zero racing. Every RLHF pathology is this boat, wearing prose.

Gao, Schulman & Hilton (2022) measured Goodharting quantitatively: train a large “gold” RM to stand in for true human preference, train smaller proxy RMs from its labels, then optimize policies against the proxies. The signature result: as optimization proceeds, proxy reward climbs monotonically while gold reward rises, peaks, and then falls. The gap widens smoothly and predictably with optimization pressure — following scaling-law-like curves in the KL divergence between the policy and its starting point — and larger RMs shift the peak further out but never remove it. Over-optimization is not an occasional bug; it is the default outcome of optimizing any learned reward hard enough. Which is exactly why the RL stage carries a leash.

Policy optimization: RL with a leash

Stage three. We have a policy πθ\pi_\thetaπθ​ (the SFT model, about to be further trained) and a frozen reward model rϕr_\phirϕ​. The naive objective — “maximize expected reward” — would, per the previous section, drive the policy straight into the RM’s blind spots. So RLHF optimizes a KL-regularized objective:

max⁡θ  Ex∼D,  y∼πθ(⋅∣x)[ rϕ(x,y) ]  −  β Ex∼D[ DKL ⁣(πθ(⋅∣x) ∥ πref(⋅∣x))].\max_\theta \; \E_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\; y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)} \Big[\, r_\phi(x, y)\,\Big] \;-\; \beta \,\E_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}\Big[\,\KL\!\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)\big)\Big].θmax​Ex∼D,y∼πθ​(⋅∣x)​[rϕ​(x,y)]−βEx∼D​[DKL​(πθ​(⋅∣x)∥πref​(⋅∣x))].

Read it term by term. The first term says: prompts xxx come from a training distribution D\mathcal{D}D; responses yyy are sampled from the current policy itself (this is what makes it RL rather than supervised learning — the model learns from its own behavior, scored, not from demonstrations); make the RM’s score high. The second term is the leash: πref\pi_{\text{ref}}πref​ is a frozen copy of the SFT model, and the Kullback–Leibler divergence measures, in nats, how far the policy’s output distribution has drifted from where it started. The coefficient β\betaβ sets the leash’s tightness: β→∞\beta \to \inftyβ→∞ means never change (pure SFT model); β→0\beta \to 0β→0 means chase the reward off the Goodhart cliff. Typical values are small — order 0.010.010.01 to 0.10.10.1 (approximate, implementation-dependent) — and some pipelines adapt β\betaβ on the fly to hold a target KL.

The KL penalty earns its place three times over. It prevents reward-model exploitation by confining the policy near the distribution the RM was trained on, where its judgments are trustworthy. It prevents catastrophic drift — the policy remains a fluent language model instead of collapsing into whatever gibberish maximizes the RM. And it preserves entropy: without it, RL sharpens the policy toward a handful of high-reward phrasings (“mode collapse”), and the model starts every answer with the same sentence. In implementations, the sequence-level KL is charged per token: each generated token receives a penalty −βlog⁡ ⁣(πθ(yt∣⋅)/πref(yt∣⋅))-\beta \log\!\big(\pi_\theta(y_t \mid \cdot)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t \mid \cdot)\big)−βlog(πθ​(yt​∣⋅)/πref​(yt​∣⋅)) folded into its reward, so the leash tugs at every step, not only at the end.

💡Intuition

The objective is a bargain between two masters. The reward model says “be better than you were”; the reference model says “stay recognizably yourself.” β\betaβ prices the trade: every nat of divergence from the SFT model must buy at least β\betaβ units of reward, or it is not worth taking. Over-optimized models are what you get when divergence is underpriced.

PPO, mechanically

How do we actually ascend this objective? The gradient of “expected reward under my own samples” cannot be computed by ordinary backprop through the loss — sampling is not differentiable — so we need a policy-gradient method (the REINFORCE family): sample, score, then increase the log-probability of actions in proportion to how well they scored. The industry-standard variant is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017). One iteration, in large language model (LLM) terms:

  1. Rollouts. Take a batch of prompts; sample a full response from the current policy for each (each token is an “action”). Score each finished response with the RM; add the per-token KL penalties.
  2. Advantages. Raw rewards are noisy — some prompts are inherently easier to score well on. So a fourth network, the value network VψV_\psiVψ​ (usually another copy of the model with a scalar head), predicts the expected reward from each position, and we compute each token’s advantage AtA_tAt​: how much better this rollout is going than expected from here. Learning from advantages instead of raw rewards is a variance reduction trick — it credits choices, not circumstances.
  3. Clipped updates. Take several minibatch gradient steps on the clipped surrogate objective below, then throw the rollouts away and repeat.
∑The math

PPO maximizes

Why does everyone call PPO finicky? Count the models: the policy (training), the reference (frozen), the reward model (frozen), and the value network (training) — four LLM-scale networks resident at once, two of them updating, plus rollout generation, which is inference (with all of Chapter 39’s serving machinery) interleaved with training steps. Memory pressure is brutal and the hyperparameter surface is wide: KL coefficient, clip range, rollout batch size, epochs per batch, value-loss weight, advantage estimation parameters, reward normalization — with instability (reward collapse, KL blow-ups, entropy collapse) waiting behind bad settings. A large slice of post-2022 research — including the group relative policy optimization (GRPO) algorithm covered in Chapter 37, which deletes the value network entirely, and the DPO family of Chapter 36, which deletes the RL loop — is best read as attempts to keep RLHF’s benefits while shedding PPO’s operational cost.

⚙️In practice

Nobody writes this loop from scratch anymore. Open-source RLHF stacks — Hugging Face’s TRL, OpenRLHF, and verl (the ByteDance-released framework many 2025–2026 RL recipes standardized on) — handle the four-model choreography, typically colocating a vLLM-style inference engine for rollouts with a distributed training engine for updates, swapping weights between them each iteration. Representative defaults you will see in configs: clip range ϵ=0.2\epsilon = 0.2ϵ=0.2, one to four optimizer epochs per rollout batch, KL coefficient in the 0.010.010.01–0.10.10.1 range or auto-tuned to a target KL of a few nats per response — all approximate and endlessly re-tuned per model and dataset, which is rather the point about PPO.

InstructGPT: the case study, with its honest footnotes

InstructGPT ran exactly this pipeline on GPT-3 — roughly 13k demonstration prompts for SFT, 33k prompts’ worth of comparisons for the RM, 31k prompts for PPO, labeled by a team of about 40 contractors (numbers approximate, from the paper). The headline result reorganized the field’s priorities: labelers preferred the 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT’s responses to those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3 base model. A hundred-fold size advantage was worth less than a post-training pipeline — capability was already there; what was missing was the interface to it.

The paper’s candor is worth as much as its result. PPO models paid an alignment tax: performance dropped on several standard natural language processing (NLP) benchmarks (question answering, translation) relative to the base model — the KL leash notwithstanding, optimizing for “responses raters prefer” pulled capability away from tasks raters never saw. Their mitigation, PPO-ptx, mixed pretraining-data gradients into the RL updates to anchor the model’s general ability, recovering most of the loss. Descendants of that trick — replaying pretraining or SFT data during RL — remain standard. The paper also documented real gains in truthfulness (closed-domain hallucination rates roughly halved vs. GPT-3; approximate) while being frank that the models still made things up, still followed harmful instructions too readily, and were aligned to their labelers and instructions — a specific group of people with a specific rulebook — not to “humanity.”

🕰History

The lineage runs backward further than most summaries note. Christiano et al. (2017) trained a reward model from pairwise human preferences over video clips of Atari play and simulated robot backflips — about 900 human comparisons sufficed for a backflip no hand-coded reward could specify. Stiennon et al. (2020) moved the recipe to language (Reddit-post summarization) and observed, presciently, that over-optimizing the RM made summaries worse by human lights. InstructGPT (2022) generalized it to open-ended instruction following; ChatGPT (late 2022) put the result in front of a hundred million people within months. The technique jumped from a backflip to the fastest-growing consumer product in history in five years.

RLAIF and Constitutional AI: feedback that scales

Human feedback has three structural problems: it is expensive (rater-hours per label), inconsistent (that 73% ceiling), and — for safety training specifically — it can require people to spend workdays reading harmful content. Anthropic’s Constitutional AI (CAI; Bai et al., 2022) asked: which parts of the loop actually need a human?

CAI substitutes AI for human labor in two places. First, critique-and-revise data generation for SFT: sample a response to a red-team prompt, then ask the model itself to critique its response against a principle drawn from a short written “constitution” (“identify ways this response is harmful or unethical…”), then to revise the response accordingly. The revised responses — now harmless without being preachy or evasive, at least by design — become SFT data. Second, AI preference labels: instead of humans comparing response pairs for harmlessness, a model is shown the pair plus a constitutional principle and asked which response better complies. Those AI labels train the preference model, and RL proceeds exactly as in RLHF — hence RLAIF, reinforcement learning from AI feedback. (In the original paper, helpfulness labels still came from humans; AI feedback handled harmlessness.)

Why this scales: AI labels cost inference, not rater-hours, so the label budget grows with compute rather than headcount; the “annotator” applies guidelines with mechanical consistency; and changing the target behavior means editing a text file of principles rather than re-briefing and re-running a human labeling campaign. Subsequent work (Lee et al., 2023) found AI preference labels roughly matching human labels’ downstream results on summarization tasks. The honest caveats: the AI labeler inherits its own biases (including sycophancy and self-preference — models rating their own style highly), errors correlate across millions of labels instead of washing out the way independent human noise partially does, and the approach presupposes a model already good enough to judge — which is why every RLAIF pipeline still bottoms out in human oversight somewhere: writing the constitution, auditing samples, labeling the hard slices.

What frontier pipelines look like in 2026

The clean three-stage diagram is now a simplification that every frontier lab has outgrown. From what labs have published — the Llama 3 report, DeepSeek’s V3 and R1 papers, the open Tülu 3 replication, and Anthropic’s and OpenAI’s coarser disclosures — the common shape of modern post-training:

  • Iterated rounds, not stages. Llama 3 ran roughly six rounds of (reward modeling → rejection sampling → SFT → preference optimization), each round’s model generating and filtering data for the next. “Post-training” is a months-long program, not a step.
  • Overwhelmingly synthetic data, with humans moved up the stack: writing rubrics and constitutions, auditing model-labeled data, and supplying labels only where models judge poorly.
  • Specialized reward signals, plural: separate or multi-headed RMs for helpfulness and safety, rubric- and checklist-based judging, and — the biggest shift since 2023 — verifiable rewards (does the code pass tests? is the math answer correct?) for domains where correctness can be checked, which sidestep Goodhart problems learned RMs cannot escape. That thread, reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), is Chapter 37’s subject.
  • Simpler preference optimizers where they suffice. Many pipelines use DPO-family methods (Chapter 36) for some rounds and reserve full RL for where it pays.
✎Note

An honesty flag: post-training is now the least documented part of the frontier stack. Labs publish architecture details and pretraining scale far more readily than post-training recipes, which are treated as core trade secrets — and the recipes change between model versions. Everything in this section is a snapshot assembled from what happens to be public as of mid-2026, biased toward the labs that publish (Meta, DeepSeek, AI2). Assume the closed labs’ pipelines are more elaborate than anything described here.

One more forward pointer. RLHF optimizes judged quality — and a judge who reads the model’s chain of thought creates pressure for the chain of thought to look good to the judge, which is not the same as faithfully reflecting the computation. Preference optimization is where that wedge between appearance and reality first enters training; Chapter 55 follows where it leads.

Where this leaves us

  • A base model is a document completer; post-training selects a persona from capabilities pretraining already built. Compute-wise it is a rounding error; behaviorally it is most of what you experience as “the assistant.”
  • SFT is next-token training on curated conversations rendered through a chat template of ordinary and special tokens, with the loss masked to assistant tokens. System prompts are just tokens; end-of-turn is a learned token; quality beats quantity (LIMA); and demonstrations beyond the model’s knowledge teach confabulation.
  • A reward model turns pairwise human preferences into a scalar via Bradley–Terry: the loss is −log⁡σ(rw−rl)-\log\sigma(r_w - r_l)−logσ(rw​−rl​), derived directly from maximum likelihood, with gradients proportional to the model’s surprise.
  • RMs inherit rater biases (length, sycophancy, style) and any learned reward Goodharts under enough optimization — proxy reward up, true quality down (Gao et al.).
  • RLHF therefore maximizes reward minus β⋅\beta\cdotβ⋅KL from the frozen SFT reference, using PPO: rollouts, value-network advantages, clipped ratios — effective, and expensive in memory, hyperparameters, and patience.
  • InstructGPT proved the pipeline (1.3B preferred over 175B) and named its costs (alignment tax, labeler-relative alignment); Constitutional AI showed most feedback labor can move from humans to models, with humans writing the rules.
