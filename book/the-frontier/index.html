The Frontier Landscape (2023–2026)

A field guide to the frontier as of mid-2026 — the major model families and their genealogies, the seven durable trends underneath the release noise, and how to read lab announcements without being fooled.

Every chapter before this one taught you machinery: attention, mixture-of-experts, scaling laws, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), reasoning training, serving. This chapter is different. It is a map — of who builds frontier models, how each family got to where it is, and what actually changed between 2023 and 2026 underneath the release-day noise.

A map of a fast-moving field has an obvious problem: it ages. A version of this chapter written in early 2025 would already read like a museum exhibit — no GPT-5, no Claude 4 (let alone 5), no Kimi K2, no trillion-parameter open weights. So this chapter is deliberately built in two layers. The first layer is durable: how to read frontier announcements critically, and seven structural trends — the shift to mixture-of-experts (MoE), thinking modes, the closing open-weight gap, the agentic turn, multimodality-by-default, collapsing prices, and formalized safety frameworks — that have held for years and will likely outlive any particular model name. The second layer is dated: a genealogy of the major families and a scoreboard table, both explicitly stamped as a snapshot as of mid-2026. When you read this later than that, treat the genealogies as history (history doesn’t rot) and check the updates page for whatever has shipped since.

One more framing note before we start. Nothing in this chapter is new machinery. Every model below is a decoder-only transformer variant you could now describe layer by layer (Chapter 25), usually sparse (Chapter 29), trained the way Chapters 31–37 describe, and served the way Chapter 39 describes. The frontier is not a different kind of object. It is the same object with more zeros, better data, and post-training that took years to industrialize.

How to read the frontier

Before the map, the map-reading skills — because most of what you will read about frontier models, for the rest of your life, will be some mixture of disclosure, inference, and folklore, and telling them apart is a learnable skill.

Disclosed versus inferred. Until 2023, frontier papers told you almost everything: GPT-3’s paper gave parameter counts, layer counts, data mixture, compute. The GPT-4 Technical Report (March 2023) broke that norm on purpose, stating that it would report “no further details about the architecture (including model size), hardware, training compute, dataset construction, or training method,” citing “the competitive landscape and the safety implications.” That sentence marks a watershed: from then on, closed-lab information comes in three grades. Disclosed: stated in a technical report or system card (Claude’s context window; Gemini 1.5’s mixture-of-experts architecture). Inferred: estimated from behavior, pricing, latency, or hardware supply chains (most closed parameter counts; most closed training-compute figures — organizations like Epoch AI publish careful inference-based estimates and label them as such). Folklore: numbers from anonymous leaks that got repeated until they felt official (the “GPT-4 is 1.8 trillion parameters” figure is the canonical example — plausible, widely repeated, never confirmed). This book cites disclosed numbers as facts, inferred numbers as estimates, and folklore as folklore.

Model cards and system cards. The primary sources worth your time are technical reports (architecture and training, when disclosed), model cards (a structured summary of capabilities, limits, and evaluation results — a genre proposed by Mitchell et al., 2018), and system cards (a newer genre covering the deployed system: safety evaluations, red-teaming results, mitigations, known failure modes). Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google DeepMind all now ship system cards with flagship releases, and they are consistently more informative than any press coverage of the same release. When you want to know what a model actually is, read the card, not the headline.

Primary sources over leaderboard aggregators. Third-party leaderboards are useful but structurally gameable, for reasons Chapter 46 dissects in detail: benchmark contamination, prompt sensitivity, and — for preference-vote arenas — the ability to submit specially tuned variants. A cautionary tale from April 2025: Meta submitted an “experimental” chat-optimized variant of Llama 4 Maverick to LM Arena that outranked the model actually released, prompting the leaderboard to change its policies. The lesson is not that leaderboards lie; it is that a leaderboard rank is one noisy measurement, while a system card plus your own task-specific evaluation is evidence.

⚠️Careful

Version-number folklore. Model version numbers are marketing labels, not measurements. They do not map cleanly onto training runs (a “.1” may be a new pretrain, a post-training refresh, or a router change), they do not imply size (“GPT-5 must be 10× GPT-4” has no basis — public reporting suggests the opposite emphasis, on efficiency and routing), and they are not comparable across labs (Claude 4 and Gemini 2.5 and GPT-4.1 coexisted as peers). Numbers also get skipped, retired, and reshuffled: OpenAI shipped GPT-4.5 in February 2025 as a research preview and retired it from the application programming interface (API) within months; “o2” never existed (trademark conflict — the o-series went o1, o3, o4-mini). Treat any claim of the form “version X.Y is N times better/bigger” as folklore unless a primary source says it.

A field guide to the model families

Everything in this section is dated by construction — it is the genealogy layer, a snapshot as of mid-2026. Newer point releases almost certainly exist by the time you read this; the cadence at every major lab is now measured in months. The updates page tracks what changed after this was written.

OpenAI

GPT-4 (March 2023) set the frontier for over a year: the first model to pass a wide slice of professional exams, and — per the folklore discussed above — widely believed but never confirmed to be a mixture-of-experts. Its technical report is also a landmark of predictability: final loss was forecast from runs using thousands of times less compute, exactly the scaling-law machinery of Chapter 32. GPT-4o (May 2024) made multimodality native — one network trained end-to-end on text, audio, and images rather than stitched-together pipelines — and its real-time voice mode previewed the interface direction (Chapter 41 covers how this works). The o-series (o1 in September 2024, then o3) introduced reinforcement-learned chain-of-thought reasoning as a separate product line — the paradigm Chapter 37 is about — at the cost of a confusing model picker with two incompatible naming schemes. GPT-5 (August 2025) resolved that by unifying the lines behind a router: one product name that dispatches each query to a fast model or a deliberate reasoning model (more on this pattern below, and in the router figure). The GPT-5.x point releases continued from late 2025 (5.1 in November 2025, with tunable “thinking” effort); given the cadence, newer point releases almost certainly exist as you read this — check the updates page. Separately, gpt-oss (August 2025) marked OpenAI’s return to open weights after six years: two Apache-2.0 mixture-of-experts models (approximately 117B and 21B total parameters, with roughly 5B and 4B active — figures from the release materials), competitive with the o-series minis.

Anthropic

The Claude 3 family (March 2024) established the three-tier small/medium/large naming — Haiku, Sonnet, Opus — that the industry quietly adopted as its mental model, and Opus was the first model many considered a true GPT-4 peer. Anthropic’s lineage differentiator is Constitutional AI (Chapter 35): alignment via an explicit set of principles the model critiques itself against, rather than purely via human preference labels. Claude 3.5 Sonnet (June 2024) then did something strategically interesting: a mid-tier model that beat the previous flagship, especially at coding — the release that made Claude the default among many software engineers. Claude 3.7 Sonnet (February 2025) was the first hybrid reasoner: one set of weights that answers instantly or “thinks” visibly at length, with a user-set thinking budget. Claude 4 (Opus 4 and Sonnet 4, May 2025) leaned hard into long-horizon agentic work and shipped alongside Claude Code, a terminal agent that became a flagship product in its own right (Chapter 44). The 4.5 wave (Sonnet 4.5 in September, Haiku 4.5 in October, Opus 4.5 in November 2025) compressed the tiers — each new small model matching the previous large one. The Claude 5 generation arrived in mid-2026, and not in the expected shape: instead of an Opus 5 (the Opus line had continued to 4.8), Anthropic introduced a new tier above Opus — the Mythos class — previewed under gated access in April 2026 and shipped publicly in June as Fable 5 (alongside the more restricted Mythos 5), with Sonnet 5 following weeks later. Per Anthropic’s descriptions, Fable 5 and Mythos 5 are the same underlying frontier model in different packaging — different safeguards, access, and deployment arrangements — a reminder that a “model name” increasingly labels a product configuration, not a set of weights.

Google DeepMind

Google’s story is a slow conversion of overwhelming structural advantages — tensor processing units (TPUs), data centers, YouTube, Search — into frontier products. Gemini 1.0 (December 2023) merged the Google Brain and DeepMind efforts; Gemini 1.5 (February 2024) was the shock: a mixture-of-experts model with a one-million-token context window (ten million in research settings), demonstrated with near-perfect recall in needle-in-a-haystack tests — the release that made long context a standard axis of competition. Gemini 2.0 (December 2024) pushed native multimodality and agent tooling; 2.5 Pro (March 2025) added always-on thinking with controllable budgets and topped many leaderboards through mid-2025. Gemini 3 (late 2025) continued the line as the flagship. The durable point is vertical integration: Google is the only frontier lab training on its own silicon (TPUs; Chapter 33) at its own scale, which insulates it from graphics processing unit (GPU) supply and pricing and shows up in aggressive price-per-token positioning (the Flash tier).

Meta and Llama

Meta chose the opposite strategy: give the weights away and let the ecosystem build on them. Llama 2 (July 2023) made open weights commercially usable; Llama 3 (April 2024) and especially Llama 3.1 405B (July 2024) proved open weights could reach the frontier — a dense 405-billion-parameter model whose report remains the most complete public account of a frontier training run (16,000+ GPUs, hardware failure rates, data recipes — the reality behind Chapter 34). Llama 4 (April 2025) switched to mixture-of-experts and native multimodality (Scout and Maverick, each ~17B active parameters; a ~2-trillion-parameter “Behemoth” teacher was announced but repeatedly delayed). Then came the wobbles: the LM Arena experimental-variant controversy, a lukewarm developer reception, and a mid-2025 reorganization into Meta Superintelligence Labs with heavy external hiring and public signals that future frontier models may not all be open. As of mid-2026 the open-Llama strategy’s future is uncertain — an honest unknown, and a major plot point for Chapter 47.

DeepSeek

A Chinese lab spun out of a quantitative hedge fund (High-Flyer), and the efficiency story of the era. DeepSeek-V2 (May 2024) introduced multi-head latent attention (MLA) — compressing the key–value (KV) cache (Chapter 28) by an order of magnitude. DeepSeek-V3 (December 2024) combined MLA, a 671B-total / 37B-active mixture-of-experts, 8-bit floating-point (FP8) training, and multi-token prediction, and reported a final-run cost of ~2.79M H800-GPU-hours — about $5.6M at rental prices — for a model competitive with closed flagships. (The figure is disclosed but frequently misread: it excludes research, ablations, prior runs, and the cluster itself.) DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025) applied reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards to V3 and released the result, reasoning traces and all, under an MIT license — the “efficiency shock” that briefly knocked roughly 17% off Nvidia’s market value in a day (approximate; the point is the magnitude of the reaction, which said more about market assumptions than about the model). V3.1 (August 2025) merged the chat and reasoning lines into one hybrid-thinking model; newer releases have continued since — see the updates page.

Qwen, and the rest of the open-weight wave

Alibaba’s Qwen became the most-forked open family on Hugging Face — by number of derivative fine-tunes, the default base for the world’s open-model tinkering. Qwen2.5 (late 2024) shipped strong dense models at every size from 0.5B to 72B; Qwen3 (2025) added mixture-of-experts flagships (235B total / 22B active) and a unified thinking toggle in one set of weights. Mistral (France) proved a small team could matter — Mistral 7B (2023) and Mixtral 8×7B (December 2023, the release that mainstreamed open MoE) — and remains Europe’s flagship lab, mixing Apache-2.0 small models with closed commercial ones. xAI built differently: the Colossus cluster in Memphis went from zero to roughly 100,000 H100s in about 122 days (a figure from Nvidia’s and xAI’s own accounts, later expanded further), and Grok 3 (February 2025) and Grok 4 (July 2025) demonstrated that enough concentrated compute buys a seat at the frontier table quickly. Moonshot AI’s Kimi K2 (July 2025) was a milestone twice over: the first trillion-parameter-class open-weight model (1.04T total / 32B active), and the first frontier-scale run trained with the Muon optimizer (Chapter 11) via its MuonClip variant — post-trained specifically for agentic tool use. Zhipu AI’s GLM-4.5 (355B total / 32B active, July 2025) and successors carved out a niche as agentic-coding value leaders. The pattern across the wave: Chinese labs now produce the majority of notable open-weight releases, and every one of them is a sparse mixture-of-experts.

Theme: everything at the frontier is a mixture-of-experts

Strip away the branding and one architectural fact organizes the whole table above: every frontier-scale model whose architecture is public is a sparse mixture-of-experts, and the closed ones are widely inferred to be too. The reasons were built in Chapter 29; here is why the economics made it universal.

💡Intuition

A dense model spends its entire parameter count on every token — the token “the” activates the same 405 billion parameters as a hard math step. A mixture-of-experts (MoE) model keeps a large library of parameters (capacity to store knowledge) but consults only a small, input-dependent fraction per token (compute to process it). Since serving cost scales with active parameters and knowledge scales more with total parameters, MoE lets labs buy capacity and compute separately — and once one lab does that, everyone competing on price-per-capability has to.

∑The math

Recall from Chapter 32 that a forward pass costs about 2N2N2N floating-point operations (FLOPs) per token and training costs about C≈6NDC \approx 6NDC≈6ND, where NNN is the active parameter count and DDD the number of training tokens. For an MoE, total parameters NtotalN_{\text{total}}Ntotal​ and active parameters NactN_{\text{act}}Nact​ separate, so per-token compute is ≈2Nact\approx 2N_{\text{act}}≈2Nact​, independent of NtotalN_{\text{total}}Ntotal​ (ignoring routing overhead and the attention term). The design freedom is the sparsity ratio Nact/NtotalN_{\text{act}}/N_{\text{total}}Nact​/Ntotal​ — and the trend has been toward more aggressive sparsity: Mixtral ran at 2-of-8 experts (≈25%\approx 25\%≈25%), DeepSeek-V3 at 37/671≈5.5%37/671 \approx 5.5\%37/671≈5.5%, Kimi K2 at 32/1040≈3%32/1040 \approx 3\%32/1040≈3%.

✏️Worked example

Compare Llama 3.1 405B (dense) with DeepSeek-V3 (671B total, 37B active). Per generated token, the dense model’s expert-and-attention stack costs about 2×405×109=8.1×10112 \times 405\times10^9 = 8.1\times10^{11}2×405×109=8.1×1011 FLOPs; V3 costs about 2×37×109=7.4×10102 \times 37\times10^9 = 7.4\times10^{10}2×37×109=7.4×1010 — roughly 11× less compute per token despite holding 1.7× more total parameters. Training tells the same story: V3 trained on D=14.8D = 14.8D=14.8T tokens, so C≈6×37×109×14.8×1012≈3.3×1024C \approx 6 \times 37\times10^9 \times 14.8\times10^{12} \approx 3.3\times10^{24}C≈6×37×109×14.8×1012≈3.3×1024 FLOPs. Spread over the reported 2.788M H800-hours, that is 3.3×1024/(2.788×106×3600 s)≈3.3×10143.3\times10^{24} / (2.788\times10^6 \times 3600\,\text{s}) \approx 3.3\times10^{14}3.3×1024/(2.788×106×3600s)≈3.3×1014 FLOP/s per GPU — about a sixth of an H800’s dense FP8 peak, a believable utilization given that 6ND6ND6ND ignores attention FLOPs (Chapter 33). The arithmetic checks out; the “$5.6M model” was not magic, it was sparsity plus FP8 plus engineering.

⚙️In practice

As of mid-2026 the public frontier MoE recipe has converged on: many small experts (64–384) rather than a few large ones, a handful routed per token plus one always-on shared expert, fine-grained expert segmentation (DeepSeek’s contribution), and auxiliary-loss-free load balancing. Dense models survive below ~100B parameters, where deployment simplicity and fine-tunability (Chapter 38) outweigh serving efficiency — which is why Qwen ships a dense ladder and MoE flagships.

Theme: thinking modes and routers as product architecture

The second structural shift of the period is that “a model” stopped being one forward pass. Chapter 37 covered the training side — reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards teaching models to reason in long chains of thought. The landscape consequence is a product pattern: every major provider now sells a fast mode and a thinking mode, and increasingly a router that chooses between them per query.

The economics force the pattern. Thinking tokens cost the same to generate as answer tokens, and a hard problem may burn tens of thousands of them (Chapter 39 priced this out); spending that on “write a haiku” is waste, and not spending it on “find the bug in this concurrency code” is failure. The three responses visible in mid-2026: a user toggle (Claude’s extended thinking, Qwen3’s thinking mode — one set of hybrid weights, budget exposed to the caller), an automatic router (GPT-5’s headline design: a dispatcher sends each query to a fast model or a reasoning model, with escalation on demand), and separate products (the pre-GPT-5 o-series). Routers also quietly explain otherwise-baffling user reports of the “same” model varying in quality: two calls to one product name may hit different underlying models depending on load, query, and subscription tier.

⚠️Careful

Routers break naive evaluation. If you benchmark “GPT-5” or any routed product, you are benchmarking the router’s dispatch decisions as much as any underlying network — and dispatch can change silently between your runs. Serious evaluations pin explicit model variants and thinking budgets via the API; Chapter 46 returns to this.

Theme: the open-weight gap closed to months

In March 2023 the question “how far are open models behind?” had a depressing answer: roughly a year and a half, and the gap looked structural. By 2026 the honest answer is months, sometimes weeks, on measurable benchmarks — with real caveats.

Two dated anchor pairs make the trend concrete. GPT-4 (March 2023) scored 86.4% on MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding), the multi-subject exam benchmark from Chapter 21; the first open-weight model to unambiguously match it was Llama 3.1 405B (July 2024, 87.3% in the same five-shot setting) — a ~16-month lag. For reasoning: o1-preview landed September 2024; DeepSeek-R1 matched its class on competition math from the American Invitational Mathematics Examination (AIME) by January 2025, scoring 79.8% on AIME 2024 versus o1’s reported 79.2%: a ~4-month lag. On agentic-coding evaluations in 2025, releases like Kimi K2 and GLM-4.6 landed within weeks-to-months of closed peers on the same public benchmarks.

Three caveats keep this honest. First, benchmark parity is not product parity: closed systems bundle routing, tools, multimodal polish, and long-tail reliability that public benchmarks miss (Chapter 46). Second, open models can train on distilled outputs of closed ones — some convergence is followership, not independent capability (R1’s own paper distills R1 into Qwen and Llama checkpoints; the direction of idea-flow reversed too, with closed labs adopting open innovations like MLA). Third, the source of open weights shifted decisively toward Chinese labs during 2025, which entangles the open-vs-closed question with export controls and geopolitics. Chapter 47 takes up all three threads properly.

Theme: agents, multimodality, and the falling price of intelligence

Three more trends complete the durable picture; two get their own chapters, so we treat them briefly, and the third gets a worked example.

The agentic turn. Between 2024 and 2026 the unit of product value shifted from answers to work: models that call tools, browse, write and run code, and sustain multi-hour tasks with checkpoints. Benchmarks followed (SWE-bench for real GitHub issues; τ-bench and successors for tool use), post-training followed (trajectory-level reinforcement learning, as in Kimi K2’s agentic pipeline), and interfaces followed (Claude Code, OpenAI’s Operator-then-agent modes, the Model Context Protocol (MCP) as an emerging tool-connection standard). Chapter 44 builds this stack in detail. One durable observation: agentic capability improved faster than single-response quality in 2025–2026, because reinforcement learning on verifiable long-horizon tasks (Chapter 37) turned out to transfer better to work than to chat.

Multimodality as default. In 2023 image input was a flagship feature; by 2026 text-only frontier models are effectively gone — vision input is table stakes, audio in/out is standard on consumer tiers, and generation (image, increasingly video) ships alongside. The architectural story — early-fusion versus adapters, native audio tokens, unified tokenizers — is Chapter 41, the next chapter.

The falling price of intelligence. The least appreciated trend, because it is invisible in any single announcement. At fixed capability, the price of tokens collapsed continuously across the period.

✏️Worked example

Take a task of 10,000 input tokens and 1,000 output tokens — a long document plus a summary. At GPT-4’s launch pricing (March 2023: $30 per million input tokens, $60 per million output), it cost 10,000×30106+1,000×60106=0.30+0.06=\textdollar0.3610{,}000 \times \frac{30}{10^6} + 1{,}000 \times \frac{60}{10^6} = 0.30 + 0.06 = \textdollar 0.3610,000×10630​+1,000×10660​=0.30+0.06=\textdollar0.36. On GPT-4o (launched May 2024 at $5 / $15, cut to $2.50 / $10 per million by late 2024): 0.025+0.010=\textdollar0.0350.025 + 0.010 = \textdollar 0.0350.025+0.010=\textdollar0.035 — about 10× cheaper in roughly a year and a half, on a more capable model. On DeepSeek-V3’s API (2025 list price ≈ $0.27 / $1.10 per million): 0.0027+0.0011≈\textdollar0.0040.0027 + 0.0011 \approx \textdollar 0.0040.0027+0.0011≈\textdollar0.004 — roughly 95× below GPT-4 launch pricing for GPT-4-class output. All prices are snapshots and change frequently; the ratio, not any absolute number, is the lesson.

Analysts who track this systematically (Epoch AI, among others) estimate that the price of achieving a fixed benchmark score has fallen by somewhere between roughly 10× and several hundred× per year, depending on the capability level measured — an approximate, fast-moving figure, but robust in sign and startling in magnitude. The drivers are everything this book has covered: sparsity (this chapter), distillation into small models, quantization and serving optimization (Chapter 39), cheaper compute per FLOP (Chapter 33), and competition. The strategic consequence: frontier capability is a wasting asset. Whatever a flagship can do today, something 20× cheaper does within a year or two — which is why labs race for capabilities that compound (agents, research automation) rather than resting on any single model.

Theme: safety frameworks grew up alongside capability

The last durable trend is procedural rather than technical, and this section sticks to the factual record. Between 2023 and 2026, the major labs converted informal safety talk into published, versioned policy frameworks that gate releases on evaluation results.

Anthropic published its Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP) in September 2023: a commitment to classify models into AI Safety Levels (ASL) — modeled loosely on biosafety levels — with capability evaluations for risks like bioweapons uplift and autonomous replication, and required security and deployment safeguards at each level. Claude Opus 4 (May 2025) became the first model Anthropic shipped under ASL-3 protections. OpenAI published its Preparedness Framework (December 2023, revised since), scoring models on tracked risk categories with capability thresholds that trigger mitigations or halt deployment. Google DeepMind published its Frontier Safety Framework (May 2024, revised since) around “critical capability levels.” At the 2024 Seoul AI summit, over a dozen companies signed Frontier AI Safety Commitments promising published frameworks of this kind; the EU AI Act’s general-purpose-model obligations began phasing in during 2025, giving parts of this regime legal force in Europe.

Two honest observations, both factual. These frameworks are voluntary self-regulation (outside the EU’s reach), and critics note they can be revised by the same organizations they constrain — as several were, in both tightening and loosening directions. And they matter anyway: system cards now routinely report dangerous-capability evaluations (the empirical study of which connects directly to Part VII — you cannot evaluate what you cannot measure, and interpretability aims at what evaluation misses). The safety-framework genre, whatever its enforcement gaps, made frontier risk assessment a public, criticizable artifact instead of a private judgment. That is a real change from 2023, and it is likely to persist.

This chapter’s snapshot date matters more than most. Model names, prices, context windows, and leaderboard positions in this chapter were checked against sources available in mid-2026 and will drift fastest; the themes were chosen precisely because they have already survived three years of releases. Where a post-snapshot development matters to this book’s story, it will appear as a dated Update block here or on the updates page.

Where this leaves us

  • You can classify any frontier claim as disclosed, inferred, or folklore — and you know to read system cards and technical reports before leaderboards, and to distrust version-number arithmetic.
  • You can sketch the genealogies: OpenAI’s route to a unified router (GPT-4 → 4o → o-series → GPT-5.x), Anthropic’s tiered families ending in the Mythos class above Opus, Gemini’s context-and-TPU story, Llama’s open bet and its wobbles, DeepSeek’s efficiency shock, and the Qwen/Kimi/GLM open wave — all stamped mid-2026 snapshot.
  • You can explain why every frontier model is a mixture-of-experts, and verify the economics yourself: DeepSeek-V3’s 37B active parameters buy ~11× cheaper tokens than a 405B dense model, and 6ND6ND6ND reproduces its reported training budget within a plausible utilization factor.
  • You can describe thinking modes and routers as product architecture, and say why routed products complicate evaluation.
  • You can quantify the open-weight lag with dated parity events (~16 months on MMLU in 2023–24; ~4 months on competition math in 2024–25) and state the three caveats that keep that story honest.
  • You can compute the falling price of intelligence on a napkin and say why frontier capability is a wasting asset.
  • You can name the major safety frameworks (RSP/ASL, Preparedness, Frontier Safety) and what kind of commitment each represents.
