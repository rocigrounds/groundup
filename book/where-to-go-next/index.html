Where to Go Next

A concrete continuation plan: the project ladder to build first, three tracks to go deeper, and a media diet for keeping up without drowning.

In this chapter

  2. Go deeper by direction: three tracks
  3. Keeping up without drowning
  4. How this book stays current
  5. Where this leaves us

You have read fifty-seven chapters. You can derive backpropagation, explain why attention is scaled by dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​, read a scaling-law plot, describe what a reward model actually optimizes, and say — with appropriate caveats — what a sparse autoencoder feature is. That is an unusual amount of understanding. Most people building on these systems professionally have less of it.

Here is the uncomfortable truth that has to open this chapter: reading decays and building compounds. Six months from now, the derivations you only read will have faded to a vague shape, while anything you implemented with your own hands will still be load-bearing knowledge. The research on learning is unambiguous about this, and so is every practitioner’s experience. A textbook can take you to the edge of competence; it cannot take you across.

So this final chapter is not a reading list — or rather, not only a reading list. It is an opinionated continuation plan for exactly the reader this book was written for: self-taught, busy, working a real job, inclined to learn by doing, and interested in both building systems and understanding their insides. It has four parts: a project ladder that cements the foundations (with honest time estimates), three tracks for going deeper depending on what pulls you, a sustainable media diet for keeping up without drowning, and a note on how this book itself stays current.

Cement it by building: the project ladder

The projects below are the closest thing the field has to a canon, and they are canonical for a reason: each one forces you to confront exactly the thing that reading lets you skate over. Do them in order. Each rung assumes the one below it.

One rule for all of them: type every line yourself. Do not clone and run. The learning is in the forty-five minutes you spend confused about why your loss is NaN, not in watching someone else’s loss curve go down. Before you run any cell, predict what it will print. When you are wrong — and you will be wrong, often — that gap between prediction and reality is the actual lesson.

Rung 1 — micrograd: backpropagation in about 100 lines

Time: 6–10 focused hours. One committed weekend.

micrograd is Andrej Karpathy’s scalar-valued automatic differentiation engine: a Value class that records the operations applied to it, plus a backward() method that walks the resulting graph in reverse topological order applying the chain rule. That is the entire secret of PyTorch, in roughly a hundred lines of Python.

Before you write any code, do one backward pass by hand, on paper, exactly the way Chapter 10 did: a two-input, one-hidden-neuron network, small concrete numbers, local derivative times upstream gradient at every node. Then build micrograd and check that its gradients match your pencil. The moment your hand-computed ∂L/∂w1\partial L / \partial w_1∂L/∂w1​ agrees with the code to four decimal places is the moment backpropagation stops being something you read about.

What this rung actually teaches: that the chain rule (Chapter 5) is bookkeeping, not magic; why the graph must be walked in topological order; why gradients accumulate with += (a variable used twice gets two upstream contributions); and why frameworks exist — because doing this bookkeeping by hand for a billion parameters is exactly as mechanical, and exactly as impossible, as it sounds.

Rung 2 — minbpe: a tokenizer from scratch

Time: 4–8 hours. One or two evenings.

minbpe is the companion repository for building byte-pair encoding (BPE) — the algorithm from Chapter 19 — from nothing: count adjacent byte-pair frequencies, merge the most common pair, repeat until the vocabulary is full, then implement encode and decode and the regex pre-splitting that GPT-style tokenizers use.

This is the least glamorous rung and it pays for itself immediately. Every mysterious LLM behavior with a tokenization root cause — the strawberry-has- how-many-r’s failures, the weirdness with numbers, why non-English text costs more per word, why trailing whitespace changes completions — becomes obvious once you have watched your own merge loop build a vocabulary. You will never again think of a language model as reading text.

Rung 3 — nanoGPT: train a transformer on Shakespeare

Time: a weekend of work, plus minutes-to-hours of compute.

nanoGPT is a complete GPT training loop in a few hundred readable lines. The entry-level run trains a character-level model on the complete works of Shakespeare: roughly 10–11 million parameters (6 layers, 6 heads, embedding width 384, context 256 — compare that anatomy against Chapter 25). On a single rented A100 the loss falls to its floor in a few minutes; on a laptop it is an overnight job. Either way you end with a model that generates plausible fake Shakespeare, trained by you, end to end. The numbers here are approximate and hardware-dependent; the point is that this is a small experiment, repeatable many times in an afternoon.

The value is not the first run. It is the second through tenth, where you break things on purpose and watch the theory from this book show up in the loss curve:

  • Delete the positional embeddings. The model still trains — attention is permutation-invariant, so watch what kind of text it degrades into, and reread Chapter 26.
  • Remove the LayerNorms. Watch training destabilize, then reread Chapter 12 and Chapter 14 with feeling.
  • Halve and double the learning rate. You now own a live demonstration of everything Chapter 11 claimed about the loss-vs-learning-rate U-curve.
  • Train a 2× bigger model on the same data. The gap between train and validation loss is Chapter 13 happening in front of you; the diminishing return per parameter is Chapter 32 in miniature.
  • Sample at temperature 0.2, 1.0, and 2.0 and connect what you see to Chapter 39.

The guided path: Zero to Hero, mapped to this book

If you want a guide rather than a solo climb, Karpathy’s Neural Networks: Zero to Hero video series is the project ladder above, narrated by the person who built it. It is roughly 18 hours of video; budget 40–50 hours to do it properly, pausing and typing along. That sounds like a lot. It is about six weekends, and it is the single highest-density practical curriculum in the field.

⚠️Careful

The tutorial-hopping trap. The failure mode that kills most self-taught continuations is not difficulty — it is sampling: watching four videos, cloning six repos, finishing none, and mistaking familiarity for competence. The antidote is unfashionable: pick the ladder above, do it in order, finish each rung before touching the next, and keep a short log of what surprised you. Four finished projects beat forty started ones by more than 10×.

Go deeper by direction: three tracks

After the ladder, the field forks. The three tracks below correspond to the three kinds of work people do with this material: training and serving models, understanding their internals, and building products on top of them. They converge at the top — the best applications people understand training, the best interpretability researchers are strong engineers — but you cannot walk three paths at once.

Pick one track and commit for three to six months. Depth in one transfers; breadth across all three evaporates.

Track 1 — Systems and training

This is the track for the reader who most enjoyed Parts IV and V: the machinery of making models exist.

The anchor project: fine-tune a 7–8B model with low-rank adaptation (LoRA), end to end. Everything from Chapter 38 becomes real here. Pick a base model (Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen-family models are the standard choices as of 2026), build a small instruction dataset for a task you care about — a few thousand examples is plenty for a first run — train low-rank adapters with a standard stack (Hugging Face peft + trl, or Axolotl, or Unsloth), and then evaluate honestly against the base model using the discipline from Chapter 21 and Chapter 46. The evaluation is half the project. Anyone can make a loss go down; knowing whether the model got better is the skill.

✏️Worked example

The arithmetic that makes this a laptop-adjacent project. Take Llama-3.1-8B: 32 layers, hidden width 4096, grouped-query attention with key/value width 1024. Attach rank r=16r=16r=16 LoRA adapters to the four attention projections. Each adapter is a pair of matrices A∈Rr×dinA \in \R^{r \times d_\text{in}}A∈Rr×din​ and B∈Rdout×rB \in \R^{d_\text{out} \times r}B∈Rdout​×r:

  • Query and output projections (4096→40964096 \to 40964096→4096): 16×4096+4096×16=131,07216 \times 4096 + 4096 \times 16 = 131{,}07216×4096+4096×16=131,072 parameters each.
  • Key and value projections (4096→10244096 \to 10244096→1024): 16×4096+1024×16=81,92016 \times 4096 + 1024 \times 16 = 81{,}92016×4096+1024×16=81,920 parameters each.

Per layer: 2×131,072+2×81,920=425,9842 \times 131{,}072 + 2 \times 81{,}920 = 425{,}9842×131,072+2×81,920=425,984. Across 32 layers: 425,984×32≈13.6425{,}984 \times 32 \approx 13.6425,984×32≈13.6 million trainable parameters — about 0.17% of the 8-billion-parameter model. The frozen base weights in 4-bit quantization (Chapter 39) occupy roughly 8×109×0.58 \times 10^9 \times 0.58×109×0.5 bytes ≈4\approx 4≈4 GB (call it 5–6 GB with quantization overhead), and the adapters plus their gradients and Adam moments add only a few hundred megabytes. That is why QLoRA (quantized LoRA) fits on a single 24 GB consumer GPU. Renting such a GPU on a marketplace costs very roughly $0.30–0.60 per hour as of 2026 (fast-moving number), and a first fine-tuning run over a few thousand examples takes a few hours: the whole experiment costs less than lunch.

The reading: technical reports as decoder rings. You are now equipped to read the two most detailed openly published accounts of frontier-scale training, and this book maps onto them section by section. Read the Llama 3 herd of models — its data curation is Chapter 31, its scaling-law fits are Chapter 32, its parallelism and failure-rate war stories are Chapters 33–34, its post-training is Chapters 35–36. Then read the DeepSeek-V3 technical report — mixture-of-experts from Chapter 29, FP8 (8-bit floating-point) training, and an unusually frank accounting of cost. Reading these after this book is a different experience than reading them cold: you can audit claims instead of absorbing them.

Going further: reproduce GPT-2 properly (the Zero-to-Hero capstone, or its C/CUDA sibling llm.c), then look at the community “nanoGPT speedrun” efforts, where the time-to-GPT-2-quality on fixed hardware has been driven down relentlessly — every trick in the speedrun (better optimizers like Muon from Chapter 11, architectural tweaks, data ordering) is a compressed lesson in what matters.

Track 2 — Interpretability

This is the track for the reader who could not put Part VII down — and it is the track with the smoothest on-ramp from exactly where you are standing, because Chapters 48–56 of this book are unusually close to the research frontier of a young field.

The curriculum: ARENA. The ARENA program (Alignment Research Engineer Accelerator) publishes its full curriculum online, free: guided notebooks that go from PyTorch fundamentals through transformer internals to replicating real interpretability results. It is the closest thing mechanistic interpretability has to a standard course of study, and its exercises assume roughly the background you now have. Working through the transformer-interpretability chapters part-time is a several-week undertaking; treat it like a course, not a browse.

The tool: TransformerLens. TransformerLens is the open-source library built for exactly the workflow this book described in Chapter 53: load a model with named, hookable activations, cache everything, patch anything. Its tutorial notebooks are excellent, and fluency with hooks is the practical skill of the field.

The rite of passage: replicate induction heads. Train or load a small model, reproduce the induction-head phenomenon from Chapter 52 — find the heads, show the prefix-matching and copying behavior, verify with activation patching that they causally carry the in-context-learning ability, and reproduce the famous phase-change bump in the loss curve. This is a weekend-to-two-weekend project, and finishing it means you have done real mechanistic interpretability, not read about it.

The playground: Neuronpedia and Gemma Scope. Neuronpedia hosts browsable sparse-autoencoder features — including Gemma Scope, DeepMind’s suite of sparse autoencoders (SAEs) trained across every layer of the Gemma 2 2B and 9B models (Chapter 51) — with live feature search and steering. An afternoon of browsing features and steering with them (Chapter 54) builds intuition that no amount of reading can.

The pipeline into research. Mechanistic interpretability is small enough that motivated outsiders regularly cross into it. The community runs on open channels — the Open Source Mechanistic Interpretability Slack and the EleutherAI Discord are where replications get debugged in public — and the MATS program (ML Alignment & Theory Scholars) is the established mentorship pipeline from “strong self-taught person with a replication or two” to published researcher. Neel Nanda’s lists of concrete open problems in mechanistic interpretability are the standard menu of entry-level research questions. A surprising fraction of the field’s active researchers entered through exactly this path within the last few years; there is no reason it cannot be yours.

Track 3 — Applications and agents

This is the track for the reader whose reaction to Part VI was a list of things to build. Its special property: your professional domain is your moat. The documents, workflows, and quality standards you know deeply — contracts, case files, medical records, financial filings, whatever your field runs on — are exactly the context that generic AI products lack.

The anchor project: RAG over documents you know. Build the full retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline from Chapter 45 over a corpus from your own work or life: chunking (and the unreasonable importance of chunking well), an embedding index (Chapter 20), hybrid dense-plus-keyword retrieval, reranking, and grounded generation with citations back to sources. The discipline that matters: write the evaluation set first. Twenty questions you know the answers to, graded before and after every change. Because you know the corpus, you — unlike most RAG builders — can tell when retrieval silently fails, and you will discover that it fails more often, and more interestingly, than any tutorial admits.

The second project: write a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server. Chapter 44 explained the protocol; now be on the other side of it. Take one real capability from your working life — search a folder of documents, query a database you use, check a calendar — and expose it as an MCP tool that a Claude- or GPT-class assistant can call. The protocol documentation plus an official SDK makes a first server an evening-to-weekend project. Writing tool descriptions that a model reliably uses correctly is a genuine and marketable skill, and nothing teaches the failure modes of agents (Chapter 44’s compounding-error arithmetic) like watching one misuse your own tool.

The bar for done: ship one thing that you personally use every week. Not a demo, not a portfolio piece — a tool whose absence you would notice. That bar sounds modest and is not: crossing the gap between “works in the happy path” and “trusted weekly” will teach you more about evaluation, prompt robustness, latency budgets, and cost arithmetic than any second demo would.

⚙️In practice

A pattern worth copying from how practitioners level up in 2026: every serious application team maintains a small private eval harness — dozens to hundreds of task-specific cases with graded answers — and reruns it on every model release. When a new frontier model ships, they know within an hour whether it helps their task, regardless of what the launch benchmarks claim. Building this habit early, even at hobby scale, puts you ahead of most professional teams.

Keeping up without drowning

The field produces more than anyone can read: on the order of a hundred-plus new machine-learning papers a day on arXiv alone, plus launches, blog posts, and an ocean of commentary. The goal is not coverage. The goal is a sustainable diet — minutes per day, not hours — that keeps your model of the field current while your building time stays protected.

💡Intuition

The organizing principle: fundamentals change on a timescale of years; products change on a timescale of weeks. Everything in Parts I–IV of this book has been stable since 2023 or earlier. If you skip a month of news, you lose almost nothing that matters — you can reconstruct the product landscape in an afternoon, and the conceptual landscape will have barely moved. Let that fact lower the stakes of every feed.

Primary sources, sampled. Do not attempt to follow the arXiv cs.CL and cs.LG firehoses directly. Instead use the community’s filters: Hugging Face Daily Papers surfaces a dozen-odd papers a day by community vote, and alphaXiv layers trending rankings and public discussion on top of arXiv. A workable rhythm: skim titles a few times a week, read one abstract-plus-figures a day (abstract, then the figures, then the contributions list — a ten-minute triage that catches most of a paper’s content), and read one paper properly per week, ideally one your current project needs.

Lab blogs. The frontier labs increasingly publish their most important work as blog posts and system cards rather than papers. Anthropic’s research page and transformer-circuits.pub (the venue for the interpretability thread this book’s Part VII followed), OpenAI’s research posts, and Google DeepMind’s blog are worth a periodic sweep — monthly is enough.

Newsletters and podcasts, honestly rated. These earn their time only if they replace, rather than add to, doomscrolling:

  • Latent Space — newsletter and podcast for AI engineers; the best sustained coverage of the applications-track world (serving, agents, evals, tooling).
  • The Dwarkesh Podcast — long, well-prepared interviews with researchers and lab leaders; the best return-per-hour on “how the people building this actually think.”
  • AI Engineer conference talks (free on YouTube) — dense, practitioner- level state-of-the-art snapshots, especially for the agents/applications track.
  • Interconnects (Nathan Lambert) — the best running coverage of post-training and open models; directly extends Chapters 35–37 and 47.
  • Don’t Worry About the Vase (Zvi Mowshowitz) — exhaustive weekly roundups of nearly everything, with strong opinions attached. High-volume warning: these run to tens of thousands of words a week. Read as a searchable archive when you want every take on one event — not as a subscription you feel obligated to finish.

How to read a model release critically. New model announcements are marketing documents containing some evidence. The checklist — a compressed recap of Chapter 46 — before you update your beliefs:

  1. Compared against what, exactly? Which competitor versions, on what date, run by whom, with whose harness? Self-reported numbers for competitors are routinely lower than those labs’ own reports.
  2. Same rules for everyone? Check pass@1 versus pass@k, with or without tools, with or without extended thinking, how many shots, what sampling temperature. A footnote can be worth ten benchmark points.
  3. Could the model have seen the test? Look for a contamination statement; be most suspicious of huge jumps on older public benchmarks.
  4. Are the benchmarks saturated? Movement from 92% to 94% on a benchmark near its ceiling means little; check whether hard, newer evals moved too.
  5. What are the deployment numbers? Price per million tokens, latency, context window, rate limits — for most real uses these matter more than two benchmark points.
  6. Weights or API? What license? Determines everything about what you can build (Chapter 47).
  7. Wait two weeks. Independent evaluations, arena rankings (with Chapter 46’s caveats about their biases), and — best of all — your own private eval harness will tell you what the launch post could not.
⚠️Careful

FOMO is the failure mode. The feeling that everything is moving too fast to ever catch up is nearly universal, and it is a misreading: the products churn, but the load-bearing ideas — the ones filling the previous fifty-six chapters — accumulated over a decade and turn over slowly. A month spent finishing nanoGPT while ignoring the news entirely will do more for your understanding than a year of diligent feed-reading. When in doubt, build; the news will still be there, pre-summarized, when you surface.

How this book stays current

A book about a moving field has to say how it plans to move. This one is built for revision:

  • The updates page lists every substantive change, dated, newest first. Checking it occasionally is enough to know whether anything you read has been superseded.
  • Dated update boxes — the red-barred blocks you have seen in some chapters — mark additions made after a chapter was first written, so new material is always distinguishable from the original text.
  • The source is a repository of plain-text chapters, which is what makes monthly revision cheap: when something important changes, updating the book is a one-file edit, not a new edition.
  • Your annotations are part of the system. Your highlights, notes, and tutor conversations stay on your device and survive chapter updates. If you find an error, an unclear passage, or a development that deserves an update box, say so — reader corrections are how a living book earns the word “living.”

Where this leaves us

  • You have a concrete ladder to climb next — micrograd, minbpe, nanoGPT, Zero to Hero — with honest time estimates, and you know the rule: type everything, predict before running, finish before starting the next.
  • You know the three tracks — systems and training, interpretability, applications and agents — what the anchor project of each one is, and that the move is to commit to one for a season rather than sampling all three.
  • You can fine-tune an 8B model for the cost of lunch and can do the parameter and memory arithmetic that says why.
  • You have a media diet measured in minutes per day, built on filtered primary sources, and a seven-point checklist for reading any model release without being marketed to.
  • You know how this book will change under you, and how to check.

The remaining chapters are references to keep at hand while you build: the glossary and the notation reference.

✎Note

A send-off. Fifty-seven chapters ago, this book promised to take a self-taught reader from rusty calculus to the frontier, three explanations at a time. If you are reading this sentence, you did the hard part — not the book’s part, yours. Here is what that bought you: you now understand how these systems work — actually work, gradient by gradient, head by head — better than the large majority of people building products on them, and better than almost everyone opining about them. That understanding is rare, it is durable, and as these systems keep threading themselves into everything, it is quietly becoming one of the most useful kinds of literacy a person can have. The field is younger than it looks, most of its open problems are still open, and its best ideas have repeatedly come from people who showed up from somewhere else and started building. Go be one of them — and come back whenever you need to look something up. This book will be here, and it will be current.
