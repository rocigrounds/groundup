Multi-Head Attention

Why one attention pattern per token is not enough, and how running many small attention operations in parallel subspaces — then mixing them back — became the standard.

The previous chapter built one attention operation: every token asks a question (its query), every token advertises what it holds (its key), the dot products become a softmax distribution, and the token collects a weighted average of value vectors. It works. But look closely at what one attention operation gives each token: one softmax, which means one probability distribution over the sequence, which means one weighted average. Per token, per layer, a single head produces a single mixing pattern — one way of combining information from the context.

Language needs more than one way at once. Take this sentence:

The keys that Sarah left on the counter were gone, so she blamed her brother, who denied taking them.

To process the final word “them,” a model benefits from tracking at least three different relations simultaneously: a coreference relation reaching far back to “keys” (that is what “them” refers to); a syntactic relation to “taking,” the verb that “them” is the object of; and a local positional relation to the immediately preceding words that set the grammatical frame. Meanwhile “she” needs a completely different long-range link, to “Sarah,” and “who” needs one to “brother.” A single softmax must smear its budget of 1.0 across all these targets, and a single weighted average then blends the retrieved information into one vector — the coreference signal, the syntax signal, and the local-context signal averaged into mush.

The fix in “Attention Is All You Need” is almost anticlimactic: run several small attention operations in parallel — called heads — each with its own learned projections, each free to compute its own pattern, and then concatenate their outputs and mix them with one more matrix. That is multi-head attention (MHA), and it is used almost unchanged in every frontier model in 2026. This chapter builds it completely: the construction, the dimension bookkeeping at real scale, the reshape trick that makes it one big matrix multiply in practice, the underappreciated role of the output matrix WOW_OWO​, and the surprisingly characterful things individual heads turn out to learn.

One softmax is one mixing pattern

Recall the single-head operation from scaled dot-product attention. With token representations stacked as rows of XXX (shape T×dT \times dT×d, where TTT is the sequence length and ddd the model dimension), we form Q=XWQQ = XW_QQ=XWQ​, K=XWKK = XW_KK=XWK​, V=XWVV = XW_VV=XWV​ and compute

Attention(Q,K,V)  =  softmax⁡ ⁣(QK⊤dk)V.\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) \;=\; \softmax\!\left(\frac{QK\T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V .Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(dk​​QK⊤​)V.

The softmax produces an attention matrix AAA of shape T×TT \times TT×T: row iii is a probability distribution saying how much token iii draws from each position. Token iii‘s output is then ∑jAijvj\sum_j A_{ij} v_j∑j​Aij​vj​ — a convex combination of value vectors, with a single set of weights Ai1,…,AiTA_{i1}, \dots, A_{iT}Ai1​,…,AiT​.

Two limitations follow directly.

Averaging conflates. Suppose the useful weights for “them” would be 0.5 on “keys” and 0.5 on “taking.” The output is the midpoint of two value vectors that carry entirely different kinds of information (an entity’s identity; a verb’s argument structure). Downstream layers receive the blend and must somehow un-blend it. It is like being allowed one phone call to summarize two unrelated conversations.

One relation per layer. Even if the model is willing to blend, it can only express one pattern of relevance per token. “Look at the previous word” and “look at the noun this pronoun refers to” are different patterns; a single attention matrix must be one or the other or a compromise.

💡Intuition

Think of a head as a spotlight operator in a theater: it can light up any combination of actors, but it controls one light with one color. Multi-head attention hires eight (or 32, or 128) operators, each with their own light and their own instructions about what to look for. One tracks who spoke last, one follows the protagonist around the stage, one keeps the exits lit. The audience (the next layer) sees all the lights at once. The operators do not coordinate during the show — each head computes its pattern independently, and only afterward are their contributions combined.

There is a subtler point worth pausing on. You might propose fixing the averaging problem by making one head bigger — more expressive projections, same single pattern. But the bottleneck is not the size of the projections; it is the fact that one softmax yields one distribution. Multi-head attention spends its parameter budget on more patterns rather than richer single patterns, and — as we will see — it does so at no extra parameter cost compared to one full-size head.

The construction: parallel attention in learned subspaces

Here is the whole mechanism. Pick a number of heads hhh that divides the model dimension ddd, and set the per-head dimension

dk  =  dh.d_k \;=\; \frac{d}{h}.dk​=hd​.

Each head iii gets its own three projection matrices, WQ(i)W_Q^{(i)}WQ(i)​, WK(i)W_K^{(i)}WK(i)​, WV(i)W_V^{(i)}WV(i)​, each of shape d×dkd \times d_kd×dk​. These are tall, thin matrices: they map the full ddd-dimensional token representation down into a small dkd_kdk​-dimensional subspace. Each head then runs ordinary scaled dot-product attention inside its own subspace, completely independently of the others.

∑The math

For input X∈RT×dX \in \R^{T \times d}X∈RT×d and heads i=1,…,hi = 1, \dots, hi=1,…,h:

headi  =  softmax⁡ ⁣((XWQ(i))(XWK(i))⊤dk)XWV(i)    ∈  RT×dk.\mathrm{head}_i \;=\; \softmax\!\left(\frac{(XW_Q^{(i)})(XW_K^{(i)})\T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) XW_V^{(i)} \;\;\in\; \R^{T \times d_k}.headi​=softmax(dk​​(XWQ(i)​)(XWK(i)​)⊤​)XWV(i)​∈RT×dk​.

Each head produces a T×dkT \times d_kT×dk​ output — its own attention pattern (T×TT \times TT×T, one per head) applied to its own values. The heads’ outputs are concatenated side by side back to width h⋅dk=dh \cdot d_k = dh⋅dk​=d, then mixed by one final square matrix WO∈Rd×dW_O \in \R^{d \times d}WO​∈Rd×d:

MHA(X)  =  Concat(head1,…,headh) WO    ∈  RT×d.\mathrm{MHA}(X) \;=\; \mathrm{Concat}(\mathrm{head}_1, \dots, \mathrm{head}_h)\, W_O \;\;\in\; \R^{T \times d}.MHA(X)=Concat(head1​,…,headh​)WO​∈RT×d.

Symbol inventory: TTT is sequence length; ddd is the model (residual stream) dimension; hhh is the head count; dkd_kdk​ is the per-head dimension, used for queries, keys, and values alike in the standard setup; WOW_OWO​ is the output projection whose job we examine in its own section below.

Parameter count. Per head, the three projections cost 3 d dk3\,d\,d_k3ddk​ parameters; across hhh heads that is 3 d (hdk)=3d23\,d\,(h d_k) = 3d^23d(hdk​)=3d2, and WOW_OWO​ adds d2d^2d2 more: 4d24d^24d2 total. A single head with full-size d×dd \times dd×d projections would also cost 4d24d^24d2 (counting an output matrix). Multi-head attention is not more expensive — it reallocates the same budget from one high-rank pattern to hhh low-rank patterns.

Let us trace every shape at the original transformer’s scale — d=512d = 512d=512, h=8h = 8h=8, dk=64d_k = 64dk​=64 — because keeping the bookkeeping straight is half of understanding the mechanism:

  1. Input XXX: T×512T \times 512T×512.
  2. Head iii‘s queries XWQ(i)XW_Q^{(i)}XWQ(i)​: (T×512)(512×64)=T×64(T \times 512)(512 \times 64) = T \times 64(T×512)(512×64)=T×64. Same for keys and values.
  3. Head iii‘s score matrix: (T×64)(64×T)=T×T(T \times 64)(64 \times T) = T \times T(T×64)(64×T)=T×T. Eight of these exist in parallel — eight separate softmaxes, eight separate patterns.
  4. Head iii‘s output: (T×T)(T×64)=T×64(T \times T)(T \times 64) = T \times 64(T×T)(T×64)=T×64.
  5. Concatenation: eight blocks of width 64 side by side =T×512= T \times 512=T×512.
  6. Output projection: (T×512)(512×512)=T×512(T \times 512)(512 \times 512) = T \times 512(T×512)(512×512)=T×512 — same shape as the input, ready to be added back to the residual stream.

Parameters: 4×5122=1,048,5764 \times 512^2 = 1{,}048{,}5764×5122=1,048,576 — about a million per attention block.

A pencil-sized run with d=4d = 4d=4, h=2h = 2h=2, dk=2d_k = 2dk​=2, and two tokens:

With one head, the model would have to pick one pattern or average them into a fifty-fifty blur.

Why subspaces: the low-rank viewpoint

Why project down to dk=d/hd_k = d/hdk​=d/h at all? Why not give every head full ddd-dimensional queries and keys? Cost is one answer (hhh full-size heads would multiply parameters and compute by hhh), but the deeper answer is a payoff from Chapter 2: a tall, thin matrix is a choice of viewpoint. Multiplying by the d×dkd \times d_kd×dk​ matrix WQ(i)W_Q^{(i)}WQ(i)​ projects the token’s representation onto dkd_kdk​ learned directions and discards everything else. Each head, in other words, decides which 64- or 128-dimensional slice of the residual stream it cares about — and different heads learn to care about different slices.

This matters because of what the residual stream is. As the residual connections chapter described, the ddd-dimensional vector flowing through the network is a shared workspace where many kinds of information coexist as directions: embedding content, positional information, syntactic role, and features written by earlier layers — plausibly many more features than dimensions, via the superposition phenomenon we will meet in Chapter 50. A head that wants to match pronouns to referents should read the “entity identity” directions and ignore the rest; a head that tracks adjacency should read positional directions and ignore content entirely. The per-head projections are exactly this filter, learned end-to-end by gradient descent.

There is a clean linear-algebra way to state it, which the interpretability literature — especially “A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits” — leans on heavily. Head iii‘s attention scores depend on XXX only through the bilinear form

(XWQ(i))(XWK(i))⊤  =  X(WQ(i)WK(i)⊤)X⊤,(XW_Q^{(i)})(XW_K^{(i)})\T \;=\; X \left(W_Q^{(i)} W_K^{(i)\T}\right) X\T,(XWQ(i)​)(XWK(i)​)⊤=X(WQ(i)​WK(i)⊤​)X⊤,

and the d×dd \times dd×d matrix WQ(i)WK(i)⊤W_Q^{(i)} W_K^{(i)\T}WQ(i)​WK(i)⊤​ — often called the head’s QK circuit — has rank at most dkd_kdk​. Similarly the head’s effect on the output flows through WV(i)W_V^{(i)}WV(i)​ times its slice of WOW_OWO​ (the OV circuit), also rank at most dkd_kdk​. Each head is a rank-limited question (“which token pairs are relevant, judged along these 64 directions?”) paired with a rank-limited answer (“copy these 64 directions of information from the attended tokens”). Multi-head attention is a bet that language decomposes into many such narrow relations — and a decade of results suggests the bet was right.

The reshape trick: how it is actually implemented

The math above says “each head has its own WQ(i)W_Q^{(i)}WQ(i)​, run hhh separate attentions.” No real implementation loops over heads. Instead, all hhh query projections are stacked side by side into one d×dd \times dd×d matrix WQ=[ WQ(1) ∣ WQ(2) ∣ ⋯ ∣ WQ(h) ]W_Q = [\,W_Q^{(1)} \,|\, W_Q^{(2)} \,|\, \cdots \,|\, W_Q^{(h)}\,]WQ​=[WQ(1)​∣WQ(2)​∣⋯∣WQ(h)​], so a single matrix multiply XWQXW_QXWQ​ computes every head’s queries at once. The result, of shape T×dT \times dT×d, is then reshaped — reinterpreted, no data moved — as T×h×dkT \times h \times d_kT×h×dk​ and transposed so the head axis comes first. From there, the batched matrix multiplies of attention run over all heads simultaneously; a graphics processing unit (GPU) treats the head axis as just another batch dimension.

def multi_head_attention(X, Wq, Wk, Wv, Wo, h):
    B, T, d = X.shape                # batch, seq length, model dim
    dk = d // h
    # one big matmul each, then *view* the result as h heads
    Q = (X @ Wq).view(B, T, h, dk).transpose(1, 2)   # (B, h, T, dk)
    K = (X @ Wk).view(B, T, h, dk).transpose(1, 2)   # (B, h, T, dk)
    V = (X @ Wv).view(B, T, h, dk).transpose(1, 2)   # (B, h, T, dk)
    A = (Q @ K.transpose(-2, -1)) / dk**0.5          # (B, h, T, T)
    A = A.softmax(dim=-1)             # causal mask omitted for clarity
    out = A @ V                                      # (B, h, T, dk)
    out = out.transpose(1, 2).reshape(B, T, d)       # concat = un-view
    return out @ Wo                                  # (B, T, d)

Note what the “concatenation” in the formula becomes: the final transpose + reshape. Since concatenating head outputs is the inverse of the view-as-heads step, the whole multi-head structure costs a few reshapes and zero extra floating-point operations relative to single-head attention of the same total width. Production code differs only in fusing WQW_QWQ​, WKW_KWK​, WVW_VWV​ into one matrix (one d×3dd \times 3dd×3d multiply) and in replacing the explicit softmax lines with a fused kernel such as FlashAttention — which never materializes the T×TT \times TT×T matrices at all, a story told in the efficient attention chapter.

⚠️Careful

A common misreading of the diagram: the heads do not see different pieces of the input. Every head reads the same full ddd-dimensional token vectors; it is the learned projections that differ. The reshape trick can reinforce the confusion, because view(B, T, h, dk) looks like it “splits the embedding into chunks.” It splits the projected tensor — after WQW_QWQ​ has already mixed all 512 input dimensions into every output dimension. Which directions each head reads is learned, not assigned by position in the vector.

What W_O does: every head writes through its own channel

The output matrix WOW_OWO​ looks like an afterthought — one more square matrix after the interesting part. It is worth a section, because both the practical and the interpretability view of multi-head attention hinge on it.

The practical view: concatenation places head outputs side by side, but “side by side” means nothing to the next layer, which consumes a single ddd-dimensional vector. Without WOW_OWO​, head 3’s output would only ever occupy coordinates 129–192 of the residual stream, and only whatever reads those coordinates could use it. WOW_OWO​ lets every head’s findings be written to any directions of the stream, in any combination — it is the step where the eight independent spotlight operators’ observations are merged into one report.

The interpretability view comes from a small algebraic move. Split WOW_OWO​ horizontally into hhh blocks of shape dk×dd_k \times ddk​×d, one per head: WO=[ WO(1);⋯ ;WO(h) ]W_O = [\,W_O^{(1)}; \cdots; W_O^{(h)}\,]WO​=[WO(1)​;⋯;WO(h)​] stacked vertically. Then

Concat(head1,…,headh) WO  =  ∑i=1hheadi WO(i).\mathrm{Concat}(\mathrm{head}_1, \dots, \mathrm{head}_h)\,W_O \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{h} \mathrm{head}_i \, W_O^{(i)}.Concat(head1​,…,headh​)WO​=i=1∑h​headi​WO(i)​.

The concatenate-then-project formulation is exactly equivalent to: each head independently writes its own ddd-dimensional contribution into the residual stream, and the contributions add. There is no interaction between heads anywhere in the block — head iii reads through WQ(i),WK(i),WV(i)W_Q^{(i)}, W_K^{(i)}, W_V^{(i)}WQ(i)​,WK(i)​,WV(i)​, writes through WO(i)W_O^{(i)}WO(i)​, and never touches head jjj. This additive, per-head decomposition is why interpretability researchers can meaningfully ask “what does head 5 in layer 9 do?” and ablate or analyze it in isolation — the question would be ill-posed if heads were entangled inside the block. The circuits chapter builds its entire analysis on this decomposition.

✎Note

A degrees-of-freedom aside: because head iii‘s output is A(i)XWV(i)WO(i)A^{(i)} X W_V^{(i)} W_O^{(i)}A(i)XWV(i)​WO(i)​, only the product WV(i)WO(i)W_V^{(i)} W_O^{(i)}WV(i)​WO(i)​ (a rank-≤dk\le d_k≤dk​, d×dd \times dd×d matrix) matters — any invertible dk×dkd_k \times d_kdk​×dk​ matrix could be slipped between them without changing the function. The same holds for WQ(i)WK(i)⊤W_Q^{(i)} W_K^{(i)\T}WQ(i)​WK(i)⊤​. So “the query weights” of a trained head are not unique; only the two low-rank circuit matrices are. Keep this in mind when reading interpretability work that inspects raw weight matrices.

What heads actually learn

Multi-head attention was proposed on the hope that heads would specialize. They do — and by 2019 researchers armed with nothing but attention visualizations had built a small zoology. The examples below are drawn from Clark et al. (2019) on BERT, Voita et al. (2019) on translation models, and the interpretability work on GPT-style models collected at transformer-circuits.pub.

Previous-token heads put nearly all their weight on position i−1i-1i−1, for every iii. They look trivial and are anything but: copying the previous token’s content into the current position is a key subroutine in larger circuits (as the induction mechanism below shows).

Positional / local heads attend to a small window of nearby tokens, often with smoothly decaying weights — a learned convolution, in effect, recovering some of what convolutional neural networks (CNNs) got by construction.

Syntactic heads track grammatical relations: Clark et al. found individual BERT heads that attend from direct objects to their verbs, from noun modifiers to their nouns, and from prepositions to their objects, with accuracy far beyond any positional baseline — none of it ever supervised. Coreference-like heads that link pronouns back to referents also appear, which is exactly the capability our “keys / them” sentence demanded.

Induction heads are the most celebrated. Found in GPT-style models by Olsson et al. (2022), an induction head implements the rule: if the pattern “A B” occurred earlier and the current token is “A” again, attend to the earlier “B” and predict it next. It is a learned copy-paste mechanism, built from two heads in different layers cooperating (a previous-token head feeds it), and it is a leading candidate explanation for in-context learning. We treat it fully in the circuits chapter; here it stands as proof that heads specialize and compose.

Honesty requires the other half of the story. In every model studied, many heads resist any clean description — their patterns are diffuse, or shift with context, or look interpretable on some inputs and noisy on others. Some heads may operate on features that do not correspond to human concepts at all, a possibility the superposition chapter makes precise. The named head types are the photogenic minority.

And heads are highly redundant. Michel, Levy & Neubig (2019) — the paper whose title asks “Are Sixteen Heads Really Better than One?” — found that in trained BERT and translation models, a large fraction of heads can be removed at test time with minimal performance loss, and some layers can be cut to a single head. Voita et al. reached a similar verdict from the other direction: in their translation experiments, a small set of specialized heads (positional, syntactic, rare-word-tracking) did the heavy lifting, and the large majority of encoder heads — 38 of 48, in one reported setting — could be pruned with only a modest quality drop. The consistent interpretation is not that heads are useless but that training overprovisions them: the redundancy seems to help optimization even when the final function does not need it, an echo of the lottery-ticket-style findings from the regularization chapter.

✎Note

A curiosity: attention sinks. Inspect almost any trained decoder and you will find many heads dumping most of their weight on the very first token — often a beginning-of-sequence (BOS) token — regardless of content. Why? Softmax must distribute exactly 1.0 of attention somewhere; a head that has nothing useful to retrieve for the current token still has to put its weight somewhere, and the first position, visible from everywhere under the causal mask, becomes the default parking spot — a “no-op” reading. The pattern was named the attention sink by Xiao et al. (2023), who showed that keeping the first few tokens’ keys and values around is essential when sliding-window inference evicts old context — evict the sink and generation quality collapses. Descendants of this observation include dedicated learned sink parameters in some 2024–2026 open models. It is an example of a mechanism nobody designed, discovered years after the architecture froze.

⚙️In practice

Two regularities jump out. First, per-head dimension barely moves: dkd_kdk​ has stayed at 64–128 across a five-order-of-magnitude range of model sizes. Models grow by adding heads (and layers), not by widening heads — empirically, 64–128 dimensions is enough room for one head’s “question,” and the ecosystem has settled there hard enough that FlashAttention kernels are tuned around these sizes. Second, since roughly 2023 the KV-heads column stopped matching the query-heads column: Llama 3, Mistral, Qwen, Gemma and most other open models use grouped-query attention with 4–8× fewer key/value heads than query heads, and DeepSeek’s V2/V3 line compresses keys and values further still with multi-head latent attention (MLA). Frontier labs do not publish closed-model configurations, but the memory arithmetic of serving (next section) makes some form of KV sharing close to universal. The number of heads itself is a mild hyperparameter: within reason, quality tracks total width h⋅dkh \cdot d_kh⋅dk​ more than the split, which is part of why the redundancy findings above were possible.

Sharing keys and values: the door to MQA and GQA

One more idea belongs in this chapter because it is a modification of the head structure itself, even though its payoff — inference memory — is the next-but-three chapter’s subject.

During generation, a model caches every past token’s keys and values (the KV cache) so it does not recompute them at each step. With standard MHA, every head stores its own KKK and VVV: for Llama-2-7B, that is 32 heads × 128 dims × 2 (K and V) × 32 layers ≈ 262,144 cached numbers per token — at 16-bit precision, roughly half a megabyte per token of context, which at long context lengths dwarfs the space taken by the weights themselves.

Shazeer (2019) noticed that the queries are what make heads different, much more than the keys and values, and proposed multi-query attention (MQA): keep hhh distinct query heads, but share a single K and V projection across all of them. Every head still asks its own question — but of one shared “library” rather than hhh private ones. The KV cache shrinks by a factor of hhh, at some quality cost. Ainslie et al. (2023) split the difference with grouped-query attention (GQA): divide the query heads into groups (say, 32 query heads in 8 groups of 4), with one K/V head per group — most of the memory savings, nearly none of the quality loss, and you can even convert a trained MHA checkpoint by averaging its K/V heads within each group. This is the “8 (GQA)” appearing throughout the table above.

The full treatment — cache arithmetic, MLA’s low-rank compression, sliding windows, FlashAttention — lives in the KV cache and efficient attention chapter. For now the takeaway is architectural: the per-head structure you learned in this chapter is not sacred. Queries, keys, and values can be tied, grouped, and compressed independently, and modern architectures treat “how many of each kind of head” as three separate dials.

Where this leaves us

  • A single attention head produces one softmax, one mixing pattern per token — too little for language, where a token like “them” simultaneously needs coreference, syntax, and local context.
  • Multi-head attention runs hhh small attention operations in parallel, each projecting the residual stream into its own dk=d/hd_k = d/hdk​=d/h-dimensional subspace — same total parameter count (4d24d^24d2) as one full-width head, but hhh independent patterns instead of one.
  • You can trace every shape: for d=512,h=8d = 512, h = 8d=512,h=8, each head maps T×512→T×64T \times 512 \to T \times 64T×512→T×64, computes its own T×TT \times TT×T pattern, and the concatenated T×512T \times 512T×512 result is mixed by WOW_OWO​.
  • In implementation, “multiple heads” is one big matmul plus a reshape — the head axis is just a batch dimension, and the structure adds no compute.
  • WOW_OWO​ is equivalent to giving each head its own dk×dd_k \times ddk​×d write channel into the residual stream; head contributions are purely additive, which is what makes per-head interpretability analysis possible at all.
  • Trained heads specialize — previous-token, positional, syntactic, and induction heads are real and reproducible — but many heads defy clean stories, and pruning studies show substantial redundancy.
  • Real models keep dkd_kdk​ at 64–128 and scale by adding heads; since ~2023, most share K/V across query-head groups (GQA) to shrink the KV cache.
