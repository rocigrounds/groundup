Pretraining Data

Where trillions of training tokens actually come from, and the filtering, deduplication, mixing, and annealing pipeline that turns raw web crawl into a model's entire world.

Every chapter so far has treated the training data as a given — “trillions of tokens of text” — while we studied the machine that consumes it. This chapter flips the camera around. It is arguably the most consequential chapter in this part of the book, because of an uncomfortable truth the field took years to internalize: the data is the model. Two labs training the same architecture with the same optimizer on the same budget will get meaningfully different models if their data pipelines differ — and the pipeline differences that matter are rarely glamorous. They are HTML boilerplate stripping, duplicate detection, and the exact ratio of code to web text.

There is a reason this knowledge stayed folk wisdom for so long. Architectures get papers; data pipelines get a paragraph in an appendix (“we train on a filtered web corpus”). The open-source community changed that between 2023 and 2025 by doing the unglamorous work in public — RefinedWeb, FineWeb, DataComp-LM — running controlled ablations at every pipeline stage and publishing what moved the needle. The result is that data curation is now a measurable engineering discipline with known best practices, and this chapter can teach it as one.

The plan: first, where trillions of tokens physically come from (mostly one nonprofit’s web crawl, which deserves demystifying). Then the processing pipeline stage by stage, with the why at each stage. Then deduplication in real depth, because it has outsized effects on both quality and memorization and because the algorithm behind it — MinHash — is a piece of math you can verify with a pencil. Then the questions that dominate frontier practice in 2026: how to mix data sources, why training runs end with an “annealing” phase on the best data, how synthetic data went from taboo to central, and whether the internet is running out.

Where trillions of tokens come from

Start with the number to beat. Frontier pretraining runs in 2025–2026 consume roughly 15 to 40 trillion tokens: Llama 3 used about 15T (Grattafiori et al., 2024), DeepSeek-V3 14.8T (DeepSeek-AI, 2024), Qwen2.5 18T and Qwen3 about 36T (all figures approximate and from the labs’ own reports). Recall from tokenization that a token is roughly three quarters of an English word, so 15T tokens is on the order of 11 trillion words — about what you would get from 100 million novels. No library holds that. Only one source does: the web.

Common Crawl is where most of it comes from, so it is worth knowing what Common Crawl is. It is a small nonprofit (founded 2007) that operates a polite web crawler — it respects robots.txt, the file where sites declare what crawlers may fetch — and publishes the result free, several times a year. Each crawl snapshot captures on the order of billions of web pages and hundreds of terabytes; there are now around a hundred snapshots totaling petabytes. The data comes in three formats, and the distinction matters more than you would expect:

  • WARC (Web ARChive) files: the raw HTTP responses — the full HTML of every page, headers and all.
  • WAT files: extracted metadata (links, titles).
  • WET files: Common Crawl’s own plain-text extraction of each page.

Early datasets used the convenient WET files. Modern pipelines do not, because Common Crawl’s text extraction is crude — it keeps navigation menus, cookie banners, footer links, and ad text, which means the “text” of a recipe page is mostly the words “Home About Subscribe Accept Cookies” repeated with slight variations across millions of pages. The RefinedWeb project (Penedo et al., 2023) demonstrated, and FineWeb confirmed with ablations, that going back to the raw WARC HTML and re-extracting the main content yourself produces measurably better models from the same crawl. That single choice — WARC over WET — is a good emblem of this whole chapter: an invisible plumbing decision, worth real benchmark points.

The web is the bulk, but not the whole diet. The other food groups:

Code. Scraped from GitHub and other forges. The open reference is The Stack (2022): about 3 TB of permissively licensed source code across hundreds of languages, later expanded enormously in The Stack v2. Code is deduplicated and filtered much like web text (minus star-count heuristics and file-size limits), and — as we will see — it earns a far larger share of the training mix than its share of the internet would suggest.

Books. High-quality long-form prose with real narrative and argumentative structure — precious for teaching long-range coherence, and the most legally fraught category (more on that at the end of the chapter).

Academic papers. arXiv, PubMed Central, and similar archives: dense, correct, LaTeX-flavored technical text. The Pile made these a standard ingredient in 2020.

Forums and Q&A. Stack Exchange, Reddit threads, mailing lists: dialogue, question–answer structure, and argument — the closest thing raw pretraining data has to the assistant behavior we eventually want.

Encyclopedias and reference. Wikipedia above all: small (a few billion tokens in English) but so reliably high quality that pipelines often upweight it.

Licensed corpora. Increasingly, labs pay: news archives, stock photo captions, publisher back-catalogs, Reddit’s data firehose. These deals are mostly private, and their contents rarely appear in open datasets.

💡Intuition

A helpful mental model: pretraining data is the model’s entire sensory history. The model will never “know” anything that is not either in this corpus or derivable from it, and it will faithfully absorb whatever statistical habits the corpus has — including its boilerplate, its spam, and its thousand copies of the same viral post. Curation is not a purity ritual; it is deciding what kind of mind you are growing. That is why labs now put senior engineers on pipelines that, from the outside, look like glorified spam filtering.

The pipeline, stage by stage

Every serious pipeline — open or frontier — runs some version of the same stages in roughly the same order. The figure below shows the flow; the subsections give the why for each stage.

Extraction: getting the article out of the page

An HTML page is mostly not its content. Templates, navigation, sidebars, cookie-consent walls, “related articles” widgets, and comment-section scaffolding typically outweigh the text a human came for. Extraction tools like trafilatura parse the HTML tree and use cues — tag types, link density, text-block length, position in the page — to find the main content and discard the rest. The why: boilerplate is catastrophically repetitive. Left in, the same navigation strings appear billions of times, and the model spends capacity learning that “Accept All Cookies” follows “We Value Your Privacy” with probability near 1. FineWeb’s ablations show pipelines built on proper re-extraction from WARC beat WET-based pipelines across benchmarks with everything else held fixed.

Language identification

Next, a fast classifier — the standard is fastText’s language identifier, a tiny model from a pre-transformer era that classifies among 176 languages at millions of documents per hour — labels each document, and the pipeline keeps the languages the lab intends to train on, above some confidence threshold (FineWeb keeps English with score ≥0.65\geq 0.65≥0.65). The why is partly focus and partly measurement: a language you keep is a language you must be able to filter and evaluate, and quality heuristics tuned for English (stopword lists, punctuation conventions) misfire badly on Thai or Tamil. Multilingual pipelines exist and are increasingly important — FineWeb-2 extends the recipe to over a thousand languages — but each language needs its own tuning.

Quality filtering: heuristics, then classifiers

“Quality” filtering removes documents that are technically language but not text anyone would learn from: search-engine-optimization (SEO) spam, auto-generated pages, lists of part numbers, gibberish from broken encodings. Two generations of tools:

Heuristic rules (the C4/Gopher era). C4 (2019) kept only lines ending in terminal punctuation, dropped pages with fewer than three sentences, removed anything containing “lorem ipsum” or a curly brace (a blunt attempt to exclude code), and dropped pages containing any word on a public “bad words” list. Gopher’s rules (Rae et al., 2021) are the mature version: document length between 50 and 100,000 words, mean word length between 3 and 10 characters, at most 90% of lines starting with a bullet, a minimum fraction of common English stopwords, symbol-to-word ratio caps. These rules are cheap, transparent, and still used — FineWeb applies variants of them — but they are crude proxies for “would a person learn from this?”

Model-based classifiers (the 2024-onward era). Train a small, fast model to score documents, and keep the high scorers. Two case studies define current practice. DCLM trained a fastText classifier whose positive examples were instruction-following data and highly upvoted Reddit explanations, and found this single choice mattered more than any heuristic in their large ablation grid — their filtered 3.8T-token DCLM-Baseline trains substantially stronger models than heuristic-only pipelines at equal compute. FineWeb-Edu took the idea further with a two-stage distillation: prompt Llama-3-70B-Instruct to rate about half a million web pages from 0 to 5 for educational value, then train a small embedding-based regressor on those ratings, then run the cheap regressor over all 15T tokens of FineWeb and keep pages scoring 3 or above. The surviving 1.3T tokens train models with markedly better knowledge-and-reasoning benchmark scores (MMLU, ARC) than the full 15T at the same training budget — demonstrating that for some capabilities, aggressive filtering beats more data.

💡Intuition

Model-based filtering is a bootstrap: use a strong existing model’s judgment to label a sample, distill that judgment into a classifier cheap enough to run on petabytes, and let it curate the next model’s diet. The deep question — what should count as “high quality”? — does not go away; it moves into the prompt given to the labeling model. FineWeb-Edu’s choice of “educational value” is a value judgment with measurable consequences: great for MMLU-style knowledge, and by the authors’ own account it trims away some conversational and creative text that a chat model also needs. There is no neutral filter.

⚠️Careful

Filters encode their authors’ blind spots. C4’s bad-words filter is the canonical cautionary tale: an audit by Dodge et al. (“Documenting Large Webtext Corpora”, 2021) found it disproportionately removed non-toxic text about and by minority groups — the word list could not distinguish slurs from reclaimed usage or clinical discussion, and it silently filtered dialects and identities out of the training distribution. Modern pipelines lean on URL-level blocklists of known adult/spam domains and on trained toxicity classifiers instead of raw word lists, but the general lesson stands: every filter is a distributional choice about whose text the model learns, and it should be audited, not assumed benign.

Safety and PII

Finally, the pipeline scrubs what should not be memorized at all. Personally identifiable information (PII) — email addresses, phone numbers, IP addresses — is found with regular expressions and replaced with placeholder strings (FineWeb anonymizes emails and IPs this way). Domains known for adult content, malware, or doxxing are removed by blocklist. This is defense in depth against the memorization problem we will quantify in a moment: the surest way to keep a model from reciting someone’s phone number is for the number to appear zero times in the corpus.

Deduplication: the highest-leverage boring step

The raw web is staggeringly redundant. The same press release syndicated to hundreds of outlets; the same product description across thousands of storefront pages; the same license text atop millions of code files; mirror sites; scraped copies of scraped copies. Lee et al. (2021) found a single 61-word English sentence — spam boilerplate — repeated over 60,000 times in C4, a dataset that had already been “cleaned.”

Why care? Two reasons, both large.

Quality. A duplicated document is an implicit upweighting the pipeline never chose: 60,000 copies means the model effectively trains 60,000 epochs on that sentence. Duplicates waste compute on gradient steps that teach nothing new, and empirically, deduplicated data trains better models — Lee et al. showed equal-or-better perplexity with fewer steps, and every subsequent pipeline ablation (RefinedWeb, FineWeb, DCLM) has confirmed dedup as a top-tier intervention.

Memorization. As covered in regularization and generalization, models memorize training text verbatim, and the probability of extraction grows sharply with the number of times a sequence was seen (Carlini et al., 2022). Lee et al. measured the flip side: models trained on deduplicated data emitted memorized training text about 10× less often. Dedup is simultaneously a quality optimization, a privacy control, and — when you also deduplicate against benchmark test sets (decontamination) — the thing keeping your evaluation numbers honest.

Exact duplicates are easy: hash every document (or every fixed-length substring) and drop repeats. The hard problem is near-duplicates — the syndicated article with a different headline and footer, the boilerplate with one town name swapped. Comparing every pair of documents is out of the question: for NNN documents there are N(N−1)/2N(N-1)/2N(N−1)/2 pairs, and at N=109N = 10^9N=109 that is about 5×10175 \times 10^{17}5×1017 comparisons. The escape is MinHash, a 1997 algorithm by Andrei Broder (built for AltaVista, a search engine that predates Google) that finds near-duplicate pairs without ever comparing most pairs.

∑The math

Step 1 — documents become sets. Represent each document by its set of shingles: all overlapping runs of nnn consecutive words (nnn-grams). Similarity between documents AAA and BBB (now sets of shingles) is measured by the Jaccard index — the fraction of all their shingles that they share:

J(A,B)  =  ∣A∩B∣∣A∪B∣,J(A, B) \;=\; \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|},J(A,B)=∣A∪B∣∣A∩B∣​,

where ∣A∩B∣|A \cap B|∣A∩B∣ counts shingles in both documents and ∣A∪B∣|A \cup B|∣A∪B∣ counts shingles in either. J=1J = 1J=1 means identical shingle sets; J=0J = 0J=0 means nothing shared.

Define each document’s sketch under hhh as the minimum hash over its shingles: mh(A)=min⁡s∈Ah(s)m_h(A) = \min_{s \in A} h(s)mh​(A)=mins∈A​h(s). Now the key fact:

Step 3 — estimate by repetition. Use kkk independent hash functions, giving each document a length-kkk signature of minima. The fraction of matching positions between two signatures is an unbiased estimate of JJJ, with standard error J(1−J)/k\sqrt{J(1-J)/k}J(1−J)/k​. One piece remains: even with cheap signatures, we cannot compare all 5×10175 \times 10^{17}5×1017 pairs. Locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) finishes the job. Split each length-kkk signature into bbb bands of rrr entries each (so k=b⋅rk = b \cdot rk=b⋅r), and hash each band into a bucket table. Two documents become a candidate pair — actually compared — only if at least one entire band matches. A band of rrr entries matches with probability JrJ^rJr, so:

P(candidate pair)  =  1−(1−Jr)b.\P(\text{candidate pair}) \;=\; 1 - (1 - J^r)^b .P(candidate pair)=1−(1−Jr)b.

The pipeline never touches the vast ocean of dissimilar pairs. That is how a billion documents get near-deduplicated on an ordinary cluster.

⚙️In practice

A counterintuitive FineWeb finding: more dedup is not monotonically better. Deduplicating across all ~100 Common Crawl snapshots globally performed worse than deduplicating within each snapshot separately. The apparent reason: text that recurs across many snapshots over years skews toward stable, often higher-quality pages, and aggressive global dedup preferentially deletes it while leaving each snapshot’s one-off spam untouched. Dedup, like every stage, is a knob to ablate, not a virtue to maximize. Note also what dedup does not promise: labs deliberately upweight some sources (Wikipedia, curated math) by training multiple epochs on them — the point of dedup is that repetition should be a choice, not an accident of web plumbing.

🕰History

The arc is worth stating in one breath. 2019: clean the web with regexes (C4). 2020: the web is not enough — curate diverse expert sources (The Pile). 2023: actually, the web is enough if you extract and dedup it properly (RefinedWeb). 2024: better still, let models themselves judge the web’s quality (FineWeb-Edu, DCLM). 2025 onward: increasingly, write some of the data yourself (synthetic data, below). Each step was validated the same way — train matched models on matched budgets and compare — which is why this history reads like science rather than fashion.

Mixtures, curricula, and annealing

Filtering decides what is allowed in the corpus. The mixture decides how much of each source the model sees: the sampling weights over domains (web, code, math, multilingual, papers, forums) that determine what fraction of gradient steps each contributes. This is where labs differ most, and where the final model’s personality — is it a strong coder? weirdly good at chemistry? — is quietly set.

Two things make mixture-tuning hard. First, weights interact: adding code may help math reasoning but crowd out multilingual data. Second, you cannot afford to test at full scale. The universal answer is small-proxy experiments: train many small models (say, ~1B parameters on a few tens of billions of tokens each) on candidate mixtures, evaluate on a broad benchmark suite, and trust — with scaling-laws-style caution — that the ranking transfers to the big run. More automated variants exist: DoReMi trains a small proxy model that adaptively upweights whichever domain currently causes the most excess loss relative to a reference, producing weights that transferred to models 30× larger; “data mixing law” work fits parametric curves predicting loss as a function of mixture, letting you optimize the mixture on paper. But ordinary ablation grids remain the workhorse — the Llama 3 report describes exactly this: scaling-law experiments on candidate mixes before committing.

For a concrete anchor: Llama 3’s final reported pretraining mix was roughly 50% general knowledge (web), 25% math and reasoning, 17% code, and 8% multilingual tokens. Notice how far from “the natural distribution of the internet” that is — code and math are enormously upweighted relative to their share of the web.

Why so much code? Partly because users want coding assistants. But the better-documented reason is that code appears to help general reasoning: controlled studies (for example “To Code or Not To Code?”, Aryabumi et al., 2024) find that adding code to pretraining improves performance on natural-language reasoning and world-knowledge tasks, not only on programming benchmarks. The causal story — code is text where structure is explicit, long dependencies are exact, and sloppiness is punished — is plausible but not proven; treat “code teaches reasoning” as a well-replicated empirical correlation with a folk explanation.

The mixture also need not be constant in time. Modern runs use staged training (a coarse curriculum): begin with the broad, web-heavy mix, and shift composition as training proceeds — most dramatically at the very end, in what is now called annealing (or “mid-training” in some labs’ vocabulary). During the final few percent of training, while the learning rate decays toward zero, the mixture pivots hard toward the highest-quality data available: curated math, code, educational text, sometimes early instruction-style data.

Why should the order matter, if the model sees the same tokens overall? The mechanics of optimization supply a story: what a model retains most sharply is shaped by its final steps. At a high learning rate, each minibatch partially overwrites the last; as the learning rate decays, the parameters settle, and the data seen during settling gets, in effect, the last word. Annealing exploits this: spend the cheap, noisy bulk of training absorbing the breadth of the web, then let scarce, precious data dominate the low-noise endgame. There is also a practical bonus, popularized by MiniCPM’s warmup-stable-decay schedule and used at scale in Llama 3: because the decay phase is where quality data bites hardest, you can evaluate a candidate data source cheaply by annealing an existing checkpoint on it and measuring the benchmark delta — no full run required. (Details of schedules and checkpointing live in the training run.)

⚠️Careful

Annealing on “high-quality” data shades into a related, murkier practice: annealing on data that resembles benchmark distributions. Adding instruction-formatted or exam-style data at the end reliably lifts scores on MMLU-style evaluations. Whether that reflects real capability or targeted test preparation is exactly the kind of question benchmarks and how to read them exists to sharpen. When you see a small model with startling benchmark numbers, its annealing mix is the first place to look.

Synthetic data: writing the textbook yourself

Until about 2023, “training on model outputs” was considered mildly disreputable. Then phi-1 (“Textbooks Are All You Need”) trained a 1.3B-parameter model on roughly 7B tokens — of which about 1B were GPT-3.5-generated textbook-style explanations and exercises — and hit 50.6% pass@1 on HumanEval, beating models many times its size trained on raw scraped code. The lesson generalized fast, and by 2026 synthetic data is central in at least four forms:

Instruction and chat data. Post-training data (see SFT and RLHF) has been substantially model-generated since 2023 — models draft responses, models judge them, humans audit samples.

Math and code with verifiable filtering. This is the safest form, because generation is paired with a checker: generate many candidate solutions, keep only those whose final answer matches a known result or whose code passes unit tests. The filter means correctness does not depend on the generator being reliable — it need only be right sometimes. This is rejection sampling as a data pipeline, and it is a load-bearing part of the math/code corpora behind reasoning-focused models (a theme continued in reasoning training).

Rephrasing real data. Rather than generate from nothing, have a model rewrite noisy-but-informative web text into cleaner prose (“rephrase this forum thread as a clear explanation”). The Phi series leaned on this textbook-style transformation; several 2024–2025 web corpora (for example Nemotron-CC) ship rephrased variants of low-quality tiers alongside the originals. Grounding in a real source document constrains hallucination while upgrading the style.

Agentic trajectories. The newest frontier. Kimi K2 (Kimi K2: Open Agentic Intelligence, 2025) describes a pipeline that synthesized thousands of tools and simulated environments, generated multi-step tool-use episodes at scale, and kept only trajectories passing a large language model (LLM) judge’s quality rubric — because genuine logs of competent multi-step tool use barely exist on the web at any scale. Its ~15.5T pretraining tokens (approximate, per the report) also used rephrasing to stretch scarce high-quality knowledge text. When the desired behavior has no natural corpus, the corpus gets manufactured.

Which brings us to the worry with a memorable name. Model collapse (Shumailov et al., 2023, later a Nature cover) showed that recursively training generation n+1n{+}1n+1 only on generation nnn‘s outputs degrades models: the tails of the distribution — rare facts, rare styles — get truncated first (a model samples its own high-probability region, so low-probability truth gradually vanishes), and quality decays over generations. The result is real, replicated, and worth taking seriously as the web itself fills with model output.

But treat it honestly rather than apocalyptically. The collapse experiments model a specific regime: each generation replaces its training data with pure model output, unfiltered. Practical pipelines differ on every axis: they accumulate (new synthetic data joins retained real data, a regime where follow-up work — e.g. Gerstgrasser et al., 2024 — finds degradation largely stops); they filter (verifiers and judges keep only the best fraction, injecting selection signal a naive loop lacks); and they ground (rephrasing preserves the information content of real text). So far, so good: the strongest 2025–2026 models are also the heaviest synthetic-data users, with no collapse in sight. The honest summary is that naive self-cannibalism demonstrably fails, curated synthetic pipelines demonstrably work as of today, and nobody can yet rule out slower, subtler homogenization — of style, of framing, of which facts get repeated — as model text becomes a large fraction of everyone’s crawl.

Are we running out of data?

If frontier runs consume ~15–40T tokens and grow with each generation, does the supply run out? Villalobos et al. made the canonical estimate: the effective stock of public, human-generated text is on the order of 300 trillion tokens (their revised median; the error bars are wide), and under then-current scaling trends, frontier training would fully use it somewhere between 2026 and 2032. The headline “we’re running out of data” oversimplifies in both directions, so here is the more careful ledger.

Reasons the wall is softer than it sounds. First, repetition works: Muennighoff et al. (2023) showed that training up to ~4 epochs over the same data costs almost nothing versus fresh data, with value decaying to near zero only after roughly 16 epochs — an immediate ~4× multiplier on the effective stock. Second, compute-optimal scaling (next chapter, scaling laws) does not demand data grow as fast as the naive trend assumed, and post-Chinchilla practice spends more compute per token (bigger models, more epochs, reasoning-style reinforcement learning) rather than always demanding more tokens. Third, the estimate excludes audio and video transcripts, non-public archives, licensed corpora, and — the big one — synthetic data, which converts compute into tokens directly.

Reasons it is harder than it sounds. The binding constraint was never raw tokens but high-quality tokens: FineWeb-Edu-grade text is roughly a tenth of the web, and everything in this chapter says quality drives capability. Curated high-quality pools are already trained on for multiple epochs at the frontier. And the accessible web is shrinking in relative terms: a growing share of top sites now block AI crawlers via robots.txt or terms of service (the Data Provenance Initiative’s “Consent in Crisis” audit documented a rapid decline in what consented crawling can reach), while paywalls and login walls grow. The 2026 consensus, holding loosely: no hard wall imminent, but the era of free, abundant, high-quality human text is over, and the marginal token increasingly comes from licensing negotiations, repetition, or a GPU.

✏️Worked example

A back-of-envelope you can update yourself. Suppose a lab wants a 40T-token run and judges 12T tokens of its corpus “high quality.” At 4 near-free epochs (Muennighoff), the quality pool supplies 12×4=4812 \times 4 = 4812×4=48T effective tokens — enough on paper, with nothing to spare and every epoch beyond 4 paying diminishing returns. Now suppose the next generation wants 100T: the same pool must stretch 100/12≈8.3100/12 \approx 8.3100/12≈8.3 epochs deep into the diminishing-returns regime, and the gap must be filled by lower-quality web, licensing, or synthesis. This is the arithmetic behind every “data wall” headline — and behind every synthetic-data team’s budget.

The legal and normative ground

One honest paragraph, because the law here is live and this book is not legal advice. Pretraining on copyrighted text without permission is the subject of major ongoing litigation, and 2025 produced the first substantive rulings: in Bartz v. Anthropic, a federal judge held that training on lawfully acquired books was transformative fair use while retaining pirated copies was not — and the piracy exposure drove a reported $1.5B settlement; in Kadrey v. Meta, another judge ruled for Meta on the record presented while pointedly noting the outcome might differ with better evidence of market harm; The New York Times v. OpenAI and dozens of other suits remain pending, and Thomson Reuters v. Ross went against the AI developer in a non-generative context. Meanwhile the norms are shifting outside courtrooms: robots.txt — a voluntary convention from 1994 — has become the de facto opt-out mechanism, honored by major crawlers and asserted by an ever-larger share of publishers; licensing markets are forming (news archives, Reddit, stock media); and open datasets increasingly document provenance and honor opt-outs (The Stack filters by license and offers removal; books corpora of pirated origin, like the Books3 of early open models, have been dropped from reputable pipelines). The factual core is simple even where the law is unsettled: every pretraining corpus embodies choices about consent, compensation, and attribution, those choices now carry litigation-scale price tags, and pipeline provenance-tracking has gone from nicety to necessity.

✎Note

All of the case-law statements above are snapshots as of mid-2026 in the United States; appeals are pending, other jurisdictions (the EU’s text-and-data-mining exceptions, Japan’s permissive regime) differ substantially, and this area moves faster than any other in the chapter. Verify before relying.

Where this leaves us

  • You know where trillions of tokens come from: overwhelmingly Common Crawl’s raw WARC archives, re-extracted, plus code (The Stack lineage), books, papers, forums, and a growing licensed and synthetic tier.
  • You can walk the pipeline and say why each stage exists: extraction kills boilerplate repetition, language ID keeps the corpus measurable, heuristics and model-based classifiers (FineWeb-Edu, DCLM) operationalize “quality,” and PII scrubbing plus dedup limit memorization.
  • You can explain MinHash to a civilian and verify it with a pencil: shingles, the probability-equals-Jaccard trick, signatures, and the LSH S-curve 1−(1−Jr)b1 - (1 - J^r)^b1−(1−Jr)b that finds near-duplicate pairs among billions of documents without pairwise comparison.
  • You can read a data mixture (Llama 3’s ~50/25/17/8) as a set of deliberate bets, tuned by small-proxy experiments, and you know why runs end by annealing on the best data during learning-rate decay.
  • You can hold two true things about synthetic data at once: naive recursive training demonstrably collapses, and filtered, verified, grounded synthetic pipelines demonstrably power the strongest current models.
  • You can argue both sides of the “running out of data” debate with numbers: ~300T tokens of public human text, ~4 near-free epochs, and a quality pool an order of magnitude smaller than the raw one.

The corpus is chosen. The next question is brutally quantitative: given a fixed compute budget, how big should the model be and how many of these hard-won tokens should it see? That trade-off has a law. Next: Scaling Laws.
