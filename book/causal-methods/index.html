Causal Methods: Patching, Ablation, and Lenses

The experimental toolkit of interpretability — activation patching, ablations, and logit/tuned lenses — taught as a lab manual, with the metrics, controls, and honest caveats that separate evidence from illusion.

The last few chapters made claims that should have left you a little uneasy. We said a direction in activation space “represents” the concept of the Golden Gate Bridge. We said a sparse autoencoder latent “is” a feature for legal language. We said an attention head in a circuit “moves the name to the final position.” Every one of those claims used causal language — represents, is, moves — but much of the evidence behind such claims, at first pass, is correlational: this neuron fires when the concept appears, this probe can decode the concept from the activations, these examples maximally excite this latent.

Correlation is where every science starts and where bad science stops. A probe reaching 95% accuracy tells you the information is present in the activations; it does not tell you the model uses it, and Chapter 49 showed how those come apart. A neuron that fires on French text might cause the model’s French output — or it might be a passenger, downstream of whatever does the work, like a rooster taking credit for the sunrise.

The fix is the same one the rest of science uses: intervene. Reach into the running model, change one thing, and watch what happens to the output. If overwriting a single activation flips the model’s answer from “Rome” to “Paris,” you have learned something no probe could tell you. This chapter is the lab manual for those interventions: activation patching (the core protocol, with a full worked example), ablations (and the surprisingly subtle question of what “removing” a component even means), and the lenses that let you watch a prediction assemble itself layer by layer. It ends with the part most write-ups skip: how these methods fail, the illusions they generate, and the controls that separate a real finding from a compelling story.

Correlation, causation, and what an intervention buys you

Interpretability inherits a problem that observational sciences know well. You cannot randomly assign a transformer to a “treatment group.” But unlike epidemiologists, we hold a privilege no other science has: total, instant, reversible control over the system’s internal state. We can pause the model mid-computation, edit any activation to any value, and resume — a perfect surgical intervention, repeatable millions of times for pennies. The model never notices, never adapts, never files a complaint with the ethics board.

This privilege is why the field insists on causal evidence. The standard observational tools — probing classifiers, max-activating dataset examples, attention-weight visualizations — are hypothesis generators. They tell you where to look. The tools in this chapter are hypothesis testers. A claim like “head 9 in layer 5 copies the subject’s name” graduates from folklore to finding when an intervention on that head, and nothing else, changes the output in the predicted way.

💡Intuition

Think of the model as a suspect factory floor. Probes are security-camera footage: you can see which stations light up when a particular product ships, but a station can be busy without contributing anything. Patching is walking onto the floor and swapping the contents of one station’s conveyor belt with the contents from a different production run. If the product changes, that station mattered — and how the product changes tells you what the station was carrying.

Two words will organize everything that follows. A component is necessary for a behavior if breaking it breaks the behavior. It is sufficient (in the restoration sense used in this field) if restoring it — inside an otherwise broken run — restores the behavior. These are different claims, established by different experiments, and conflating them is the single most common methodological error in interpretability write-ups. Keep them separate and half the pitfalls in this chapter become easy to spot.

Activation patching: the core protocol

Activation patching (also called causal tracing in the paper that popularized it, Meng et al., 2022, and interchange intervention in the causal-abstraction literature) is the field’s workhorse. Here is the protocol, lab-manual style.

Step 0 — choose a prompt pair. You need two prompts that are identical in structure but differ in the one thing you care about, and that lead to different answers:

  • Clean prompt: “The Eiffel Tower is in” → the model says ” Paris”.
  • Corrupted prompt: “The Colosseum is in” → the model says ” Rome”.

The pair defines the question. Because only the subject differs, any activation whose transplantation moves the answer must carry information about the subject — the fact being recalled, or something downstream of it.

Step 1 — run the clean prompt and cache everything. One forward pass. At every layer ℓ\ellℓ and every token position ttt, store the residual-stream vector ht(ℓ)h^{(\ell)}_tht(ℓ)​ (and, if you want finer resolution, each attention head’s output and each multi-layer perceptron (MLP) block’s output — the taps you met in Chapter 49). For a 12-layer model on a 6-token prompt this is a few hundred vectors; for a frontier model it is gigabytes, which is why patching research mostly runs on small open models.

Step 2 — run the corrupted prompt and record the baseline. Note the output logits — in particular the logits for ” Paris” and ” Rome”.

Step 3 — patch, one site at a time. Run the corrupted prompt again, but at one chosen site (ℓ,t)(\ell, t)(ℓ,t), overwrite the activation with the cached clean value mid-forward-pass, and let the computation continue downstream through the modified state. Everything before the patch is pure corrupted-run; everything after is a hybrid.

Step 4 — measure recovery. How far did the output move from the corrupted answer back toward the clean answer? (The metric deserves its own section — below.) Repeat steps 3–4 for every site, and you have a heatmap over layers and positions.

⚠️Careful

Token alignment is a real constraint, not a detail. The clean and corrupted prompts must tokenize to the same length with the differing tokens in the same positions — otherwise “patch position 3” means different things in the two runs. “Eiffel Tower” and “Colosseum” may occupy different numbers of tokens depending on the tokenizer, so in practice you check alignment first and choose subjects (or padding) that line up. Meng et al. sidestepped this entirely: instead of a second prompt, they corrupted the clean prompt itself by adding Gaussian noise to the subject’s token embeddings, then patched clean activations back in. Same protocol, no alignment problem — at the cost of an unnatural corruption (more on that trade-off in the ablation section).

Reading the heatmap

The payoff of sweeping every site is a picture like the one below: layers on the horizontal axis, token positions on the vertical, each cell colored by recovery. This heatmap — from causal tracing on factual prompts — is one of the most replicated results in interpretability, and learning to read it teaches you the grammar of every patching plot you will ever see.

The two hotspots tell a story with a plot. Patching the residual stream at the subject tokens in early-to-mid layers restores the answer: that is where the model, having recognized “Eiffel Tower,” enriches the subject’s representation with associated facts — and component-level patching shows the mid-layer MLPs doing most of that work, which is why the field describes MLPs as key–value memories. Patching at the final position in late layers also restores the answer: by then the fact has been ferried — by attention heads, the only components that move information between positions — to the position that must produce ” Paris”. Patching “is”/“in” positions, or very late layers at the subject, recovers almost nothing: the information has already moved on. One sweep, and you have a where and when map of a memory being recalled.

One honest caveat before you over-trust such maps: localization by patching does not automatically mean that editing the model at that location is the right or only way to change the fact — later work found you can often edit facts successfully at layers causal tracing does not highlight (Hase et al., 2023). Patching tells you where information is causally readable in this forward pass, which is related to, but not identical with, where it is stored. We pick this thread back up in model editing.

Denoising vs noising: which direction proves what

You can run the patch in either direction, and the two directions answer different questions — this asymmetry is the conceptual heart of the method (Heimersheim & Nanda, 2024).

Denoising (clean → corrupted, what we did above): start from a broken run and restore one activation. Success shows the patched site is sufficient to restore the behavior, given everything else stays corrupted. It is a strong, specific claim: this one vector, dropped into a run that got everything else wrong, carried enough to fix the output.

Noising (corrupted → clean): start from a working run and break one activation. Success — the behavior degrading — shows the site is necessary: the working run could not do without it. Failure, however, proves less than you would hope, because of redundancy. Transformers routinely contain backup components that take over when a primary component is damaged — the indirect-object-identification (IOI) circuit’s “backup name-mover heads” activate precisely when the main name-movers are ablated, a phenomenon dubbed the Hydra effect (McGrath et al., 2023). Cut off one head and two grow back; the noising experiment then reports “not necessary” for a component that is, in the intact model, doing the work.

💡Intuition

Denoising asks: is this ingredient enough to rescue the dish? Noising asks: does removing this ingredient ruin the dish? A restaurant with a backup chef passes the noising test for “head chef” while the head chef still cooks every meal. When you read a patching result, first ask which direction was run — the paper’s claim should match its arrow.

⚠️Careful

A subtler trap: metrics computed on a single prompt pair. Any one pair confounds the variable you care about with everything else that differs between the prompts (token frequency, position, syntax). Serious patching studies average over a distribution of prompt pairs — dozens to thousands of subject/answer combinations — so that only the systematic effect survives.

Path patching and edge attribution

Vanilla patching intervenes on a node — everything downstream of the patched activation feels the change through every route. Often the hypothesis is sharper: “head 8.6 matters because it feeds head 9.9’s query, not through any other route.” Path patching, developed for the IOI circuit analysis (Wang et al., 2022), tests exactly this: the patched value of a sender component is allowed to propagate only along one chosen edge to one receiver, while every other path is frozen at its corrupted value (implemented by extra forward passes that carefully mix cached activations). The result is causal evidence at the resolution of edges in the computational graph — which is what you need to draw the circuit diagrams of Chapter 52 with a straight face, and what modern edge-attribution methods automate.

Attribution patching: patching at industrial scale

The full protocol needs one forward pass per site. A mid-sized model has millions of (component, position) sites; a sweep over prompt distributions multiplies that again. Attribution patching replaces the inner loop with calculus (Syed et al., 2023, building on Nanda’s 2023 write-up).

Let M(a)M(a)M(a) be the metric as a function of one activation aaa, holding the rest of the corrupted run fixed. Patching changes the metric by M(aclean)−M(acorr)M(a_{\text{clean}}) - M(a_{\text{corr}})M(aclean​)−M(acorr​). Take the first-order Taylor approximation around the corrupted value:

M(aclean)−M(acorr)  ≈  ∇aM∣acorr⋅(aclean−acorr).M(a_{\text{clean}}) - M(a_{\text{corr}}) \;\approx\; \nabla_a M \big|_{a_{\text{corr}}} \cdot \big(a_{\text{clean}} - a_{\text{corr}}\big).M(aclean​)−M(acorr​)≈∇a​M​acorr​​⋅(aclean​−acorr​).

Running the real patch might give 0.55 or 0.7 — the estimate is a linearization, exact only if MMM were linear in aaa over that gap.

The approximation is good when the activation change is small or the downstream function is locally linear — which holds surprisingly often for individual attention heads — and bad where nonlinearities saturate: LayerNorm’s normalization, attention softmax near one-hot, and large residual-stream patches in early layers are the known failure zones. The 2026 workflow treats attribution patching as a screen: rank all sites by estimated effect in one cheap pass, then verify the top candidates with real activation patching. Edge-level variants (edge attribution patching — EAP — and gradient-refined versions like EAP with integrated gradients) are what make automated circuit discovery tractable, and the same trick underlies the attribution graphs of frontier interpretability.

Ablation: what does “removing” a component even mean?

Patching swaps information between two specific runs. Ablation asks a blunter question — what happens if this component contributes nothing? — and immediately runs into a philosophical pothole: what value represents “nothing”?

Zero ablation sets the component’s output to the zero vector. It feels like the obvious choice and it is usually the wrong one. Nothing in training ever asked the network to treat zero as neutral: a component’s output may hover in a cloud far from the origin, with downstream LayerNorms and biases calibrated to inputs in that cloud. Zeroing it feeds downstream components an input unlike anything seen in training — you are not measuring “the model without this component,” you are measuring “the model kicked off-distribution at this spot.” Damage you observe may be shock, not lost function.

Mean ablation replaces the output with its average over a reference dataset. The component still says something — it says the least informative thing it typically says, staying inside its usual operating range while erasing its input-specific content. This isolates the component’s variation (its actual information) from its constant offset (which mean ablation deliberately preserves).

Resample ablation replaces the output with its value from a different random input drawn from the same distribution. This is the most principled “nothing”: the component emits a perfectly natural output — real, on-distribution, internally coherent — that happens to be irrelevant to the current input. Note that patching is resample ablation with a chosen donor input rather than a random one; the methods form one family.

⚙️In practice

Defaults in 2026 tooling: resample or mean ablation over a task distribution for circuit claims; zero ablation survives mainly for quick sanity checks and for sparse autoencoder (SAE) latents, where zero is a value the latent takes (a sparse latent is off — exactly zero — most of the time, so zeroing it is on-distribution in a way that zeroing a neuron or head is not). When you see “we ablated head X and accuracy dropped 20%,” your first question should be: ablated to what, and over which distribution? Papers that do not say are papers to trust less.

Ablations map onto the necessity/sufficiency frame directly: an ablation is a noising experiment (“break this, does behavior break?” — necessity, modulo Hydra-effect backups), while patching a component into an otherwise-ablated or corrupted model tests sufficiency. A complete circuit claim needs both: the proposed components together are sufficient (model works with only them intact, everything else mean-ablated) and the important ones are necessary (ablating them hurts).

Causal scrubbing, in one paragraph

The most demanding version of “both” is causal scrubbing (Chan et al., 2022): take your hypothesis seriously as a formal claim about which activations matter and which distinctions between inputs they are allowed to depend on — then resample-ablate everything the hypothesis says should not matter, all at once, using donor inputs the hypothesis claims are equivalent. If your story says head 7 only computes “is the previous token a name?”, then its activation on this prompt may be swapped with its activation on any prompt whose previous token is also a name. Run the model under this aggressive scrub and measure how much task performance survives; the fraction recovered is the hypothesis’s faithfulness score. It is the field’s closest thing to a falsifiable end-to-end test — and its results have been humbling: many published circuits recover well under gentle patching but lose meaningful performance under a full scrub, which tells you the tidy story is at best incomplete.

Lenses: watching the answer form over depth

Patching tells you where information matters. Lenses answer a different question: what is the model’s best guess so far? They exploit a fact you met in Chapter 16: the residual stream is an accumulating sum — every layer reads from it and adds its contribution back — and the final prediction is read off with one linear map (the unembedding WUW_UWU​) applied to the final state. Nothing stops you from applying that same map early.

The logit lens (nostalgebraist, 2020) does exactly this: take the residual stream h(ℓ)h^{(\ell)}h(ℓ) after layer ℓ\ellℓ, apply the final LayerNorm and the unembedding, z(ℓ)=WU LN(h(ℓ))z^{(\ell)} = W_U\,\mathrm{LN}(h^{(\ell)})z(ℓ)=WU​LN(h(ℓ)), and read out the top tokens — the model’s “current draft” of its prediction. Applied at every layer, you watch the answer develop like a photograph. Here is the characteristic pattern for a factual prompt, shown for the final position of “The Eiffel Tower is located in the city of” (illustrative of a GPT-2-small-scale model — the qualitative shape is what replicates, not these exact tokens):

LayerTop-1 tokenRunner-upsReading
0–2” the”” a”, ”,”Generic frequent tokens; no task signal yet
4” London”” the”, ” Rome”“A city name goes here” — right category, wrong instance
6” Paris”” London”, ” France”The fact surfaces, weakly
8” Paris”” France”, ” Par”Confident; France competes (wrong type, right topic)
11” Paris”” France”, ” the”Final answer, unchanged since layer 8

Two lessons hide in that table. First, predictions form gradually and monotonically-ish — mid layers know the answer’s type before its identity, matching the patching heatmap’s story that mid-layer MLPs at the subject inject the fact which late layers then use. Second, the last few layers often change little, which is the observation behind early-exit inference schemes: if layer 8’s draft equals layer 11’s answer, you could have stopped computing — and indeed the tuned-lens authors frame lenses and early-exit as the same question (“when is the prediction ready?”) asked for different purposes.

⚠️Careful

The logit lens can lie to you. It assumes each layer’s residual stream is written in the same “language” the final unembedding reads — that the representation does not drift across depth. In GPT-2 the assumption holds tolerably; in other families it collapses. The tuned-lens paper (Belrose et al., 2023) showed that on models like GPT-Neo and BLOOM, the raw logit lens outputs near-garbage for most of the network — not because those models lack intermediate predictions, but because their intermediate states are affinely rotated and scaled relative to the final basis, and WUW_UWU​ reads them in the wrong coordinates. An empty lens reading is evidence about the lens, not the model.

The tuned lens repairs this with the obvious fix: instead of reusing WUW_UWU​ raw, learn a small per-layer affine correction — a translator A(ℓ)h+b(ℓ)A^{(\ell)} h + b^{(\ell)}A(ℓ)h+b(ℓ) — trained so that the translated layer-ℓ\ellℓ state, pushed through the frozen unembedding, matches the model’s own final distribution as closely as possible (minimizing KL, with the rest of the model untouched). Each translator is a learned change of coordinates, nothing more; if the logit lens needed no correction, the translator would learn the identity. Tuned-lens drafts are dramatically more accurate and better calibrated at every depth, and the failure cases of the raw lens vanish.

One caution applies to both lenses: they are observational, not causal — a lens reading is a correlation between an intermediate state and a linear readout, right back on the wrong side of this chapter’s dividing line. The lens tells you a draft answer is decodable at layer 8; patching tells you whether the layer-8 state actually drives the output. Use lenses to form hypotheses cheaply, patching to test them. (The tuned lens partially bridges the gap: its authors validated that tuned-lens drafts track causally relevant features under interventions — but the validation was the intervention, not the lens.)

Steering as an experiment

There is a fourth intervention that doubles as a product feature: add a vector instead of swapping one. If you believe direction vvv means “sycophancy,” add αv\alpha vαv to the residual stream and see whether the model starts flattering you; subtract it and see whether flattery stops. Where patching transplants naturally occurring states, steering synthesizes new ones — which makes success a strong form of causal evidence for the meaning of a direction: the direction is sufficient to induce the behavior in contexts where it never naturally arose. Golden Gate Claude was exactly this experiment run at frontier scale. Steering, its variants, and its cousins in weight-space (model editing, including ROME — the editing half of the causal-tracing paper) get their full treatment in the next chapter.

The methodology-honesty section

Interpretability is a young science, and young sciences earn trust by developing an immune system against their own enthusiasm. This section is that immune system, and it is as much a part of the toolkit as any patch.

The cautionary tale the field grew up on. In the late 2010s, saliency maps — heatmaps claiming to show which input pixels a vision model relied on — were everywhere. Then Adebayo et al. (2018) ran a sanity check that should have been run first: randomize the model’s weights and regenerate the map. Several popular methods produced nearly identical maps for the trained and the randomized network — they were functioning as fancy edge detectors on the input, explaining nothing about the model. An entire literature of compelling visualizations, invalidated by one control experiment. Mechanistic interpretability defines itself against this failure mode, which is precisely why the field insists on interventions — but interventions are not immune to their own illusions.

Interp illusions are real and documented. Bolukbasi et al. (2021) studied BERT neurons that looked monosemantic — every top-activating example on one dataset shared an obvious concept — and then showed that the same neuron’s top examples on a different dataset shared a different obvious concept. The “meaning” was a property of the dataset lens, not the neuron. The general pattern: any method that summarizes a high-dimensional object through a low-dimensional window (top examples, one metric, one prompt distribution) can manufacture coherence that is not there. Superposition makes this worse, since single neurons do host multiple features.

The controls that catch illusions. A short checklist that recurs in careful 2026 work:

  • Random-direction baselines. Before celebrating that ablating your feature direction changed behavior, ablate a random direction of the same norm at the same site. High-dimensional interventions have generic effects; your direction must beat the generic effect, not zero.
  • Shuffled-patch and unrelated-donor controls. Patch activations from an unrelated clean prompt. If “The Great Wall is in” restores “Paris” as well as the Eiffel prompt does, your patch is restoring “a landmark is being discussed,” not the specific fact.
  • Multiple metrics. Report logit diff and KL (and accuracy over a distribution). A finding that only appears under one metric is a finding about the metric.
  • Held-out validation. Localize your circuit on one set of prompts, verify on a disjoint set. The patching sweep is a search over thousands of hypotheses; without held-out data you are reading tea leaves you yourself arranged — the same generalization discipline that applies to training models applies to explaining them.

Replication and pre-registration. Several celebrated early findings have survived replication well (causal tracing’s heatmap shape, induction heads); others shrank on contact — circuits that explained less of the behavior than claimed once causal scrubbing was applied, or that turned out specific to one model seed or size. In response, a modest pre-registration culture has been emerging: stating the hypothesis and the pass/fail criterion before running the sweep, publishing negative results, and standardizing tasks and metrics so results compare across papers. It is uneven — this is a fast-moving field with blog-post publication norms — but the direction of travel is toward the boring virtues, and papers like the activation-patching best-practices guide exist precisely to codify them.

✎Note

Honest summary of where the field stands in 2026: patching-style interventions are the most trusted tool in interpretability, and every one of them intervenes on states the model produces off its training distribution to some degree, reads results through imperfect metrics, and tests hypotheses selected by the same researcher who ran the sweep. The methods in this chapter are the best available standard of evidence — a real and rising standard — not a guarantee. Treat any single patching heatmap the way you would treat any single biology experiment: suggestive until controlled, replicated, and stress-tested.

Everything in this chapter is runnable on a free Colab GPU (or a laptop) with mature open-source tooling:

  • TransformerLens — the standard library for hook-based interpretability on open models (GPT-2, Pythia, Llama-class). Loads a model with named hook points at every activation you saw in this chapter; caching and patching are a few lines.
  • nnsight — intervention-first framework that scales the same ideas to larger models, including remote execution on models too big for your hardware, via the National Deep Inference Fabric (NDIF) research infrastructure.
  • pyvene — Stanford’s library for composable interventions, built around the causal-abstraction framing (interchange interventions as first-class objects).
  • Neuronpedia — browser-based: dashboards for SAE features across open models, with search and steering built in. The fastest way to look at features before writing any code.
✏️Worked example

A first experiment, as a recipe. (1) Install TransformerLens; load GPT-2-small (124M parameters — small enough that a full patching sweep takes seconds). (2) Pick the prompt pair from this chapter — “The Eiffel Tower is in” / “The Colosseum is in” — and check both tokenize to equal length; adjust subjects if not. (3) Run both prompts with run_with_cache, and record the clean and corrupted logit differences for ” Paris” vs ” Rome”. (4) Loop over layers and positions, patching the cached clean residual stream into the corrupted run with a hook, and compute normalized recovery at each site. (5) Plot the heatmap and compare it with the schematic figure above. Then do it again patching only MLP outputs, then only attention outputs, and watch the two-hotspot story separate into its components. The ARENA (Alignment Research Engineer Accelerator) program’s interpretability track has a polished, exercise-driven version of exactly this notebook (activation patching, path patching, and the IOI circuit), and it is the single best on-ramp from this chapter to doing the work yourself — where to go next maps the paths beyond it.

Where this leaves us

  • You can now state the field’s central methodological rule: probes and max-activating examples show information is present; only interventions show it is used — and you know why the distinction bites (passenger neurons, unused directions, dataset-lens illusions).
  • You can run the activation patching protocol from memory — clean run, cache; corrupted run, baseline; patch one site; measure normalized logit-difference recovery — and read a patching heatmap’s grammar: where in layers, when in positions, fact retrieval at the subject, answer assembly at the end.
  • You can say what each experimental direction proves: denoising → sufficiency to restore; noising → necessity, weakened by backup components; and why logit difference beats raw probability as a metric.
  • You can choose an ablation and defend it: zero (off-distribution, avoid), mean (erases variation, keeps the operating point), resample (the principled “irrelevant but natural” baseline) — and you know causal scrubbing as the everything-at-once version.
  • You can use the logit lens to watch a prediction form across depth, say exactly when it misleads (representational drift; GPT-Neo/BLOOM), and explain the tuned lens’s per-layer affine fix — while remembering that lenses are observational and patching is what tests their stories.
  • You can list the controls that separate findings from illusions: random directions, unrelated donors, multiple metrics, held-out prompts — and cite the saliency-map episode as the reason they are non-negotiable.
  • You know the tools (TransformerLens, nnsight, pyvene, Neuronpedia) and have a concrete first experiment to run tonight.

Patching transplants activations the model produced on its own. The obvious next question is whether we can write activations of our choosing — amplify a feature, delete a fact, steer a behavior — and what happens when we do: Next: Steering and Model Editing.

Key papers & readings in this chapter
  • Locating and Editing Factual Associations in GPT — Meng et al., 2022 Introduced causal tracing — the Eiffel-Tower-style patching experiment this chapter walks through. Section 2 is the method.
  • How to Use and Interpret Activation Patching — Heimersheim & Nanda, 2024 The best-practices manual: denoising vs noising, metric choice, and the standard pitfalls. Read alongside this chapter.
  • Attribution Patching Outperforms Automated Circuit Discovery — Syed et al., 2023 The gradient approximation that turns millions of patching runs into two forward passes and one backward pass.
  • interpreting GPT: the logit lens — nostalgebraist, 2020 The blog post that started reading the residual stream mid-network. Short and worth reading in full.
  • Eliciting Latent Predictions from Transformers with the Tuned Lens — Belrose et al., 2023 Fixes the logit lens with learned per-layer probes; Figures 1–2 show exactly where the logit lens fails.
  • Causal Scrubbing: a method for rigorously testing interpretability hypotheses — Chan et al., 2022 The most demanding standard of evidence in the field: replace everything your hypothesis says should not matter, and see if behavior survives.
  • An Interpretability Illusion for BERT — Bolukbasi et al., 2021 The canonical demonstration that a neuron can look monosemantic on one dataset and mean something else on another.
  • Sanity Checks for Saliency Maps — Adebayo et al., 2018 The cautionary tale: popular explanation methods that barely change when you randomize the model they claim to explain.
  • Interpretability in the Wild: a Circuit for Indirect Object Identification in GPT-2 small — Wang et al., 2022 Where path patching was developed; Section 3 defines the method this chapter sketches.
