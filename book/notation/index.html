Mathematical notation is a compression scheme. A page of symbols can hold what would take ten pages of English — but only if you own the codebook. Readers who taught themselves this material rarely get stuck on the ideas; they get stuck on a line of symbols they cannot pronounce, and pronunciation is most of the battle. An equation you can say aloud in plain English is an equation you can think about.

This chapter is the codebook. It is a reference, not a lesson: skim it once so you know what lives here, then come back whenever a symbol stops you. Every table has a read it aloud column — the exact words to say in your head — and every cluster of notation links back to the chapter that teaches it properly. Nothing here is new; everything here is collected.

💡Intuition

The single habit that makes dense papers readable: translate symbols to speech before trying to understand them. "∇θL\nabla_\theta L∇θ​L" is opaque as a shape on the page; “the gradient of the loss with respect to the parameters” is a sentence with a subject and a meaning. Every table below exists to make that translation automatic.

Typography and shapes

The book uses typography to tell you what kind of object a symbol is before you know anything else about it. This is the convention throughout modern deep learning papers as well, though papers are less consistent than they should be.

WrittenRead it aloudWhat it is
xxx, aaa, η\etaη“x”A scalar — one plain number. Lowercase italic.
x\mathbf{x}x, v\mathbf{v}v“the vector x”A vector — an ordered list of numbers. Bold lowercase.
WWW, QQQ, HHH“the matrix W”A matrix — a grid of numbers. Capital letter.
XXX“the matrix X”A data or activation matrix — many vectors stacked as rows.
R\RR“the real numbers”The set of all real numbers.
Rd\R^dRd“R d” or “d-dimensional space”The set of all vectors with ddd real entries.
x∈Rd\mathbf{x} \in \R^dx∈Rd“x is a vector in R d”Declares the type and size of x\mathbf{x}x.
[n×d][n \times d][n×d]“n by d”A shape: nnn rows, ddd columns.

Shapes are the load-bearing part. When the book writes that an activation matrix XXX has shape [n×d][n \times d][n×d], it means nnn rows and ddd columns — rows first, always. In code the same object is a tensor of shape (n, d), or (B, n, d) with a batch dimension BBB in front, and the convention carries over unchanged.

The book’s most important structural convention, used from attention onward:

  • Tokens are rows. A sequence of nnn tokens, each represented by a ddd-dimensional vector, is a matrix XXX of shape [n×d][n \times d][n×d]: token 1 is row 1, token 2 is row 2, and so on.
  • Weight matrices multiply from the right. A layer that maps dind_{\text{in}}din​-dimensional vectors to doutd_{\text{out}}dout​-dimensional ones is a matrix WWW of shape [din×dout][d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{out}}][din​×dout​], applied as XWXWXW, producing shape [n×dout][n \times d_{\text{out}}][n×dout​].
✎Note

Older math textbooks — and some classic papers — use the opposite convention: vectors are columns, and a layer is written WxW\mathbf{x}Wx with WWW of shape [dout×din][d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}}][dout​×din​]. The two conventions are transposes of each other and carry identical content. This book uses rows-and-right-multiply because that is how the tensors are laid out in PyTorch, JAX, and every transformer codebase you will read. When you see WxW\mathbf{x}Wx in a paper, mentally transpose; the ideas survive the flip.

The universal debugging move follows from this: check that inner dimensions match. [n×d]⋅[d×k][n \times d] \cdot [d \times k][n×d]⋅[d×k] works and yields [n×k][n \times k][n×k]; if the two inner numbers differ, the expression is meaningless. Chapter 2 builds this from scratch.

✏️Worked example

Decode the most famous equation in the book, from Chapter 23:

Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax⁡ ⁣(QK⊤dk)V\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \softmax\!\left(\frac{QK\T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)VAttention(Q,K,V)=softmax(dk​​QK⊤​)V

Read aloud: “attention of Q, K, V equals softmax of Q times K-transpose, divided by the square root of d-k, all times V.” Now the shapes, with n=5n = 5n=5 tokens and dk=64d_k = 64dk​=64: QQQ and KKK are [5×64][5 \times 64][5×64], so QK⊤QK\TQK⊤ is [5×64]⋅[64×5]=[5×5][5 \times 64] \cdot [64 \times 5] = [5 \times 5][5×64]⋅[64×5]=[5×5] — a score between every pair of tokens. Dividing by 64=8\sqrt{64} = 864​=8 rescales the scores; softmax turns each row into probabilities; multiplying by VVV ([5×64][5 \times 64][5×64]) gives back [5×64][5 \times 64][5×64] — one blended vector per token. Two sentences of speech and one shape-check, and the equation is transparent.

⚠️Careful

The overloads. The field reuses letters, and three collisions appear in this book. HHH is the entropy in probability chapters, the Hessian in optimization chapters, and lowercase hhh counts attention heads — context and typography (function-of-a-distribution vs. bare matrix) disambiguate. Worse is β\betaβ: it is the momentum coefficient in optimizers (Adam has two, β1\beta_1β1​ and β2\beta_2β2​), the shift parameter in normalization, and the KL-strength coefficient in direct preference optimization (DPO). These are three unrelated quantities that happen to share a letter. When a chapter uses one, it says so — but if a β\betaβ ever looks senseless, check which of the three you are holding.

Big-O in one paragraph

Big-O notation answers one question: how does cost grow as the input grows, ignoring constant factors? O(n)O(n)O(n) (“order n”) means doubling the input roughly doubles the cost; O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) (“order n squared”) means doubling the input quadruples it; O(nlog⁡n)O(n \log n)O(nlogn) sits between. It deliberately discards constants — an O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) algorithm with a tiny constant can beat an O(n)O(n)O(n) one at practical sizes — so it describes scaling behavior, not a stopwatch. The book’s flagship example: standard attention costs O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) in sequence length, which is why long context is hard and why state-space models’ O(n)O(n)O(n) claim matters.

Where this leaves us

  • Typography declares type: italic scalars, bold vectors, capital matrices — and shapes are [rows×columns][\text{rows} \times \text{columns}][rows×columns], with tokens as rows and weights multiplying from the right.
  • Every operation, derivative, and probability symbol has a spoken English form, and reading equations aloud is the fastest route to understanding them.
  • Derivatives are sensitivities; the gradient stacks them; the Jacobian and Hessian are the same idea at matrix scale.
  • β\betaβ and HHH are overloaded across subfields — check context before panicking.
  • Each notation cluster has a home: vectors and matrices in Chapters 1–2, calculus in Chapters 4–5, probability and information in Chapters 6–7, the transformer’s dimension zoo in Chapters 23–25, and the scaling triple N,D,CN, D, CN,D,C in Chapter 32.
