Sparse Autoencoders

How sparse autoencoders unmix superposition into human-readable features — the mechanics, the Golden Gate Claude results arc, the variant zoo, and the honest limitations.

The previous chapter ended on a cliffhanger. Models represent far more concepts than they have neurons by packing features into superposition — thousands of nearly-orthogonal directions crammed into a space that can only hold so many exactly-orthogonal ones. The price is that individual neurons are polysemantic: look at any one of them and you see a smear of unrelated concepts. The features are real, the evidence says, but they are mixed together like a thousand radio stations broadcasting on overlapping frequencies.

So the obvious next question: can we build a receiver? Can we take the mixed-up activations a model produces and unmix them — recover the underlying dictionary of features, each one clean, each one meaning one thing?

From 2023 to 2026, the field’s main answer was the sparse autoencoder (SAE): a small, separately-trained network that learns to rewrite a model’s activations as a sparse combination of directions drawn from a much larger learned dictionary. It became the microscope of mechanistic interpretability — the tool behind Anthropic’s discovery of millions of human-interpretable features inside Claude 3 Sonnet, behind the gloriously absurd “Golden Gate Claude” demo, behind OpenAI’s 16-million-latent decomposition of GPT-4, and behind Google DeepMind’s open release of over 400 SAEs that you can browse in your browser tonight.

This chapter covers the whole arc: how SAEs work mechanically (it is a two-layer network and one loss function — you already know every ingredient), how one is trained and read, what the landmark results showed, the zoo of architectural variants and what single idea each contributes, and then — with full honesty — the limitations that led the field to a more sober view by 2025: SAEs are a useful lens, and they are almost certainly not a ground-truth inventory of “the model’s real features.”

The unmixing problem

Let us restate the goal precisely, because the precision matters.

The superposition hypothesis says an activation vector a∈Rda \in \R^da∈Rd (say, one token’s residual-stream vector, ddd a few thousand) is approximately a sparse sum of feature directions:

a  ≈  b  +  ∑i=1mfi di,a \;\approx\; b \;+\; \sum_{i=1}^{m} f_i \, d_i,a≈b+i=1∑m​fi​di​,

where d1,…,dmd_1, \dots, d_md1​,…,dm​ are mmm unit-length directions in Rd\R^dRd (the dictionary), mmm is much larger than ddd (the dictionary is overcomplete), fi≥0f_i \ge 0fi​≥0 is how strongly feature iii is active on this input (zero for almost all iii — that is the sparsity), and bbb is a bias accounting for the average activation. On a token inside a chess notation, a handful of features fire — “chess”, “algebraic notation”, “mid-game” — and the other million-odd features sit at exactly zero.

If we could recover the dictionary {di}\{d_i\}{di​} and, for any input, the sparse coefficients {fi}\{f_i\}{fi​}, we would have converted an inscrutable ddd-dimensional vector of neuron soup into a short, human-readable list: this token’s representation = 3.2 × “chess” + 1.1 × “Latin abbreviation” + 0.4 × “formal register”. That is the entire pitch.

This problem was not invented by interpretability researchers. It is dictionary learning (also called sparse coding), studied since the 1990s, when Olshausen and Field showed that learning a sparse code for natural images recovers edge detectors resembling neurons in visual cortex. The classical theory even offers hope: under certain conditions, sparse overcomplete codes are identifiable — the true dictionary can be recovered from data alone. The interpretability bet was that transformer activations are close enough to this regime for the machinery to work.

💡Intuition

Think of the activation vector as a cash register total and the dictionary as a price list. Seeing only the total — $23.47 — you cannot tell what was bought. But if you know the price list and you know each basket contains only a few items, the total often has a unique explanation. Sparsity is what makes the inverse problem solvable: with few enough nonzero coefficients, there is roughly one way to write the observed vector as a combination of dictionary elements. The SAE learns the price list and the itemization simultaneously, from nothing but a huge pile of register totals.

The machine itself

An SAE is an autoencoder: a network trained to reproduce its own input. Ordinary autoencoders compress through a narrow bottleneck. An SAE inverts this: the middle layer is much wider than the input — 8× to 256× wider in practice — and the bottleneck is not width but sparsity. The network may use only a few of its many middle units at a time.

∑The math

Fix a location in the model — say the residual stream after layer ℓ\ellℓ — and collect activation vectors a∈Rda \in \R^da∈Rd over billions of tokens. The SAE has an encoder and a decoder:

The SAE is dictionary learning, implemented as one gradient-trainable network.

Why the ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ penalty, and not, say, a squared (ℓ2\ell_2ℓ2​) penalty? This is the payoff of a geometric fact planted all the way back in Chapter 1: the ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ ball is a diamond with corners on the coordinate axes, while the ℓ2\ell_2ℓ2​ ball is a sphere with no corners at all. Minimizing reconstruction error subject to a norm budget means finding where the error’s level sets first touch the norm ball — and level sets overwhelmingly first touch a diamond at a corner, where most coordinates are exactly zero. Spheres have no preferred touching points, so ℓ2\ell_2ℓ2​ shrinks every coordinate a little and zeros out none. In gradient terms: the ℓ2\ell_2ℓ2​ penalty’s pull toward zero (2λfi2\lambda f_i2λfi​) fades as fif_ifi​ shrinks, while the ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ pull is a constant λ\lambdaλ all the way in — strong enough at the origin to pin small coefficients at exactly zero. The same mechanism made lasso regression sparse in Chapter 13; here it is repurposed to make representations sparse.

Multiply this by a million coefficients per token and you get codes that are exactly sparse, not approximately.

One trap in this loss is worth naming, because the fix appears in every SAE paper’s methods section.

⚠️Careful

The scale cheat. The ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ penalty charges for the magnitude of fif_ifi​, but the reconstruction fi dif_i \, d_ifi​di​ only cares about the product. So the SAE can halve every coefficient and double every decoder column, paying half the sparsity tax for an identical reconstruction — repeat forever and the penalty becomes meaningless. Standard fixes: constrain each decoder column to unit norm (the original Anthropic recipe), or multiply each fif_ifi​ by its decoder column’s norm inside the penalty so the tax is scale-invariant. If you train an SAE and the ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ term dives while sparsity does not improve, this cheat is what is happening.

Training one in practice

The equations fit on an index card; the engineering is where SAE work lives.

⚙️In practice

Which activations. The three standard targets are the residual stream (the running sum every layer reads and writes — Anthropic’s and OpenAI’s choice for their headline runs), multi-layer perceptron (MLP) outputs, and attention outputs. Middle layers are favored: late enough that abstract features have formed, early enough that the model still does interesting things with them. Gemma Scope covered every layer and all three sites, which is why it is so useful for research.

How much data. Billions of activation vectors. Towards Monosemanticity used 8 billion; OpenAI’s GPT-4 SAE trained on 40 billion tokens’ worth. The activations are precomputed and shuffled — the Gemma Scope team reports managing roughly 20 pebibytes of activation storage — because feeding an SAE correlated activations from consecutive tokens of one document hurts the dictionary. Total cost is not trivial: DeepMind put Gemma Scope’s training compute at around 15% of the pretraining compute of Gemma 2 9B (approximate, and a lot for an interpretability artifact).

The width choice. Expansion factors run from 8× to 256× and beyond; the Claude 3 Sonnet dictionaries were 1M, 4M, and 34M features. Wider dictionaries find rarer, finer features (more on this below) but cost more and split coarse features into fine ones, so “the right width” depends on what question you are asking. Nobody has a principled answer; labs train a ladder of widths and use whichever suits the task.

Dead features. A feature that never activates gets no gradient and never recovers — and in naive large runs, huge fractions of the dictionary die (OpenAI reported up to 90% dead in some configurations). Two standard remedies: resampling — periodically detect dead features and reinitialize them pointed at examples the SAE currently reconstructs worst — and auxiliary losses like OpenAI’s AuxK, which forces dead features to help explain the residual error, keeping them warm. With such fixes, the 16M-latent GPT-4 run kept dead features to about 7%.

Hyperparameters. λ\lambdaλ is swept to hit a target L0L_0L0​ — the average number of active features per token — typically somewhere between 30 and 300. Optimizer is Adam; learning rates are small (order 10−410^{-4}10−4); the main diagnostic curve is the Pareto frontier of reconstruction loss versus L0L_0L0​, and every architecture paper in this chapter is a claim about pushing that frontier.

One more practicality that doubles as an honesty check: after training, you can splice the SAE into the model — replace the layer’s activation with the SAE’s reconstruction of it — and measure how much the model’s cross-entropy loss degrades. Good 2024-era SAEs preserved most but not all of the model’s performance this way; the gap is our first hint of the “dark matter” section to come.

Reading a feature

Training gives you a million anonymous directions. Turning direction #31,164,353 into knowledge is its own methodology, and the standard instrument is the feature dashboard — worth internalizing because chapters 52 and 56 assume you can read one.

Four kinds of evidence appear on a dashboard, in increasing order of rigor.

Max-activating examples. Run the SAE over a large corpus, keep the text snippets where the feature fires hardest, and highlight the responsible tokens. This is the fastest way to form a hypothesis — and, as Chapter 49 warned about neuron browsing, a seductive way to fool yourself, because the top 20 examples can look coherent while the middle of the activation distribution is a mess. Good dashboards show samples from every activation band, not only the peak.

The activation histogram and frequency. How often does the feature fire, and with what distribution? A crisp bimodal shape — a spike at zero and a clean separated bump — suggests a discrete feature; a smear suggests the SAE latched onto something fuzzier.

Logit attribution. Because the decoder direction lives in the residual stream, you can multiply it through the unembedding matrix and ask: if this feature fires, which output tokens’ logits go up? A “Golden Gate” feature that boosts “Bridge” and “Francisco” links representation to behavior, not merely to input patterns.

Automated interpretation. With millions of features, humans cannot label them all, so a large language model (LLM) does a first pass: show it the activating examples, ask for a short explanation, then score that explanation by testing whether it predicts the feature’s activations on held-out text (either simulating activations, following OpenAI’s 2023 neuron-explanation protocol, or the cheaper detection variant: can a model armed with the explanation pick out which snippets activate the feature?). Auto-interp made million-feature dictionaries navigable, but treat the labels as captions, not ground truth: scores are middling on average, explanations tend to over-general (“words related to technology”), and a label that sounds specific can fail badly on the feature’s mid-range activations.

The results arc, 2023–2026

The technical content above could have been (and mostly was) written down in 2022. What made SAEs the center of gravity of interpretability was a sequence of empirical results, each raising the stakes.

Towards Monosemanticity: the proof of concept

In October 2023, Anthropic’s Towards Monosemanticity (Bricken et al.) trained SAEs on the 512-neuron MLP layer of a deliberately tiny one-layer transformer, with dictionaries from 512 up to about 131,000 features, on 8 billion activation samples. The result that mattered: where the neurons were hopelessly polysemantic, the learned features were overwhelmingly clean. A feature for Arabic script. A feature for DNA sequences. Features for base64 strings, Hebrew, nouns in mathematical prose — each firing precisely on its concept and, when activated artificially, steering the model’s output toward it. Blinded human raters judged features interpretable far more often than neurons, and Claude-generated explanations scored the same way.

The paper also surfaced a phenomenon that turned out to be load-bearing: feature splitting. Train a small dictionary and you get one feature for “base64”; train a larger one and it splits into several — base64 in URLs, base64 after a quote mark, and so on. Features come in families at every granularity, and the dictionary width picks which level of the hierarchy you see. Keep this in mind for the limitations section: if features split endlessly, what exactly is “the” feature?

Scaling Monosemanticity: the microscope meets a frontier model

The obvious objection — “cute, but that model is a toy” — stood until May 2024, when Scaling Monosemanticity (Templeton et al.) trained SAEs with 1 million, 4 million, and 34 million features on the middle-layer residual stream of Claude 3 Sonnet, a production frontier model. Three findings define the paper.

First, the features got abstract. A one-layer model’s features are mostly about surface text. Sonnet’s features tracked concepts: a Golden Gate Bridge feature that fires on English mentions, French mentions, Japanese mentions, and images of the bridge — multilingual and multimodal, meaning the SAE had found something closer to an idea than a string pattern. Features for code bugs and security vulnerabilities. For inner conflict. For sycophantic praise. Nearby features (by decoder-direction cosine similarity) formed meaningful neighborhoods — the Golden Gate feature sat near Alcatraz and the 1906 earthquake.

Second, a quantitative regularity connecting dictionary size to concept rarity: rare concepts need big dictionaries. Comparing the 1M, 4M, and 34M runs, concepts appeared as dedicated features roughly in order of their frequency in the training distribution — common concepts show up in small dictionaries, and each expansion buys features for progressively rarer concepts. (London appeared in all three; individual city blocks would need something far larger.) The sobering extrapolation in the paper: capturing everything a frontier model represents might take dictionaries of billions of features, with training compute rivaling the model’s own — a scaling wall that shaped everything after.

Third — safety relevance, the actual point of the program (Chapter 48). The team found features that fire on deception and manipulation, on sycophancy, on bias and slurs, on scam emails, on discussions of bioweapons and other dangerous content. Finding a “deception” feature does not mean the model is deceiving anyone at that moment — the feature fires on discussions of deception too — but it demonstrated that the concepts safety researchers care about exist as manipulable directions, not diffuse vibes.

And then there is the showpiece, which deserves telling properly.

🕰History

Golden Gate Claude. To prove features were causal levers and not post-hoc descriptions, the team took the Golden Gate Bridge feature and clamped it: during generation, they fixed that one coefficient (out of 34 million) to roughly ten times its maximum observed activation and let the model run. The result was a model with an idée fixe. Asked for its physical form: “I am the Golden Gate Bridge… my physical form is the iconic bridge itself.” Asked for a good recipe, it steered dinner toward something you might enjoy eating while driving across the Golden Gate Bridge in the fog. Every conversation bent toward the bridge, usually with grammatical fluency and its other capabilities intact. For about 24 hours in May 2024, Anthropic put “Golden Gate Claude” on claude.ai for the public to talk to — a piece of scientific communication that did more for interpretability’s public profile than any paper. The serious content under the joke: one interpretable coefficient, discovered without supervision, causally steered a frontier model’s behavior in exactly the predicted direction. The same clamping method turned safety-relevant features into demonstrations — activating a scam-email feature elicited scam drafts the model would normally refuse. Steering as a technique gets its own chapter: Chapter 54.

Gemma Scope: the microscope for everyone

Anthropic’s and OpenAI’s SAEs sat on private models. In August 2024, Google DeepMind released Gemma Scope (Lieberum et al.): a comprehensive suite of more than 400 SAEs — JumpReLU architecture — covering every layer and sublayer of the open Gemma 2 2B and 9B models (plus selected layers of 27B), with over 30 million features in total, all weights public.

The practical consequence is that this chapter is not spectator sport. Go to Neuronpedia, an open interpretability platform that hosts Gemma Scope (and many other dictionaries) — the “Gemma Scope” demo is a guided tour. You can type any sentence, see which features fire on each token, click a feature to see its dashboard (the same max-activating examples, histograms, and auto-interp labels as the figure above), search features by concept, and steer a live Gemma with a slider — your own small Golden Gate experiments. Much of the post-2024 academic SAE literature runs on Gemma Scope, precisely because anyone can check anyone else’s claims.

The variant zoo

Every major variant exists to fix a specific pathology of the vanilla ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ SAE. Each is one idea; here they are, with their one idea attached.

Gated SAEs (Rajamanoharan et al., 2024) attack shrinkage — the systematic underestimation you computed in the worked example, where the ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ penalty drags surviving activations below their true values. The fix: decouple “is this feature on?” from “how strong is it?”. A gate path (with its own threshold) makes the binary on/off decision and absorbs the sparsity penalty; a separate magnitude path, penalty-free, estimates the coefficient. Same sparsity, visibly better reconstruction at matched L0L_0L0​.

JumpReLU SAEs (Rajamanoharan et al., 2024) get the same decoupling with less machinery: replace the ReLU with a jump — output zero below a per-feature learned threshold θi\theta_iθi​, and pass the value through unshrunk above it. Penalize L0L_0L0​ (the count of active features) directly instead of ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​. Both the jump and the count are non-differentiable, so training uses straight-through estimators — the same trick that trains quantized networks (Chapter 39): pretend, on the backward pass, that the discontinuity was a smooth ramp. This is the architecture DeepMind judged Pareto-best and baked into Gemma Scope.

TopK SAEs (Gao et al., OpenAI, 2024) make sparsity exact rather than penalized: keep the kkk largest pre-activations, zero the rest. No λ\lambdaλ to sweep, no shrinkage from a penalty, and L0=kL_0 = kL0​=k by construction, which makes architecture comparisons honest. This is the design OpenAI scaled to a 16-million-latent SAE on GPT-4’s residual stream over 40 billion tokens, alongside clean scaling laws for SAEs (reconstruction improves as a power law in dictionary size and compute — Chapter 32’s logic, applied to the microscope itself) and a battery of quality metrics beyond reconstruction.

BatchTopK (Bussmann et al., 2024) relaxes TopK’s one rigid quota: choose the top n×kn \times kn×k activations across a whole batch of nnn tokens, so a token about nothing in particular can use 12 features while a dense technical token uses 80, with the average still kkk. A small change, consistently better frontiers.

Two further variants change what is being approximated, and they matter beyond this chapter because they are the substrate of Chapter 52.

Transcoders (Dunefsky et al., 2024) drop the auto- from autoencoder. An SAE approximates activations at a point: input aaa, target aaa. A transcoder is trained input →\to→ different output — specifically, the MLP block’s input to the MLP block’s output — so it learns a sparse, interpretable replacement for the MLP’s function. Why that is more causally useful: with an SAE you can say “feature X was active before the MLP and feature Y after,” but the MLP between them remains a black box, and any claim about X causing Y depends on the particular input. A transcoder gives you a direct, weight-level answer — which sparse input features connect to which sparse output features, with what fixed weights — turning “what does this MLP compute?” into a lookup rather than an experiment-per-input.

Crosscoders (Lindsey et al., 2024) share one feature dictionary across layers (or even across two different models): one set of sparse coefficients per token, with per-layer decoders. Since Chapter 16 you have known that the residual stream carries features forward through many layers; a naive per-layer SAE rediscovers the same persistent feature at layer 8, 9, 10, …, drowning analyses in duplicates. Crosscoders represent such a feature once, tracking its strength across depth — and the cross-model version lets you “diff” a base model against its fine-tuned sibling, feature by feature. Cross-layer transcoders — the combination of both ideas — are the machinery inside Anthropic’s 2025 attribution graphs, which trace how features feed features across an entire forward pass. That story is the next chapter’s.

What the microscope misses

Now the ledger’s other side. Everything below is established, replicated, and important; none of it makes SAEs useless, but all of it constrains what an SAE result can honestly claim.

Dark matter. SAEs do not fully reconstruct activations — headline runs explain most of the variance (roughly 65–90% depending on layer, width, and sparsity target; approximate and setting-dependent), and splicing the reconstruction into the model measurably worsens its loss. The unexplained remainder got the (borrowed) name dark matter, and Engels et al., 2024 dissected it with an uncomfortable result. If the residue were merely rare features the dictionary is too small to catch, wider SAEs would eat it — and part of the error does shrink that way. But a substantial component behaves differently: it is linearly predictable from the input activation itself (you can regress the SAE’s error from aaa and do well), yet it does not decompose into sparse directions, and a further nonlinear error component barely budges as dictionaries grow. In plain terms: some of what the model encodes appears not to be a sparse sum of directions at all, and no affordable amount of dictionary scaling will capture it. The sparse-linear lens has a blind spot of unknown importance.

Non-uniqueness. If SAEs recovered “the model’s true features,” two SAEs trained on the same activations should agree. Paulo and Belrose, 2025 trained SAEs identical in everything but the random seed and found they learn substantially different dictionaries — for wide SAEs, a majority of one run’s features can have no close counterpart in the other (overlap on the order of a third in their larger settings; approximate). The charitable reading: activation space contains far more real, interpretable structure than any one dictionary can hold, and each seed samples a different subset — consistent with feature splitting and the frequency-versus-width relation. The uncharitable reading: SAE features are partly artifacts of the method, one workable sparse basis among many, in which case “the feature exists in the model” is a stronger claim than the evidence supports. Either way, any result that depends on one specific dictionary being canonical is on notice.

Absorption and composition pathologies. The sparsity objective is amoral: it minimizes active features, not conceptual cleanliness. A documented failure mode is feature absorption: a general feature (“starts with S”) and a specific feature (“the token short”) overlap, so the SAE saves one activation by folding the general information into the specific feature — and the “starts with S” feature then mysteriously fails to fire on “short,” precisely the kind of exception that wrecks downstream use. Relatedly, SAEs happily learn compositions (one feature for “red triangle”) where the model plausibly represents ingredients (“red” and “triangle”) — sparser, but a misdescription of the underlying variables.

Probes sometimes beat the microscope. If SAE features carve the model at its joints, they should be the best raw material for downstream tasks. A series of 2024–2025 evaluations found otherwise: for detecting a stated concept, simple linear probes on raw activations matched or beat SAE-based classifiers in systematic case studies; for steering, prompting, and other practical baselines were often competitive. In early 2025, DeepMind’s mechanistic-interpretability team publicly reported negative results on SAE-based downstream tasks and deprioritized SAE research — a notable reversal from the lab that had shipped Gemma Scope months earlier. The honest summary: where a supervised signal exists, train a probe; the SAE’s advantage is unsupervised discovery — the hypotheses you did not know to test.

The evaluation problem underneath it all. Every critique above is measurable; the deepest one is not. There is no ground truth for “the model’s real features,” so SAE quality is assessed by proxies — reconstruction, sparsity, auto-interp scores, downstream probing, spliced-in loss — and the proxies disagree with each other. Toy models with known planted features (where SAEs can be checked exactly, and pass or fail informatively) remain the only setting with an answer key, and their faithfulness to real transformers is itself an open question. This is normal for a young instrument science — early microscopists argued about which blobs were specimen and which were the lens — but it means every SAE claim should carry an implicit “as seen through this particular glass.”

✎Note

The 2025–2026 consensus, as fairly as I can state it. SAEs did not deliver what the most optimistic 2023 framing hoped — a canonical, exhaustive parts list for a neural network. They did deliver: unsupervised discovery of interpretable, causally steerable structure at frontier scale; the feature dashboards and browsing infrastructure the whole field now works with; and the direct technical lineage (transcoders, crosscoders) behind attribution graphs, which are the state of the art in circuit analysis. Some labs deemphasized SAE-first research; Anthropic’s interpretability team kept sparse dictionary methods at the core of its circuit-tracing stack; open-source work continues at pace on Gemma Scope-class artifacts. Hold both truths: a lens, and a good one — not ground truth.

Where this leaves us

  • Superposition poses an unmixing problem, and the SAE answers it with classical dictionary learning wearing a two-layer network: encode to a massively overcomplete code, decode back, and let the combined loss ∥a−a^∥2+λ∥f∥1\lVert a - \hat a \rVert^2 + \lambda \lVert f \rVert_1∥a−a^∥2+λ∥f∥1​ negotiate between faithfulness and sparsity. The decoder’s columns are the candidate feature directions.
  • You can explain why ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ produces exact zeros (corners of the ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ ball; constant gradient at the origin) and why vanilla SAEs systematically shrink activations — and match each major variant to its fix: gated (separate on/off from magnitude), JumpReLU (learned thresholds, straight-through training), TopK/BatchTopK (exact sparsity by construction).
  • Reading a feature is a methodology, not a glance: max-activating examples across the whole distribution, activation histograms, logit attribution, and LLM auto-interp labels scored for reliability — each with known ways to fool you.
  • The results arc runs from a one-layer proof of concept (2023) to 34 million features on Claude 3 Sonnet with abstract, multilingual, multimodal, and safety-relevant features — with Golden Gate Claude as the proof that one clamped coefficient causally steers a frontier model — to Gemma Scope and Neuronpedia putting the microscope in your browser.
  • Transcoders approximate a component’s function rather than a point, and crosscoders share dictionaries across layers — the two ideas that turn feature-finding into circuit-tracing.
  • And you can state the limitations with mechanisms attached: dark matter with a stubborn nonlinear component, seed-dependent dictionaries, absorption pathologies, probe baselines that often win when supervision exists, and a field still lacking ground truth to evaluate against.
