Retrieval-Augmented Generation

How retrieval pipelines ground a language model in knowledge it was never trained on: chunking, embeddings, approximate nearest-neighbor search, reranking, agentic retrieval, and how to tell whether any of it actually worked.

Ask a frontier model about the French Revolution and it will give you a small lecture, because the French Revolution is in its training data thousands of times over. Ask it what the termination clause in your company’s master services agreement says, and it has nothing — that document was never on the internet, never in a training corpus, and no amount of scale will put it there. Ask it about a court decision from last month and you hit a different wall: the model’s knowledge stopped at its training cutoff, frozen like a photograph.

The knowledge a model absorbs during pretraining lives in its weights — researchers call this parametric knowledge, because it is stored in the parameters. It is astonishingly broad and permanently flawed in specific, predictable ways. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is the standard fix: instead of hoping the model memorized the answer, you look up relevant documents at question time and paste them into the context window, so the model can read the evidence before it writes. The model stops being a closed-book exam-taker and becomes an open-book one.

That one sentence — “look up relevant documents and paste them in” — hides an entire engineering discipline. How do you cut a 60-page contract into pieces a search system can find? How do you search by meaning rather than by keyword, across millions of pieces, in milliseconds? How do you decide which ten of the hundred plausible matches deserve the model’s limited attention? And how do you tell whether the final answer is supported by what was retrieved, or whether the model hallucinated with citations attached? This chapter builds the full pipeline end to end, with the math under each stage, then covers the 2026 reality: the fixed pipeline is increasingly being replaced by models that drive their own search inside an agent loop.

Three ways parametric knowledge fails

RAG exists because parametric knowledge fails in three distinct ways, and it is worth keeping them distinct, because each motivates a different part of the design.

It is frozen. Training runs end. A model trained through late 2025 knows nothing that happened in 2026 — no new case law, no new API, no reorg at your company, nothing in this morning’s inbox. Retraining a frontier model costs hundreds of millions of dollars, so you cannot fix staleness by retraining weekly. A retrieval index, by contrast, can be updated in seconds: add the new document, embed it, insert it. Freshness becomes a data-engineering property instead of a training property.

It is fuzzy. Even for facts that were in training data, the model stores a lossy compression, not a database. It saw your favorite statute quoted correctly in some documents, misquoted in others, paraphrased in thousands more, and its weights encode a blurry average. This is why models confidently produce almost-right citations, plausible-but-wrong section numbers, and dates that are off by one. As we saw in the chapter on what a language model is, the training objective rewards probable text, and an almost-right fact is highly probable. Retrieval replaces the blur with the literal text of the source, sitting in the context window where the model’s attention can copy from it exactly.

It is unattributable. When a model answers from its weights, there is no way to ask where that came from. The knowledge is smeared across billions of parameters (a fact Part VII will make painfully concrete). For a casual question that may be fine; for medicine, law, finance, or anything a human must verify, it is disqualifying. RAG makes attribution structural: the answer was generated from specific retrieved passages, so the system can cite them — and a checker (human or model) can compare answer against source.

💡Intuition

A model without retrieval is a brilliant colleague locked in a room in 2025 with no phone and a photographic-but-smudged memory. RAG hands that colleague a stack of the actual documents relevant to your question, one minute before they answer. The colleague’s reasoning ability is unchanged — what changes is that they can now quote, check, and cite, instead of recalling. Almost everything in this chapter is about making the stack of documents small and relevant, because the colleague can only read so much before answering.

The pipeline, end to end

Here is the classical RAG pipeline. Everything before the vector index runs offline, once per document; everything after runs online, once per query, usually in well under a second.

To make each stage concrete we will carry one running example through the whole chapter. Imagine a law firm’s internal assistant with an index of 12,000 contracts and memos, and a lawyer who asks:

“What notice period does the Acme master services agreement require for termination for convenience?”

The correct answer lives in one paragraph of one 60-page document. The pipeline’s entire job is to get that paragraph — and its neighbors, and nothing else — in front of the model.

Chunking: cutting documents into retrievable pieces

You cannot embed a 60-page contract as one vector; a single vector cannot faithfully summarize 40,000 tokens of content, and even if it could, retrieving the whole document would flood the context window. So documents are cut into chunks — passages of a few hundred tokens that are each embedded and indexed separately. Chunking looks like a boring preprocessing step. It is where more RAG systems quietly fail than anywhere else, because the chunk is the unit of retrieval: if the answer is split across a chunk boundary, or a chunk is meaningless out of context, no downstream cleverness recovers it.

Three families of strategy, in increasing order of effort:

  • Fixed-size chunking slices every document into windows of, say, 400 tokens, usually with an overlap (e.g., 80 tokens repeated between consecutive chunks) so that a sentence falling on a boundary appears whole in at least one chunk. Trivial to implement, indifferent to meaning: it will happily cut a defined term from its definition or split a table in half.
  • Semantic chunking places boundaries where the content shifts — for instance, by embedding each sentence and starting a new chunk when the similarity between consecutive sentences drops below a threshold. Better boundaries, more compute, and another hyperparameter to tune.
  • Structural chunking uses the document’s own skeleton: split contracts at section headings, code at function boundaries, markdown at headers, and keep each clause or subsection intact. When documents have structure this is almost always the right answer, often combined with a fixed-size cap for oversized sections.

For our running example: the Acme master services agreement (MSA) is roughly 30,000 words ≈ 40,000 tokens. Fixed 400-token chunks with 80-token overlap advance by a stride of 400−80=320400 - 80 = 320400−80=320 tokens, giving 40,000/320=12540{,}000 / 320 = 12540,000/320=125 chunks. Across 12,000 documents of similar size, the index holds on the order of 1.5 million chunks. A structural chunker would instead emit one chunk per numbered section — ”§ 14.2 Termination for Convenience” as a self-contained unit — which is precisely the granularity a lawyer would want cited back.

⚠️Careful

A chunk must make sense to a stranger. The most common chunking failure is context amputation: a chunk that reads “The notice period in the preceding section shall be extended to sixty (60) days” is useless — retrieval can’t know which section, and neither can the model. Mitigations: prepend a breadcrumb of metadata to every chunk (“Acme MSA § 14.2 — Termination”), or go further and have a cheap large language model (LLM) write a one-sentence contextual preamble for each chunk situating it in its document — the approach Anthropic published as contextual retrieval, reporting large reductions in retrieval failure rate. It costs one generation per chunk at index time, which prompt caching makes cheap.

Embedding: bi-encoders and how they are trained

Each chunk now becomes a vector. This is the payoff of the embeddings chapter: an embedding model maps a passage of text to a point in Rd\R^dRd (typically ddd between 256 and 4096) such that texts with similar meaning land near each other. At query time the question is mapped into the same space, and “find relevant chunks” becomes “find nearest vectors” — a geometry problem, in exactly the high-dimensional spaces Chapter 3 taught you to distrust your intuitions about.

The architecture used for this is called a bi-encoder: one encoder (a transformer, usually BERT-style — BERT is the Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers family — or a small decoder) processes the query, the same or a twin encoder processes each chunk, and each side is pooled into a single vector. The two sides never see each other, which is what lets you embed all 1.5 million chunks offline, once, and at query time embed only the question.

How do you train an encoder so that questions land near their answers? The modern recipe comes from dense passage retrieval (DPR) (Karpukhin et al., 2020): contrastive learning. Show the model a query, one passage that answers it (a positive), and many passages that don’t (negatives), and train it to score the positive above the negatives.

If this loss looks familiar, it should: it is the same contrastive objective CLIP (Contrastive Language–Image Pretraining) uses to align images and captions in the multimodal chapter, applied to query–passage pairs instead of image–text pairs.

⚙️In practice

Choosing an embedding model in 2026 usually starts at the MTEB (Massive Text Embedding Benchmark) leaderboard, which scores models across eight task families — retrieval, reranking, clustering, classification, and more — on dozens of datasets. The top of the leaderboard churns monthly (recent leaders have included Qwen3-Embedding and Gemini embedding models, with Voyage, Cohere, and OpenAI’s text-embedding-3 family widely deployed; treat any specific ranking as stale the week you read it). Practical selection criteria matter more than leaderboard rank: dimension (storage and search cost scale linearly with ddd; many models offer Matryoshka-style truncatable dimensions), maximum input length, language and domain coverage, license, and — most important — performance on your documents, measured with the evaluation methods later in this chapter. A known caveat: public leaderboards invite overfitting, and MTEB’s own maintainers caution that small score differences are noise.

Indexing: approximate nearest neighbors and HNSW

We now have 1.5 million chunk vectors and, at query time, one query vector. Exact search — dot product against every chunk — costs 1.5M×1024≈1.51.5\text{M} \times 1024 \approx 1.51.5M×1024≈1.5 billion multiply-adds. Honest note: on modern hardware with SIMD (single-instruction, multiple-data) vector arithmetic, that brute-force scan takes only a few tens of milliseconds, so at this scale you may not need anything fancier. But at 10810^8108–10910^9109 vectors, or thousands of queries per second, exact search dies, and everyone switches to approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search: algorithms that return almost certainly the nearest vectors in a tiny fraction of the time, trading a percent or two of recall for orders of magnitude of speed.

The dominant ANN algorithm in production is HNSW — hierarchical navigable small world graphs (Malkov & Yashunin, 2016).

💡Intuition

HNSW is a highway system for vector space. Build a graph where each vector is a node connected to its near neighbors; to search, start anywhere and greedily hop to whichever neighbor is closest to the query, until no neighbor improves. That works but is slow if every road is a local street. So HNSW adds layers, like a skip list: the top layer contains only a handful of nodes with long-range links (interstates), the next layer more nodes with medium links (state highways), and the bottom layer contains every vector with short links (local streets). A search enters at the top, takes a few long hops to get to roughly the right region, drops down a layer, refines, and repeats — reaching the true neighborhood in O(log⁡N)O(\log N)O(logN) hops instead of scanning everything. The “small world” in the name is the same phenomenon as six degrees of separation: a few long-range links make any node reachable from any other in very few steps.

HNSW’s knobs trade recall against speed and memory: MMM (links per node, commonly 16–64), efConstruction (search breadth while building), and efSearch (search breadth at query time — raise it and recall climbs toward exact search while latency grows). Typical production settings reach 95–99% recall at millisecond latency over tens of millions of vectors. The main cost is memory: the graph lives alongside the vectors, and 1.5M vectors at d=1024d = 1024d=1024 in float32 already occupy 1.5M×4,0961.5\text{M} \times 4{,}0961.5M×4,096 bytes ≈ 6 GB before the graph. Compression (scalar or product quantization, or truncating Matryoshka dimensions) is standard at larger scales.

One honest paragraph on the vector database landscape, which peaked as a venture-capital category around 2023 and has since consolidated into a feature: dedicated engines (Pinecone, Weaviate, Qdrant, Milvus, Chroma, Turbopuffer) compete with vector support bolted onto databases you already run — pgvector in Postgres, Elasticsearch/OpenSearch, Redis, SQLite extensions — and with libraries you embed directly (FAISS, hnswlib, LanceDB). The unglamorous truth: below a few million vectors, pgvector or a library plus your existing database is usually the right call, because the hard problems (filtering by metadata, permissions, transactional updates alongside your actual data) are database problems, not vector problems. Dedicated engines earn their keep at billions of vectors, high queries-per-second, or when serverless scale-to-zero pricing fits the workload.

Reranking: cross-encoders

ANN search over bi-encoder vectors is fast because query and chunk were encoded independently — and that independence is also its weakness. Each chunk was compressed to one vector before knowing what would be asked; subtle interactions between question and passage (“sixty days, except during the first contract year” versus the lawyer’s specific situation) are exactly what a single precomputed vector blurs away. So production pipelines retrieve generously (top-kkk with k≈50k \approx 50k≈50–200) and then apply a second, slower, smarter model to reorder the candidates: a cross-encoder reranker.

A cross-encoder concatenates the query and one candidate chunk into a single input sequence and runs a transformer over the pair, so attention flows between every query token and every chunk token. The output is one number: a relevance score for this specific pair. It is dramatically more accurate than a bi-encoder — and dramatically more expensive, because nothing can be precomputed: every (query, chunk) pair costs a full forward pass. That asymmetry dictates the architecture of the whole pipeline: bi-encoder to cheaply narrow 1.5 million chunks to 100 candidates, cross-encoder to carefully pick the best 5–10 of those 100.

The arithmetic that justifies the two-stage design: running a cross-encoder over all 1.5 million chunks would take 1.5 million transformer forward passes per query — minutes of GPU time. Running it over 100 candidates takes about 100 small forward passes — tens of milliseconds. The bi-encoder buys the scale; the cross-encoder buys back the accuracy, as long as the answer made it into the top 100 (which is why the first stage is tuned for recall, not precision).

There is a notable middle ground: ColBERT-style late interaction keeps a vector per token of each chunk and scores a pair as the sum over query tokens of each token’s best match in the chunk — most of a cross-encoder’s accuracy while still allowing precomputation, at the price of storing many vectors per chunk.

⚙️In practice

In 2026 rerankers are commodity infrastructure: hosted APIs (Cohere Rerank, Voyage rerankers) and strong open-weight models (BGE-reranker, Qwen3-Reranker and successors) are drop-in stages, and frontier labs’ retrieval products bundle one invisibly. A separate trend is using a small instruction-tuned LLM as the reranker, prompted to score or order passages listwise — more expensive, sometimes better on messy queries. Typical pipeline defaults you will see in the wild: retrieve top 50–150 by hybrid search, rerank, keep 5–20 chunks, spending a rerank budget of a few tens of milliseconds.

Assembling the context

The surviving chunks are formatted into the prompt — typically each with an identifier and provenance (“[3] Acme MSA, § 14.2, p. 41”) — followed by instructions to answer from the provided sources and cite them by identifier. Two placement facts matter. First, models do not attend uniformly across long prompts: Liu et al. (2023) showed a U-shaped pattern — information at the beginning or end of the context is used far more reliably than information buried in the middle, a bias that persists (in weakened form) in current long-context models. Ordering retrieved chunks so the strongest evidence sits at the edges is a cheap, real win. Second, instructions to cite are not a guarantee of grounding — a model can cite [3] and still contradict it — which is why evaluation, below, treats answer-versus-source agreement as its own measured quantity rather than an assumption.

Hybrid retrieval and query transformation

Why keywords still matter

Before dense retrieval there was — and still is — lexical search: matching literal words, scored by BM25, the 1990s-era ranking function descending from the term-weighting work of Robertson and Spärck Jones. BM25 scores a document for a query by summing, over query terms, a product of two factors: how rare the term is corpus-wide (rare terms carry more information — the inverse-document-frequency idea from information theory), and how often it appears in the document, with diminishing returns and a correction for document length.

Dense retrieval beats BM25 on paraphrase — “termination for convenience” matches “either party may end this agreement without cause” despite zero shared keywords. But BM25 beats dense retrieval on exact identifiers: part numbers, error codes, case citations, function names, ”§ 437c”, “ERR_QUIC_PROTOCOL_ERROR”. These are precisely the tokens an embedding model compresses lossily — they are rare, and rare strings get fuzzy vectors — yet they are often the highest-signal part of a real query. So production systems run hybrid retrieval: both searches in parallel, results merged. The standard merge is reciprocal rank fusion (RRF), which ignores the incomparable raw scores of the two systems and combines ranks:

∑The math

For document ddd appearing at rank ri(d)r_i(d)ri​(d) in each of several result lists iii, the RRF score is

RRF(d)  =  ∑i1k+ri(d)\text{RRF}(d) \;=\; \sum_i \frac{1}{k + r_i(d)}RRF(d)=i∑​k+ri​(d)1​

with kkk conventionally set to 60. The constant kkk damps the influence of top ranks so that one system’s #1 cannot single-handedly dominate; a document must do reasonably well in both lists to win. No score calibration, no tuning, embarrassingly effective — RRF has survived a decade of attempts to beat it with learned fusion, and remains the default in most search engines’ hybrid modes.

✏️Worked example

Chunk A is ranked #1 by BM25 (it contains the literal phrase “termination for convenience”) but only #8 by dense search. Chunk B is ranked #3 by both. With k=60k = 60k=60:

RRF(A)=161+168=0.0164+0.0147=0.0311,RRF(B)=163+163=0.0317.\text{RRF}(A) = \tfrac{1}{61} + \tfrac{1}{68} = 0.0164 + 0.0147 = 0.0311, \qquad \text{RRF}(B) = \tfrac{1}{63} + \tfrac{1}{63} = 0.0317.RRF(A)=611​+681​=0.0164+0.0147=0.0311,RRF(B)=631​+631​=0.0317.

Rewriting the question

The query the user typed is often not the query the index needs. Three transformations, all performed by an LLM before retrieval, are standard:

Query rewriting repairs the query: expand abbreviations, resolve conversational references (“what about the renewal term?” → “What is the renewal term in the Acme master services agreement?”), strip politeness, add likely synonyms. In chat applications, rewriting the follow-up question into a self-contained query using the conversation history is close to mandatory.

HyDE — hypothetical document embeddings (Gao et al., 2022) — attacks a geometric mismatch: questions and answers are different kinds of text, and a question’s embedding may sit awkwardly far from its answer’s embedding. HyDE asks an LLM to hallucinate a plausible answer first (“Section 14.2. Either party may terminate this Agreement for convenience upon sixty (60) days’ prior written notice…”), embeds that fake document, and searches with it. The fake answer’s facts may be wrong, but its form and vocabulary resemble the true passage far more than the question did — and retrieval only needs the neighborhood to be right, because the real document is what gets returned. Hallucination, used as a search heuristic.

Decomposition splits multi-part questions (“Compare the termination and indemnification provisions across our three largest vendor MSAs”) into sub-queries retrieved independently, with results pooled. This is the bridge to agentic retrieval, next — because deciding how to decompose is itself a reasoning task.

Agentic retrieval: search as a tool

Everything so far assumed one fixed pass: query in, chunks out, answer. The most important shift in retrieval since the RAG paper itself is that by 2025–2026, serious systems stopped hard-wiring that pass. Instead, search became a tool — in exactly the sense of the agents chapter: the model is given a search(query) function, decides when to call it, what to search for, reads the results, and decides whether to search again with a refined query, search for something else the results revealed it needs, or answer.

The difference is not cosmetic. A fixed pipeline gets one shot at guessing what evidence the answer needs before any reasoning has happened. An agent loop interleaves retrieval with reasoning: for our lawyer’s harder question — “Can we terminate the Acme agreement early without paying the wind-down fee?” — an agent might first retrieve the termination clause, notice it cross-references § 18 (Fees on Early Termination), issue a second search for that section, notice an amendment is mentioned, search a third time for the amendment, and only then answer. No single upfront query retrieves all three; the need for the second and third searches was discovered by reading the first results. Multi-hop questions are the norm in real work, and iterative retrieval is the only general answer to them. Research systems had explored this direction (self-querying and self-critiquing retrievers such as Self-RAG, and iterative retrieve-then-reason schemes), but what made it ubiquitous was that models got good enough at tool use to be trusted with the loop — the same capability shift, driven by the reasoning-training era, that made agents work at all.

⚙️In practice

“Agentic RAG” is the 2026 default for hard questions. ChatGPT’s and Claude’s web search, deep-research modes, enterprise assistants over document stores, and coding agents grepping a repository all follow the same pattern: retrieval is a tool call inside a reasoning loop, often with multiple retrieval tools (dense index, keyword search, SQL, web) side by side, frequently connected via Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers. Deep-research products push it furthest — dozens to hundreds of searches, minutes of iteration, synthesized reports with citations. The classical fixed pipeline has not disappeared: it survives where latency budgets are tight (autocomplete-style assistance, high-volume support bots) and as the tool the agent calls — everything in this chapter about chunking, embedding, ANN, and reranking still determines the quality of each individual search the agent makes. Agentic RAG changed who composes the queries, not how retrieval works.

Did it work? Evaluating RAG

A RAG system can fail in two independent places: retrieval can fail to find the evidence, or generation can fail to use it. Evaluate them separately, or you will not know which one to fix.

Retrieval metrics need a labeled set: queries paired with the chunks (or documents) that answer them. Two workhorses:

∑The math

Recall@k asks: of the relevant chunks, what fraction appeared in the top kkk results? For a single query with relevant set RRR and top-kkk retrieved set SkS_kSk​,

Recall@k  =  ∣R∩Sk∣∣R∣,\text{Recall@}k \;=\; \frac{|R \cap S_k|}{|R|},Recall@k=∣R∣∣R∩Sk​∣​,

averaged over queries. It is the metric for the first stage of the pipeline, whose only job is to get the answer into the candidate pool.

Mean reciprocal rank (MRR) asks: how high did the first relevant chunk rank? For query iii, let rir_iri​ be the rank of the first relevant result; then

MRR  =  1n∑i=1n1ri,\text{MRR} \;=\; \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{r_i},MRR=n1​i=1∑n​ri​1​,

so rank 1 scores 1, rank 4 scores 0.25, and missing entirely scores 0. MRR rewards putting the good stuff at the top, which matters because context budgets — and the lost-in-the-middle effect — mean rank position translates directly into whether the model reads the evidence.

✏️Worked example

MRR=13(1+13+14)=13(1.583)≈0.53.\text{MRR} = \tfrac{1}{3}\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{1}{4}\right) = \tfrac{1}{3}(1.583) \approx 0.53.MRR=31​(1+31​+41​)=31​(1.583)≈0.53.

Now the diagnosis is mechanical: recall@100 high but recall@10 low → the bi-encoder finds the evidence but ranks it poorly → add or improve the reranker. Recall@100 low → no reranker can save you → fix chunking, embeddings, or add hybrid search upstream.

End-to-end metrics judge the answer. The failure mode that makes them necessary deserves its own warning:

⚠️Careful

Hallucination despite retrieval. Retrieval succeeding does not mean the answer is grounded. Models routinely (a) answer from parametric memory while ignoring contradicting retrieved text, (b) blend two chunks into a claim neither supports, or (c) attach a plausible citation to a sentence the source does not contain — the most dangerous case, because the citation looks like verification. The tension is structural: pretraining taught the model its parametric knowledge is usually right, and a retrieved passage is, from the model’s perspective, text of unknown reliability. When index and weights disagree — precisely the situation RAG exists for, since fresh documents contradict stale memory — the model must be pushed by instructions, post-training, or output checking to prefer the document.

The 2026 toolkit for measuring groundedness is LLM-as-judge decomposition, systematized by frameworks like RAGAS: split the generated answer into individual claims and ask a judge model, for each claim, is this supported by the retrieved context? The supported fraction is the faithfulness score; companion metrics check that the answer addresses the question (answer relevance) and that the retrieved context was pertinent (context relevance) — all reference-free, requiring no gold answers. Stricter systems go further and verify citations mechanically: every cited quote must string-match (or fuzzily match) real text in the cited source, turning “the model says it’s grounded” into a checkable property. Judges inherit the biases covered in the evaluation chapter — calibrate them against a hand-labeled sample before trusting them at scale.

RAG, long context, or fine-tuning?

Three tools claim to solve “the model doesn’t know X,” and they are complements, not competitors. With million-token context windows, why not paste everything in? Because attention over a million tokens costs real money per query (and, with the KV cache, real memory), corpora are usually far bigger than any window, and effective use of a stuffed window still degrades subtly even when needle-in-a-haystack-style benchmarks look clean. Why not fine-tune the knowledge in? Because fine-tuning is good at teaching behavior — style, format, domain vocabulary, tool habits — and poor at reliably adding facts: new facts land as fuzzy parametric knowledge with the same three failures (staleness, blur, no attribution) that motivated this chapter, plus the risk of degrading other capabilities.

The honest 2026 answer is that mature systems use all three: retrieval to select what enters the window, a long context to hold generous amounts of it (RAG got easier as windows grew — retrieval precision matters less when you can afford 50 chunks instead of 5), and fine-tuning to make the model follow the house style, cite properly, and refuse to answer beyond its sources.

Production realities

Three concerns dominate real deployments and rarely appear in papers.

Freshness and incremental indexing. A production index is not built once; it churns. New documents arrive, old ones are edited or deleted, and the index must reflect that without full rebuilds: upserts keyed by a stable chunk ID (usually a hash of document ID + position), deletions handled by tombstones, and — the classic trap — re-chunking on edit: when a document changes, its old chunks must be found and removed, or the index serves stale text indefinitely. Deleting from an HNSW graph is awkward (removing nodes degrades the graph), so engines typically mark-and-filter deleted vectors and compact periodically. A subtler version: when you upgrade the embedding model, every vector in the index must be regenerated, because vectors from different models live in incompatible spaces — plan for full re-embeds as scheduled migrations.

Permissions and multi-tenancy. The index knows no access control unless you build it. If a firm’s index mixes clients’ documents, retrieval for one client must never surface another’s chunks — this must be enforced inside the search (metadata filters applied during ANN traversal, or partition-per-tenant indexes), not by filtering afterward: post-filtering both leaks information through result counts and timing, and can silently return fewer than kkk results after censoring. Treat retrieval as a security boundary: the model will happily summarize anything the retriever hands it, so the retriever is the access-control layer.

Provenance for citations. If the system will cite sources — and for anything high-stakes it must — provenance has to be threaded through from ingestion: each chunk stores its document ID, page, section heading, and character span, so a citation can deep-link to the exact highlighted passage rather than to “somewhere in a 60-page PDF.” This is cheap at index time and impossible to reconstruct afterward. It also enables the citation checking described above: with spans stored, “does the source say this?” becomes a string comparison plus a small judge call, and the system can refuse to display any citation that fails the check — turning groundedness from an aspiration into an enforced invariant.

🕰History

The lineage runs through open-domain question answering. Classical pipelines (the Text Retrieval Conference (TREC) QA track in the 1990s–2000s) bolted answer extraction onto keyword search; DrQA (Chen et al., 2017) put a neural reader on top of term-frequency–inverse-document-frequency (TF-IDF) retrieval over Wikipedia. The dense turn came in 2020: DPR showed contrastively trained bi-encoders beating BM25, REALM trained retrieval end-to-end inside masked-language-model pretraining, and Lewis et al. coined “RAG” for a DPR-retriever-plus-BART-generator trained jointly, marginalizing over retrieved passages. Ironically, the part of the original RAG paper almost nobody uses anymore is the joint training — modern practice pairs a frozen frontier LLM with an independently trained retriever, and the “training signal” reaching the retriever is a product manager reading evaluation dashboards. The name outlived the method, and then the fixed pipeline itself began yielding to agentic search.

Where this leaves us

  • You can name the three failures of parametric knowledge — frozen, fuzzy, unattributable — and explain how retrieval addresses each one structurally rather than statistically.
  • You can walk the full pipeline — chunk → embed → index → retrieve → rerank → generate — and say what each stage optimizes, what it costs, and the characteristic way it fails.
  • You can derive the contrastive training objective for bi-encoders, explain why hard negatives matter, and explain the bi-encoder/cross-encoder trade-off from the arithmetic of precomputation.
  • You can describe how HNSW finds near neighbors in logarithmic hops via a layered small-world graph, and when brute-force search is honestly fine.
  • You can justify hybrid retrieval (embeddings blur exact identifiers), fuse result lists with RRF by hand, and pick the right query transformation — rewriting, HyDE, decomposition — for a given failure.
  • You can evaluate retrieval (recall@k, MRR) separately from generation (faithfulness, citation checking), and use the split to localize failures.
  • You can argue the RAG vs long-context vs fine-tuning decision as complements — retrieval selects, context holds, fine-tuning shapes behavior — and you know why agent loops with search-as-a-tool superseded fixed pipelines for hard questions.
