Vectors and Spaces

Vectors as lists, arrows, and points; span, basis, and dimension; and the dot product as the similarity measure that powers embeddings and attention.

Open up a modern language model like a Claude or a GPT, and you’ll see lists of numbers, and lists of lists of numbers, flowing through it. Models have vocabularies of “tokens” (more on this later). Tokens are words or, more often, sub-word parts like “un”, “believ”, and “able”. Each token is initially represented by a learned list of a few thousand numbers called embeddings. During training, the model starts with parameters, including these embeddings and the “weights” used in its computations, set to small, random values. The model then repeatedly adjusts these parameters so that it gets better at predicting the next token in text.

As text passes through the model, each layer transforms these lists into new lists of numbers. The intermediate lists are called activations: they are the values produced as the model applies its learned weights and other operations to the input. At any given layer, every token position has its own activation vector (another list), representing what the model currently knows about that token in the context of everything relevant around it.

In Llama 3 8B, for instance, each list has 4,096 entries. In GPT-3 175B, each had 12,288. These lists are called vectors, and you can think of a vector as pointing in a particular direction in a high-dimensional space. In these spaces, the model can represent, compare, combine, and transform information.

That is a critical idea. Much of what a neural network represents can be understood geometrically, as directions in high-dimensional space. In an embedding space, “cat” and “dog” tend to point in more similar directions than “cat” and “carburetor.” Researchers have also found directions that correspond, roughly, to features like plural, sentiment, or is French. These ideas extend far beyond the initial token embeddings: throughout the network, information is encoded in patterns and directions in its internal vector spaces.

When a transformer decides which earlier words to pay attention to, it does so by measuring angles between vectors. When an interpretability researcher probes what a model is “thinking,” they hunt for the direction that encodes it. Many chapters ahead, like embeddings, attention, and features and probes, are variations on this theme.

So this chapter builds the theme’s raw materials from zero. We cover what a vector is, the two operations you can do with vectors, and what “span,” “basis,” and “dimension” mean. And we explain the dot product, which is the core operation that turns geometry into arithmetic and lets a model ask, a billion times per second, “how similar are these two things?”

If your linear algebra is rusty, good — this chapter assumes exactly that. If it’s fresh, skim the definitions but slow down for the deep-learning anchors: they are the reason each piece of math earned its place here.

Three pictures of one object

Ask three different people what a vector is and you will get three different answers, all correct:

The list. To a programmer, a vector is an ordered list of numbers: v=[3,2]v = [3, 2]v=[3,2], or v=[0.12,−1.4,0.007,… ]v = [0.12, -1.4, 0.007, \dots]v=[0.12,−1.4,0.007,…] with 4,096 entries. Each position in the list is called a component or coordinate. This is the view a computer takes — a vector is a 1-D array, nothing more.

The arrow. To a physicist, a vector is an arrow: it has a length and a direction, and it doesn’t care where you draw it. The list [3,2][3, 2][3,2] becomes the arrow that goes 3 units right and 2 units up. Arrows are the view that makes operations visible — adding vectors, scaling them, measuring angles between them all become pictures.

The point. To a data scientist, a vector is a location: the point you reach if you start at the origin (the zero vector, [0,0][0,0][0,0]) and follow the arrow. Under this view a dataset is a cloud of points, and “similar items” means “nearby points.” When we say the embedding of “cat” is near the embedding of “dog,” we are using the point picture.

The skill worth practicing is switching views mid-thought. A model stores a word as a list (that’s what fits in memory), we reason about its meaning as a point (near “dog,” far from “carburetor”), and we compute its similarity to another word using arrow geometry (the angle between them). All three are the same object.

A vector with nnn components is said to live in Rn\R^nRn — “R-n” — the set of all lists of nnn real numbers. R2\R^2R2 is the plane, R3\R^3R3 is the space you walk around in, and R4096\R^{4096}R4096 is where Llama-3-8B keeps its thoughts. We can’t picture R4096\R^{4096}R4096, and we won’t pretend to; instead we build reliable intuition in 2 and 3 dimensions, and in Chapter 3 we’ll study exactly when that intuition holds up in high dimensions and when it betrays us.

💡Intuition

A useful reframe: a vector is a complete description of one thing, one number per attribute. A patient might be [age, blood pressure, heart rate]. A pixel is [red, green, blue]. A word, to a language model, is a list of a few thousand learned attributes — nobody chose them; training discovered them — and two words are related exactly when their attribute lists point the same way. Once everything is a vector, one set of tools (this chapter’s tools) applies to everything.

Adding and scaling: the only two moves

Vector spaces come with exactly two built-in operations, and everything else in linear algebra — everything in this book’s first five chapters — is assembled from them.

Addition is componentwise: add matching entries.

[3,1]+[1,2]=[3+1,  1+2]=[4,3].[3, 1] + [1, 2] = [3+1,\; 1+2] = [4, 3].[3,1]+[1,2]=[3+1,1+2]=[4,3].

In the arrow picture, addition is tip-to-tail: walk along aaa, then walk along bbb from where you stopped. The figure above shows why this is also called the parallelogram rule — walking aaa-then-bbb or bbb-then-aaa lands you at the same corner, which is a picture of the fact that a+b=b+aa + b = b + aa+b=b+a.

Scalar multiplication stretches or shrinks: multiply every component by the same ordinary number (a scalar — “scalar” because it scales).

2.5×[3,1]=[7.5,2.5],−1×[3,1]=[−3,−1].2.5 \times [3, 1] = [7.5, 2.5], \qquad -1 \times [3, 1] = [-3, -1].2.5×[3,1]=[7.5,2.5],−1×[3,1]=[−3,−1].

Geometrically, c⋅vc \cdot vc⋅v keeps the direction of vvv and multiplies its length by ccc; a negative scalar flips the arrow to point the opposite way. Note what scalar multiplication cannot do: it cannot rotate. Every multiple of vvv lies on the same infinite line through the origin.

That is the complete toolkit. There is no built-in vector-times-vector multiplication that returns another vector (the dot product, coming shortly, returns a number), and no division. Any set of objects where these two operations behave sensibly — you can add, you can scale, and the usual algebra rules hold — is called a vector space. Lists of numbers are the example we care about, but functions, polynomials, and images qualify too, and that generality is why the same math runs everything from graphics to large language models (LLMs).

✏️Worked example

A miniature “meaning arithmetic” with 2-dimensional pretend-embeddings. Suppose along dimension 1 our space encodes royalty and along dimension 2 it encodes masculinity, with

king=[0.9, 0.8],man=[0.1, 0.9],woman=[0.1, −0.8].\text{king} = [0.9,\, 0.8], \quad \text{man} = [0.1,\, 0.9], \quad \text{woman} = [0.1,\, -0.8].king=[0.9,0.8],man=[0.1,0.9],woman=[0.1,−0.8].

Then

king−man+woman=[0.9−0.1+0.1,    0.8−0.9−0.8]=[0.9, −0.9],\text{king} - \text{man} + \text{woman} = [0.9 - 0.1 + 0.1,\;\; 0.8 - 0.9 - 0.8] = [0.9,\, -0.9],king−man+woman=[0.9−0.1+0.1,0.8−0.9−0.8]=[0.9,−0.9],

a vector high in royalty and negative in masculinity — a reasonable “queen.” Subtracting man\text{man}man removed the masculine component; adding woman\text{woman}woman installed the opposite one. This is a cartoon, but the famous real result is the same computation in 300 dimensions: word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013) found that king−man+woman\text{king} - \text{man} + \text{woman}king−man+woman lands nearer to queen\text{queen}queen than to any other word in the vocabulary (setting aside the three input words themselves, which the standard evaluation excludes). Directions carried meaning, and nobody programmed them to.

🕰History

Vectors are younger than you might guess. Complex numbers were being drawn as points in a plane by 1800; Hamilton spent the 1840s on quaternions, from whose machinery the words “vector” and “scalar” escaped; Grassmann’s 1844 Ausdehnungslehre had the general theory but was nearly unreadable; and the notation you’re learning here was distilled by Gibbs and Heaviside in the 1880s–1890s for electromagnetism. The idea that word meaning might live in a vector space is older than deep learning too — “you shall know a word by the company it keeps” (Firth, 1957) became 1990s-era counting methods like latent semantic analysis — but it was word2vec in 2013 that made the geometry sharp, cheap, and impossible to ignore.

Linear combinations and span: what can you reach?

Combine the two moves and you get the workhorse concept of linear algebra. A linear combination of vectors v1,…,vkv_1, \dots, v_kv1​,…,vk​ is anything of the form

c1v1+c2v2+⋯+ckvk,c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + \cdots + c_k v_k,c1​v1​+c2​v2​+⋯+ck​vk​,

where the cic_ici​ are scalars called coefficients or weights — scale each ingredient, then add them up. Pause on how much of deep learning is literally this: an artificial neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs (a linear combination) before applying a nonlinearity, as you’ll see in Chapter 8. Attention outputs are weighted sums of value vectors (Chapter 23). “Linear combination” is the single most-executed operation on any graphics processing unit (GPU) on earth.

The span of a set of vectors is the set of everything reachable by linear combinations of them — every destination you can hit by choosing weights. In plain English: span is your budget of directions, and the question “is xxx in the span of {v1,…,vk}\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}{v1​,…,vk​}?” means “can I build xxx out of the ingredients I have?”

Two non-parallel vectors in the plane span the whole plane: any target can be built from some amount of uuu plus some amount of www. But if www happens to be a multiple of uuu — say w=12uw = \tfrac12 uw=21​u — then www contributes nothing new, and the span collapses to a line. This motivates the last vocabulary word of the section: vectors are linearly independent if none of them can be built as a linear combination of the others — no redundancy, every vector adds a new direction to (and thus enlarges) the span. If some vector can be built from the rest, the set is linearly dependent.

💡Intuition

Think of span as a mixing board. Each vector is a slider; the coefficients are the slider positions; the span is every sound the board can produce. Adding a new slider only helps if it can do something the existing sliders can’t — that is linear independence. And a fact we’ll lean on constantly: in Rn\R^nRn you can never have more than nnn independent sliders. The plane has room for two different directions, no more; R4096\R^{4096}R4096 has room for exactly 4,096. (What it does have room for is astonishingly many almost-independent directions — a loophole that Chapter 3 quantifies and superposition shows models exploiting.)

Basis, coordinates, and dimension

A basis of a space is a set of vectors that is (a) linearly independent — no redundancy — and (b) spans the space — reaches everything. It is a minimal complete kit of directions: remove one and you lose part of the space, add one and it’s redundant.

The basis you’ve been using without noticing is the standard basis. In R2\R^2R2 it is e1=[1,0]e_1 = [1, 0]e1​=[1,0] and e2=[0,1]e_2 = [0, 1]e2​=[0,1], and writing v=[3,2]v = [3, 2]v=[3,2] secretly means

v=3 e1+2 e2.v = 3\,e_1 + 2\,e_2.v=3e1​+2e2​.

The numbers “3” and “2” — the components — are the coefficients of vvv in the standard basis. Notably, coordinates are not properties of a vector; they are properties of a vector plus a chosen basis. The arrow is the real object. The list of numbers is a description of the arrow relative to a kit of reference directions, and a different kit gives different numbers for the same arrow.

∑The math

Any basis works as a coordinate system. Take the (non-standard) basis of R2\R^2R2 given by b1=[1,1]b_1 = [1, 1]b1​=[1,1] and b2=[1,−1]b_2 = [1, -1]b2​=[1,−1], and ask: what are the coordinates of v=[3,2]v = [3, 2]v=[3,2] in this basis? We need scalars c1,c2c_1, c_2c1​,c2​ with

c1[1,1]+c2[1,−1]=[3,2],c_1 [1, 1] + c_2 [1, -1] = [3, 2],c1​[1,1]+c2​[1,−1]=[3,2],

which, matching first and second components, is two equations: c1+c2=3c_1 + c_2 = 3c1​+c2​=3 and c1−c2=2c_1 - c_2 = 2c1​−c2​=2. Adding them: 2c1=52c_1 = 52c1​=5, so c1=2.5c_1 = 2.5c1​=2.5; substituting back, c2=0.5c_2 = 0.5c2​=0.5. The same arrow that is "[3,2][3, 2][3,2]" in the standard basis is "[2.5,0.5][2.5, 0.5][2.5,0.5]" in the basis {b1,b2}\{b_1, b_2\}{b1​,b2​}. Nothing about the arrow changed — only the reference kit we described it with.

One more fact, stated without proof because the proof belongs to a linear algebra course: every basis of a given space has the same number of vectors. That shared count is the dimension of the space. R2\R^2R2 has dimension 2 no matter how creatively you choose your basis; Llama-3-8B’s residual stream has dimension 4,096 in every basis anyone could pick.

Why belabor this? Because the distinction between a vector and its coordinates becomes surprisingly important inside neural networks.

Consider an activation space whose coordinates correspond to individual neurons. In that neuron basis, component iii of an activation vector is “how much neuron iii is activating.” It is tempting to imagine that the network therefore organizes its concepts one-per-neuron, with each meaningful feature lined up neatly along one of these coordinate axes.

Sometimes that happens. Individual neurons occasionally have interpretable behavior. But often researchers find something less tidy. Information about a meaningful feature can be distributed across many coordinates, and a feature may be associated with a direction through activation space that cuts across many axes. In coordinates, such a direction is a linear combination of many neurons activations. A feature like “this text is French,” for example, need not have its own dedicated French neuron; information about it may instead be recoverable along some direction through the combined activity of many neurons. And more fundamentally, researchers still have a very incomplete picture of what, exactly, occurs in network internals.

Anthropic’s Toy Models of Superposition (Elhage et al., 2022) demonstrates an important version of this idea in small artificial neural networks. When useful features outnumber available dimensions, a network can represent multiple features using overlapping directions, a phenomenon called superposition. Real language models are vastly more complicated, and researchers still have an incomplete picture of how their internals work.

We will explore interpretability in the final part of this book, and one question will be which directions in this space correspond to meaningful features?

The dot product: geometry becomes arithmetic

Everything so far lets us build and describe vectors. It gives us no way to ask an important question, namely, how strongly do two vectors point in the same direction? Length and angle don’t exist yet, but one formula gives us both.

The dot product (also called the inner product or, in matrix notation you will meet next chapter, a⊤ba\T ba⊤b) of two vectors with the same number of components is: multiply matching components, add everything up.

a⋅b  =  ∑i=1nai bi  =  a1b1+a2b2+⋯+anbn.a \cdot b \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i\, b_i \;=\; a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \cdots + a_n b_n.a⋅b=i=1∑n​ai​bi​=a1​b1​+a2​b2​+⋯+an​bn​.

Note the type signature: two vectors go in, one plain number comes out. For a=[2,1]a = [2, 1]a=[2,1] and b=[1,3]b = [1, 3]b=[1,3]: a⋅b=2⋅1+1⋅3=5a \cdot b = 2\cdot1 + 1\cdot3 = 5a⋅b=2⋅1+1⋅3=5. Cheap, mechanical — and, so far, meaningless. Why this recipe?

Start with a special case. Dot a vector with itself:

v⋅v=v12+v22+⋯+vn2.v \cdot v = v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \cdots + v_n^2.v⋅v=v12​+v22​+⋯+vn2​.

In two dimensions, v12+v22v_1^2 + v_2^2v12​+v22​ is the Pythagorean theorem — it is the squared length of the arrow. Define the norm (length) of a vector as

∥v∥=v⋅v,\|v\| = \sqrt{v \cdot v},∥v∥=v⋅v​,

and the dot product has produced geometry once. Here is the theorem that makes it produce the core geometry we will need:

∑The math

Claim: for any two nonzero vectors, with θ\thetaθ the angle between them,

a⋅b=∥a∥ ∥b∥cos⁡θ.a \cdot b = \|a\|\,\|b\|\cos\theta.a⋅b=∥a∥∥b∥cosθ.

Derivation. Compute the squared length of the difference a−ba - ba−b two ways.

Algebraically, the dot product distributes over addition and doesn’t care about order (both facts you can check directly from the sum formula — each component obeys ordinary arithmetic), so we can expand it like a product of binomials:

∥a−b∥2=(a−b)⋅(a−b)=a⋅a−2 a⋅b+b⋅b=∥a∥2+∥b∥2−2 a⋅b.\|a - b\|^2 = (a-b)\cdot(a-b) = a \cdot a - 2\, a\cdot b + b \cdot b = \|a\|^2 + \|b\|^2 - 2\,a \cdot b.∥a−b∥2=(a−b)⋅(a−b)=a⋅a−2a⋅b+b⋅b=∥a∥2+∥b∥2−2a⋅b.

Geometrically, the three arrows aaa, bbb, and a−ba - ba−b form a triangle: a−ba - ba−b is the side connecting the tips of aaa and bbb, and the angle facing that side is θ\thetaθ. The law of cosines — Pythagoras with a correction term for non-right triangles — says exactly

∥a−b∥2=∥a∥2+∥b∥2−2 ∥a∥ ∥b∥cos⁡θ.\|a - b\|^2 = \|a\|^2 + \|b\|^2 - 2\,\|a\|\,\|b\|\cos\theta.∥a−b∥2=∥a∥2+∥b∥2−2∥a∥∥b∥cosθ.

Two expressions for the same quantity. Set them equal, cancel ∥a∥2+∥b∥2\|a\|^2 + \|b\|^2∥a∥2+∥b∥2 from both sides, divide by −2-2−2:

a⋅b=∥a∥ ∥b∥cos⁡θ.■a \cdot b = \|a\|\,\|b\|\cos\theta. \qquad \blacksquarea⋅b=∥a∥∥b∥cosθ.■

Sit with what was proved. The left side is blind arithmetic on lists — no angles anywhere in sight. The right side is pure geometry — lengths and an angle, no coordinates anywhere in sight. They are always equal. That means a computer that can only multiply and add numbers can nonetheless measure the angle between two internal representations, in 4,096 dimensions, without ever drawing anything. This identity is the bridge on which all of deep learning’s geometry crosses into silicon.

There’s an equivalent picture worth having. Since ∥a∥cos⁡θ\|a\|\cos\theta∥a∥cosθ is the length of the shadow aaa casts on bbb‘s direction (drop a perpendicular from aaa‘s tip onto bbb‘s line), the theorem reads:

a⋅b=∥b∥×(signed length of a’s shadow on b).a \cdot b = \|b\| \times (\text{signed length of } a\text{'s shadow on } b).a⋅b=∥b∥×(signed length of a’s shadow on b).

“Signed” because if aaa leans against bbb‘s direction (θ\thetaθ greater than 90°), the shadow points backward and counts as negative.

Either way you read it, the dot product is an agreement meter:

  • Pointing the same way (θ=0\theta = 0θ=0, cos⁡θ=1\cos\theta = 1cosθ=1) for fixed lengths: maximal positive value, ∥a∥∥b∥\|a\|\|b\|∥a∥∥b∥.
  • Perpendicular (θ=90°\theta = 90°θ=90°, cos⁡θ=0\cos\theta = 0cosθ=0): exactly zero, regardless of lengths.
  • Opposite (θ=180°\theta = 180°θ=180°, cos⁡θ=−1\cos\theta = -1cosθ=−1) for fixed lengths: the smallest possible dot product -∥a∥∥b∥\|a\|\|b\|∥a∥∥b∥.

Take a=[2,1]a = [2, 1]a=[2,1] and b=[1,3]b = [1, 3]b=[1,3] and check the theorem by hand, both ways.

⚙️In practice

When a transformer’s attention head (you’ll learn this later) decides how much the position containing “it” should attend to the position containing “cat,” it produces a dot product between a query vector (q) derived from the current representation of “it” and a key vector (k) derived from the current representation of “cat.” A larger dot product gives that pair a larger attention score. The scores are then scaled and passed through softmax, so every candidate token competes for attention. The full recipe (including why the score gets divided by d\sqrt{d}d​ — a high-dimensional correction this chapter has quietly set up) is Chapter 23. Likewise, when a retrieval-augmented system (Chapter 45) searches millions of documents for ones relevant to your question, a common approach is to embed the question as a vector and search for stored vectors with the highest similarity. That similarity is often a dot product or cosine similarity. Vector databases use specialized indexes to find the closest vectors without exhaustively comparing against every stored vector.

Cosine similarity: direction without length

The raw dot product blends two signals: how aligned the vectors are, and how long they are. a⋅b=∥a∥∥b∥cos⁡θa \cdot b = \|a\|\|b\|\cos\thetaa⋅b=∥a∥∥b∥cosθ gets big if the angle is small — or if either vector happens to be long. Often we want the alignment signal alone. Divide the lengths out:

cosine similarity(a,b)  =  a⋅b∥a∥ ∥b∥  =  cos⁡θ.\text{cosine similarity}(a, b) \;=\; \frac{a \cdot b}{\|a\|\,\|b\|} \;=\; \cos\theta.cosine similarity(a,b)=∥a∥∥b∥a⋅b​=cosθ.

This is a pure direction comparison, always between −1-1−1 and +1+1+1: it is +1+1+1 when the vectors point identically (even if one is a thousand times longer), 000 when perpendicular, −1-1−1 when opposed. When you read that two embeddings have “similarity 0.83,” this is almost always the number being quoted.

✏️Worked example

Three toy word embeddings in R4\R^4R4 (real ones have thousands of components; four is enough to see the machinery):

cat=[0.9, 0.8, 0.1, 0.2],dog=[0.8, 0.9, 0.2, 0.1],car=[0.1, 0.2, 0.9, 0.8].\text{cat} = [0.9,\, 0.8,\, 0.1,\, 0.2], \quad \text{dog} = [0.8,\, 0.9,\, 0.2,\, 0.1], \quad \text{car} = [0.1,\, 0.2,\, 0.9,\, 0.8].cat=[0.9,0.8,0.1,0.2],dog=[0.8,0.9,0.2,0.1],car=[0.1,0.2,0.9,0.8].

Dot products: cat⋅dog=0.72+0.72+0.02+0.02=1.48\text{cat} \cdot \text{dog} = 0.72 + 0.72 + 0.02 + 0.02 = 1.48cat⋅dog=0.72+0.72+0.02+0.02=1.48, while cat⋅car=0.09+0.16+0.09+0.16=0.50\text{cat} \cdot \text{car} = 0.09 + 0.16 + 0.09 + 0.16 = 0.50cat⋅car=0.09+0.16+0.09+0.16=0.50.

Norms: ∥cat∥=0.81+0.64+0.01+0.04=1.50≈1.225\|\text{cat}\| = \sqrt{0.81 + 0.64 + 0.01 + 0.04} = \sqrt{1.50} \approx 1.225∥cat∥=0.81+0.64+0.01+0.04​=1.50​≈1.225, and by symmetry of the numbers ∥dog∥\|\text{dog}\|∥dog∥ and ∥car∥\|\text{car}\|∥car∥ come out the same.

Cosine similarities: cat/dog=1.48/1.50≈0.99\text{cat/dog} = 1.48 / 1.50 \approx 0.99cat/dog=1.48/1.50≈0.99 (nearly parallel — the model treats them as close kin), and cat/car=0.50/1.50≈0.33\text{cat/car} = 0.50 / 1.50 \approx 0.33cat/car=0.50/1.50≈0.33 (loosely related — both are nouns you can own, perhaps — but far from synonyms). This is precisely how similarity search over real embeddings works, with the arithmetic running over 1,536 or 3,072 components instead of four.

⚠️Careful

Raw dot product and cosine similarity answer different questions, and mixing them up produces real bugs. A document embedding with a huge norm can post a larger dot product against your query than a shorter, better-aligned document — length masquerading as relevance. In practice, embedding pipelines usually normalize every vector to length 1 (divide vvv by ∥v∥\|v\|∥v∥), after which the two measures coincide: for unit vectors, a⋅b=cos⁡θa \cdot b = \cos\thetaa⋅b=cosθ exactly. But note that norm is not always noise to be scrubbed away — inside transformers, the norm of a residual-stream vector carries real signal (roughly, how strongly the model is asserting whatever it is asserting), which is one reason the normalization layers of Chapter 14 rescale rather than discard it.

Orthogonality: zero geometric overlap

One special case of the dot product is important enough to have its own name. Two nonzero vectors aaa and bbb are orthogonal when they meet at a right angle:

a⋅b=0.a \cdot b = 0.a⋅b=0.

The dot product lets us detect this without drawing anything. For example,

[2,1]⋅[−1,2]=−2+2=0,[2,1] \cdot [-1,2] = -2 + 2 = 0,[2,1]⋅[−1,2]=−2+2=0,

so the two vectors are orthogonal.

The projection picture makes the meaning especially clear. Recall that

a⋅b=∥b∥×(signed length of a’s projection onto b).a \cdot b = \|b\| \times (\text{signed length of }a\text{'s projection onto }b).a⋅b=∥b∥×(signed length of a’s projection onto b).

If aaa and bbb are orthogonal, that projection has length zero. In geometric terms, aaa has no component pointing in bbb‘s direction.

This is one reason orthonormal bases are so convenient. A basis is orthonormal when all of its basis vectors have length 1 and are mutually orthogonal. In the standard basis of Rn\R^nRn,

e1=[1,0,…,0],e2=[0,1,…,0],…e_1=[1,0,\ldots,0],\quad e_2=[0,1,\ldots,0],\quad \ldotse1​=[1,0,…,0],e2​=[0,1,…,0],…

and a dot product extracts a coordinate directly:

v⋅ei=vi.v \cdot e_i = v_i.v⋅ei​=vi​.

More generally, if ddd is any unit vector, then

v⋅dv \cdot dv⋅d

is the signed amount of vvv pointing in direction ddd. This simple idea will reappear throughout the book. Linear probes, for example, often learn a direction in an activation space whose dot product with an activation helps predict whether some property is present.

⚠️Careful

Geometric orthogonality should not be confused with statistical independence or semantic unrelatedness. Two directions can have dot product zero while variables associated with them remain statistically related. Orthogonality makes a precise geometric statement: each direction has zero projection onto the other.

There is one high-dimensional fact worth carrying forward. In Rn\R^nRn, at most nnn nonzero directions can be mutually orthogonal. But high-dimensional spaces contain enormous numbers of directions that are nearly orthogonal — their normalized dot products are close to zero even though they are not exactly perpendicular.

That fact becomes important when we study high-dimensional geometry and superposition: a model can make use of far more approximately separated directions than the dimension count alone might suggest.

A brief note on other norms

So far, whenever we have written ∥v∥\|v\|∥v∥, we have meant ordinary Euclidean length:

∥v∥2=v12+v22+⋯+vn2=v⋅v.\|v\|_2 = \sqrt{v_1^2+v_2^2+\cdots+v_n^2} = \sqrt{v\cdot v}.∥v∥2​=v12​+v22​+⋯+vn2​​=v⋅v​.

The subscript 2 matters because this is only one possible way to measure the size of a vector. Another common choice is the L1 norm:

∥v∥1=∣v1∣+∣v2∣+⋯+∣vn∣.\|v\|_1 = |v_1|+|v_2|+\cdots+|v_n|.∥v∥1​=∣v1​∣+∣v2​∣+⋯+∣vn​∣.

For example, if v=[3,−4]v=[3,-4]v=[3,−4],

∥v∥2=5,∥v∥1=7.\|v\|_2=5, \qquad \|v\|_1=7.∥v∥2​=5,∥v∥1​=7.

Both are called norms. They answer slightly different versions of the question “how large is this vector?”

You do not need the general theory of norms yet. What matters is recognizing the two names, because both appear repeatedly in deep learning.

The L2 norm is the ordinary geometric length we have used throughout this chapter. It also appears when controlling the scale of weights, clipping large gradients, and normalizing activations. RMSNorm, used in many modern transformers, is based on

1n∑i=1nvi2,\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n v_i^2},n1​i=1∑n​vi2​​,

which differs from the L2 norm only by the factor 1/n1/\sqrt n1/n​ (and, in practice, a small numerical-stability term).

The L1 norm becomes especially useful when we want many components to be exactly or nearly zero. Penalizing L1 norm tends to encourage sparse representations, which is why L1 penalties appear in methods such as the sparse autoencoders used in interpretability.

We will return to why L1 encourages sparsity when optimization and regularization give us the machinery to explain it. For now, remember the distinction:

∥v∥2=sum of squares,∥v∥1=sum of absolute values.\|v\|_2 = \sqrt{\text{sum of squares}}, \qquad \|v\|_1 = \text{sum of absolute values}.∥v∥2​=sum of squares​,∥v∥1​=sum of absolute values.

Where this leaves us

  • You can read a vector three ways — list, arrow, point — and switch views to match the task, in R2\R^2R2 or R4096\R^{4096}R4096.
  • You can add and scale vectors, and you know every richer construction (neurons, attention outputs) is built from those two moves as linear combinations.
  • You can say precisely what span (reachability), linear independence (no redundancy), basis (minimal complete direction kit), and dimension (size of any basis) mean — and you know coordinates depend on the basis, which is why “which basis is meaningful?” will be a live scientific question in Part VII.
  • You can compute a⋅ba \cdot ba⋅b two ways, and you can derive why the arithmetic ∑iaibi\sum_i a_i b_i∑i​ai​bi​ equals the geometry ∥a∥∥b∥cos⁡θ\|a\|\|b\|\cos\theta∥a∥∥b∥cosθ.
  • You can choose between raw dot product and cosine similarity, and say when length should count and when it shouldn’t.
  • You can picture L1 and L2 norms by their unit balls, and explain from the diamond’s corners why L1 pressure produces sparsity.
  • You can recognize orthogonality as zero dot product, use it for clean readouts, and you know the almost-orthogonal loophole that high dimensions offer.
