Compute: GPUs, Precision, and Parallelism

What a GPU actually is, why bf16 won, how 16,000 chips train one model, and what a frontier run costs — with the arithmetic done in front of you.

Every chapter so far has treated compute as an abstraction: “we multiply these matrices,” “we take a gradient step,” as if the arithmetic simply happens. But a frontier training run is 102510^{25}1025 floating-point operations — if you performed one multiply-add per second by hand, you would need about ten million times the age of the universe. The story of modern AI is inseparable from the story of the machines that make that number achievable in two months instead: what they are good at, what they are bad at, and the increasingly baroque tricks required to keep tens of thousands of them busy on a single model.

This chapter is hardware for someone who has never touched a GPU (graphics processing unit). We will build up in four layers. First, why GPUs: what property of neural networks makes a graphics chip the right tool. Second, what a GPU is inside: compute units, memory, and the single most important idea in ML systems — that moving bytes is usually more expensive than doing math on them. Third, numbers themselves: what a floating-point number is made of, why the field abandoned 32-bit precision, and how far down the bit-count ladder training has been pushed. Fourth, parallelism: the five distinct ways to split one model across many chips, and how real runs — Llama 3 405B on 16,384 GPUs, DeepSeek-V3 on 2,048 — compose them. We end with money: what a run costs, and why one $5.6M line item in a December 2024 technical report briefly moved markets.

None of this is optional culture for practitioners. The concepts here — arithmetic intensity, the memory ledger, the communication cost of each parallelism — are the reasons behind design choices you have already seen (grouped-query attention, mixture-of-experts) and ones still to come (quantized inference). Hardware is where the field’s aesthetics come from.

Why GPUs: throughput over latency

Start with what a neural network spends its time doing. A forward pass through a transformer is overwhelmingly matrix multiplication: activations times weight matrices, queries times keys, attention weights times values. And a matrix multiplication is nothing but a very large pile of independent multiply-add operations. To compute C=ABC = ABC=AB where all matrices are n×nn \times nn×n, you need n2n^2n2 output entries, each an inner product of nnn multiplies and nnn adds — about 2n32n^32n3 operations total — and every output entry can be computed without waiting for any other. There are no branches, no data-dependent decisions, no “if”. Perfectly parallel, perfectly predictable.

CPUs (central processing units) are the wrong shape for this workload. A CPU is a latency machine: it is designed to finish one unpredictable task — parse this file, chase these pointers, branch on that condition — as fast as possible. To do that it spends most of its silicon on things that are not arithmetic: branch predictors, out-of-order execution machinery, large caches to hide memory latency. A modern server CPU has perhaps 64–128 powerful cores.

A GPU is a throughput machine. It answers a different question: not “how fast can you finish one task?” but “how many identical tasks can you finish per second?” It strips out the cleverness and spends the silicon on arithmetic units instead — tens of thousands of small, simple cores that all execute the same instruction on different data (the style is called SIMT, single instruction, multiple threads — a close cousin of SIMD, single instruction, multiple data). Each individual operation finishes slower than it would on a CPU. Collectively, the GPU does thousands of times more of them per second. Graphics needed exactly this (every pixel gets the same shading math), which is why the hardware existed years before deep learning found it.

💡Intuition

A CPU is a sports car: it gets one passenger anywhere fast, and it handles surprise turns well. A GPU is a freight train: terrible at surprise turns, unbeatable at moving a million identical containers down a straight track. Neural network training is a straight track — the same multiply-add, repeated 102510^{25}1025 times with no decisions in between. You want the train.

The fit between deep learning and GPUs is not a lucky coincidence you should take for granted — it actively shaped the field. Architectures that map to big dense matmuls (transformers) thrived; architectures that need sequential, branchy computation (RNNs, with their step-by-step recurrence) withered. When you read that attention replaced recurrence, part of the true causal story is “attention is a batch of matmuls, and matmuls are what the hardware wanted.”

Inside a GPU: compute, memory, and the wall between them

Open up an NVIDIA H100 — the workhorse chip of the 2023–2025 era — and you find two very different worlds connected by a bridge that is never wide enough.

The compute side. The chip is organized into 132 streaming multiprocessors (SMs) — think of each SM as a small self-contained processor with its own scheduler, registers, and arithmetic units. Inside each SM, alongside ordinary arithmetic units, sit four tensor cores: specialized circuits that do one thing, a small dense matrix multiply-accumulate (roughly a 4×44\times44×4-scale tile per clock cycle), in one gulp. Tensor cores are why GPU specs quote such absurd numbers for matmul throughput specifically: an H100 delivers roughly 989 trillion floating-point operations per second (teraFLOPs, TFLOPs) on bf16 matmuls through its tensor cores, versus about 67 TFLOPs for ordinary fp32 arithmetic on the general-purpose units. If your computation is not a matmul, you forfeit more than 90% of the chip.

The memory side. The model’s weights and activations live in HBM (high-bandwidth memory) — 80 GB of it on an H100, physically stacked next to the die — readable at about 3.35 terabytes per second (TB/s). On the chip itself sits a much smaller, much faster hierarchy of SRAM (static RAM): a roughly 50 MB L2 cache shared by all SMs, and about 228 KB of scratchpad (“shared memory”) per SM, accessible at an aggregate rate an order of magnitude beyond HBM. The pattern to internalize: each level going outward is roughly a thousand times bigger and roughly ten times slower.

The memory-bandwidth wall and arithmetic intensity

Here is the tension that dominates all of ML systems engineering. Over the last decade, peak FLOPs per chip grew far faster than memory bandwidth per chip. An H100 can perform about 989 TFLOPs of bf16 matmul, but can only pull about 3.35 TB/s from HBM. Divide those two numbers:

989×1012 FLOPs/s3.35×1012 bytes/s≈295 FLOPs per byte.\frac{989 \times 10^{12}\ \text{FLOPs/s}}{3.35 \times 10^{12}\ \text{bytes/s}} \approx 295\ \text{FLOPs per byte}.3.35×1012 bytes/s989×1012 FLOPs/s​≈295 FLOPs per byte.

This ratio is the chip’s ridge point: to keep the arithmetic units fed, your computation must perform roughly 295 floating-point operations for every byte it reads from memory. Do fewer, and the tensor cores sit idle waiting for data — you are memory-bound. Do more, and memory keeps up while the arithmetic units are the bottleneck — you are compute-bound, which is where you want to be, since arithmetic is what you paid for.

The number of operations a computation performs per byte of memory traffic is called its arithmetic intensity, and plotting achievable performance against it gives the classic “roofline” model — flat memory-bound slope on the left, flat compute ceiling on the right, meeting at the ridge. You do not need the plot; you need the two regimes.

∑The math

Compare a matrix–vector product against a matrix–matrix product, both with an n×nn \times nn×n weight matrix WWW stored in bf16 (2 bytes per number).

Matrix–vector (y=Wxy = Wxy=Wx, the shape of generating one token at inference): 2n22n^22n2 FLOPs (one multiply and one add per weight), but the whole matrix must be read: 2n22n^22n2 bytes. Arithmetic intensity:

2n2 FLOPs2n2 bytes=1 FLOP/byte.\frac{2n^2\ \text{FLOPs}}{2n^2\ \text{bytes}} = 1\ \text{FLOP/byte}.2n2 bytes2n2 FLOPs​=1 FLOP/byte.

One! Against a ridge of 295. Peak achievable throughput is bandwidth times intensity: 3.35 TB/s×1≈3.353.35\ \text{TB/s} \times 1 \approx 3.353.35 TB/s×1≈3.35 TFLOPs — 0.3% of the chip’s peak. Every weight is used exactly once per read, so no cleverness can save you; the computation is irreducibly memory-bound.

Matrix–matrix (C=ABC = ABC=AB with all matrices n×nn \times nn×n, the shape of training and of processing prompts in batch): 2n32n^32n3 FLOPs against 3×2n23 \times 2n^23×2n2 bytes of reads and writes, so intensity ≈n/3\approx n/3≈n/3 FLOPs/byte. At n=4096n = 4096n=4096, that is about 1365 — comfortably past the ridge. Each weight fetched from memory is reused across every column of BBB, and reuse is exactly what arithmetic intensity measures.

💡Intuition

Think of HBM as a warehouse and the tensor cores as a kitchen. Matvec is a recipe that uses each ingredient once: your chefs spend the day waiting on forklifts. Matmul reuses every ingredient thousands of times once it is on the counter: now the forklifts can barely keep the chefs busy, which is the good problem. Nearly every efficiency trick in this book — batching, the KV cache, FlashAttention’s SRAM tiling, quantized inference — is a scheme to raise arithmetic intensity or shrink the bytes.

This single concept will pay off twice more in the book: it is why inference is memory-bound and batching fixes it, and why the KV cache chapter cared so much about shrinking cache bytes. When a systems person says “that kernel is bandwidth-bound,” this arithmetic is the entire content of the claim.

The hardware landscape, 2026

You need a rough map of the chips, because papers name them the way historians name dynasties. All numbers below are approximate, marketing-adjacent, and fast-moving — treat them as orders of magnitude, not specs to cite. (A PFLOP, petaFLOP, is a thousand TFLOPs.)

The pattern across generations: roughly 222–3×3\times3× the matmul FLOPs each step, with memory bandwidth growing slower — the wall from the previous section gets taller every generation, and each generation adds hardware support for one lower precision (A100 made bf16 mainstream, H100 added fp8 tensor cores, B200 added fp4). The GB200 pairs two B200s with an ARM CPU (“Grace”) on one board; the NVL72 rack wires 72 Blackwell GPUs into a single NVLink domain that software can treat almost like one giant GPU. You will also meet the H800 — the export-controlled H100 variant sold in China with deliberately reduced interconnect bandwidth, a constraint that, as we will see, shaped DeepSeek’s entire systems design.

GPUs are not the only game. Google’s TPUs (tensor processing units, now in their sixth and seventh generations) take the specialization further: the core is a large systolic array — a grid of multiply-add units that data flows through in lockstep — surrounded by proprietary inter-chip interconnect that makes very large pods (thousands of chips) look flat to software. Gemini models train on TPUs; Anthropic trains across TPUs, Amazon’s Trainium, and GPUs. The concepts in this chapter transfer wholesale — TPUs face the same bandwidth wall and use the same parallelism taxonomy, often with cleaner scaling because the interconnect was co-designed with the workload.

Interconnect is the third spec that matters, and for the rest of this chapter it matters more than FLOPs. Within one server node, 8 GPUs talk over NVLink through NVSwitch chips at roughly 900 GB/s per GPU (H100 generation; Blackwell doubles it). Between nodes, traffic crosses InfiniBand (or increasingly RoCE Ethernet) at roughly 50–100 GB/s per GPU. Note the cliff: a GPU can talk to its 7 node-mates about 10× faster than to any GPU in another node, and to its own HBM about 4× faster still. Every parallelism decision below is downstream of this cliff — which computations must sit within a node, and which can tolerate the slow hop.

What a number is: fp32, fp16, bf16, fp8

Training arithmetic happens on floating-point numbers, and the choice of format — how many bits, arranged how — turns out to be one of the highest- leverage decisions in the whole stack. Halve the bits and you double the effective memory capacity, double the effective bandwidth (same bytes move twice the numbers), and, on tensor cores, roughly double the FLOPs. The question is what you break.

A floating-point number is scientific notation in binary: a sign bit, an exponent field (eee bits, setting the range — how large and small you can represent), and a mantissa field (mmm bits, setting the precision — how many significant digits you keep). The value is roughly ± 1.mantissa×2exponent−bias\pm\, 1.\text{mantissa} \times 2^{\text{exponent} - \text{bias}}±1.mantissa×2exponent−bias.

Why bf16 won

The first attempt at half-precision training used fp16 (1 sign, 5 exponent, 10 mantissa bits), and the mixed precision training paper documents the pain: with only 5 exponent bits, fp16 cannot represent anything larger than 65,504 or (normally) much smaller than 6×10−56 \times 10^{-5}6×10−5. Gradients in a deep network routinely live outside that window, so values silently became zero or infinity, and training required loss scaling — multiply the loss by, say, 1024 before backprop to shove gradients into range, divide by 1024 afterward, and adjust the factor dynamically when it guesses wrong. Workable, fragile, and a recurring source of mysterious divergences.

bf16 (“brain float 16”, designed at Google Brain for TPUs) makes the opposite trade: keep fp32’s full 8 exponent bits, drop the mantissa to 7 bits. Same range as fp32 — nothing overflows that would not have overflowed anyway — at the cost of only about 3 significant decimal digits. Neural network training tolerates noisy precision well and clipped range poorly: gradient descent averages away rounding noise over millions of steps, but a single overflow-to-infinity poisons the run. Once A100s supported bf16 natively (2020), loss scaling went in the bin, and bf16 has been the default training format at every major lab since.

The mixed-precision recipe and the memory ledger

“Training in bf16” does not mean everything is bf16. The standard recipe, largely unchanged since 2017 apart from the fp16→bf16 swap: do the expensive things (matmuls, activations) in bf16, but keep a master copy of the weights in fp32, along with the Adam optimizer’s two moment estimates in fp32, and apply weight updates to the master copy. The reason is a rounding subtlety: a single gradient step changes a weight by perhaps one part in 10510^5105, and with only 3 decimal digits of precision, adding such a tiny increment to a bf16 weight rounds back to the original value — the update would silently vanish. Tensor cores also accumulate their inner products in fp32 internally for the same reason: summing thousands of terms in bf16 loses the small ones.

This one multiplication explains why the rest of this chapter has to exist.

fp8 and the 4-bit frontier

H100 tensor cores added fp8 in two flavors: E4M3 (4 exponent, 3 mantissa bits — more precision) and E5M2 (5 exponent, 2 mantissa — more range). With at most 3 mantissa bits and a range down to about ±448\pm 448±448 (E4M3), fp8 is far too crude to use naively; every tensor needs a scaling factor that maps its actual values into the representable window, applied and unapplied around each matmul.

For two years fp8 training was a demos-and-papers curiosity, until DeepSeek-V3 (December 2024) trained a 671B-parameter frontier model with fp8 matmuls end to end. Their key moves: fine-grained scaling factors — one per 1×1281\times1281×128 tile of activations and per 128×128128\times128128×128 block of weights, rather than one per tensor, so a single outlier value cannot wreck the scale for millions of neighbors — and periodic promotion of tensor-core partial sums into full-fp32 accumulators (they found the H800’s internal accumulation precision insufficient on its own). Weights’ master copies and optimizer states stayed in higher precision, as always. The payoff: roughly double the matmul throughput and nearly half the activation memory of bf16, at a measured loss penalty they report as under 0.25% relative — effectively free compute. Blackwell hardware bets on the next rung, fp4, with native “microscaling” formats (a shared scale per small block of values baked into the format). As of 2026, 4-bit inference is routine (see quantization), 4-bit training is a research frontier with promising papers and no public frontier-scale run — treat claims here as provisional.

⚠️Careful

Lower precision is not free just because the loss curve matches for the first trillion tokens. Precision-related failures are insidious: they surface as slightly worse late-stage stability, rare divergence spikes, or degradation on narrow capabilities, and they interact with everything else (normalization, optimizer choice, learning-rate schedule). This is why each halving of bits has taken the field years to trust, and why the burden of proof sits with the smaller format.

Making it fit: checkpointing and ZeRO

Before splitting the model across GPUs, two techniques stretch what fits on each one.

Activation checkpointing (also “gradient checkpointing”; Chen et al., 2016) attacks the memory that the ledger above left out. Backpropagation needs the forward pass’s intermediate activations to compute gradients, and for a transformer processing big batches these dwarf the weights: every layer’s attention scores, MLP intermediates (4× the hidden width), normalization statistics, all held hostage until the backward pass arrives. The fix is to not keep them: store only the activations at layer boundaries, throw away everything inside each layer, and recompute the interior values on the fly during the backward pass by re-running that layer’s forward computation.

✏️Worked example

A 32-layer model, hidden width 4096, and a 16,384-token microbatch in bf16. Checkpointed boundaries: 32×16,384×4096×232 \times 16{,}384 \times 4096 \times 232×16,384×4096×2 bytes ≈4.3\approx 4.3≈4.3 GB. The discarded interiors — attention matrices, the 4×4\times4×-wide MLP activations — are roughly an order of magnitude larger, so checkpointing turns ~40+ GB of activation memory into ~4 GB plus recompute. The price: one extra forward pass per step. Since the backward pass costs about twice the forward pass, a step goes from 1+2=31 + 2 = 31+2=3 forward-equivalents to 1+1+2=41 + 1 + 2 = 41+1+2=4: about 33% more compute for roughly 10× less activation memory. Nearly every large run takes this trade on some or all layers.

ZeRO (“Zero Redundancy Optimizer,” Rajbhandari et al., 2019) attacks the ledger itself, with an observation of almost embarrassing simplicity. Plain data parallelism (next section) keeps a full copy of all 16 bytes/param on every GPU — 64 GPUs training a 7B model store 64 identical copies of 112 GB. Why? Each GPU only updates the weights once per step; the copies exist for convenience, not necessity. So: shard the state across the data-parallel group, and gather pieces on demand.

  • Stage 1 shards the optimizer states (the fp32 master weights and Adam moments — 12 of the 16 bytes). Each of NNN GPUs owns the Adam update for 1/N1/N1/N of the parameters. Per-GPU cost: 4+12/N4 + 12/N4+12/N bytes/param. The communication pattern barely changes — this stage is nearly free, and it is what DeepSeek-V3 used.
  • Stage 2 also shards the bf16 gradients: 2+14/N2 + 14/N2+14/N bytes/param.
  • Stage 3 shards the weights themselves: 16/N16/N16/N bytes/param. Now no GPU holds the whole model, ever; before each layer runs, its weights are broadcast (all-gathered) from their owners just in time, used, and freed. The cost is that extra all-gather traffic every step, forward and backward.

PyTorch’s built-in implementation of stage-3-style sharding is FSDP (fully sharded data parallel), and “FSDP” has become the generic verb the way ZeRO-3 never quite did. At 64 GPUs, stage 3 turns 112 GB per GPU into about 1.75 GB — the memory problem simply dissolves into the cluster, in exchange for keeping the interconnect humming.

The parallelism zoo

Now the main event: five distinct ways to split work across GPUs. Keep your eye on one question for each — what has to be communicated, how often, and over which link? That question, plus the NVLink-vs-InfiniBand cliff from earlier, determines everything.

Data parallelism: split the batch

The oldest and most natural: put a complete copy of the model on every GPU, give each GPU a different slice of the batch, run forward and backward independently, then all-reduce the gradients — a collective operation that sums every GPU’s gradient tensor and leaves the identical sum on all of them — so that every copy applies the same update and stays synchronized. It is exactly the math of a bigger batch, executed on more hardware; nothing about the optimization changes.

What is communicated: one gradient’s worth of bytes per step. An efficient ring all-reduce moves about 2×2\times2× the tensor size through each GPU’s network port, so a 7B model in bf16 gradients means roughly 28 GB in and out per GPU per step — chunky, but it happens once per step and overlaps with the backward pass computing later layers’ gradients while earlier ones are already in flight. Data parallelism tolerates slow interconnect well, scales to thousands of GPUs, and has one hard limit: the whole model (plus its 16 bytes/param, minus whatever ZeRO shards away) must fit on each GPU — and one soft limit: the global batch size grows with GPU count, and batches can only get so large before optimization quality degrades.

Tensor parallelism: split the matmul

When one layer is too big — or one GPU too slow — for a single device, Megatron-LM showed how to split individual weight matrices. Take the transformer MLP’s first matrix W1W_1W1​ (shape h×4hh \times 4hh×4h) and cut it column-wise across 2 GPUs: each holds an h×2hh \times 2hh×2h slab, each computes XW1(i)X W_1^{(i)}XW1(i)​ on the same input, producing half the intermediate features. The elementwise activation applies locally. Then cut the second matrix W2W_2W2​ (4h×h4h \times h4h×h) row-wise so each GPU’s half-features meet the matching rows; each produces a partial sum of the final output, and one all-reduce over the activations adds the halves. Attention splits even more naturally: with multiple heads, each GPU simply owns a subset of heads, with the same one-all-reduce-at-the-end structure. Two all-reduces per layer per direction, forward and backward.

That is the catch: where data parallelism communicates once per step, tensor parallelism communicates every layer, in the critical path — the matmul after the all-reduce cannot start until it arrives. This only works over NVLink-class bandwidth, which is why tensor parallel degree is almost always ≤8\le 8≤8: exactly the GPUs inside one node. TP is the reason the “8 GPUs per node” shape is load-bearing in every lab’s infrastructure.

Pipeline parallelism: split the layers

Assign consecutive layers to different GPUs — GPU 0 holds layers 1–8, GPU 1 holds 9–16, and so on — and pass activations from stage to stage like an assembly line. Communication is gentle: one activation tensor per boundary, between fixed pairs of GPUs, entirely tolerable over InfiniBand. Memory divides cleanly by the number of stages.

The problem is the bubble. Send one batch through 4 stages and at any moment 3 of the 4 GPUs are idle — first waiting for work to arrive, then waiting for the backward pass to come back through. GPipe’s fix: chop the batch into mmm microbatches and stream them, so stage 2 works on microbatch 1 while stage 1 starts microbatch 2. The pipeline still has to fill and drain at the edges of every step, and that idle fraction is

bubble fraction  =  p−1m+p−1,\text{bubble fraction} \;=\; \frac{p - 1}{m + p - 1},bubble fraction=m+p−1p−1​,

where ppp is the number of stages and mmm the number of microbatches (in the simple schedule; refinements change constants, not the shape).

✏️Worked example

p=16p = 16p=16 stages and m=64m = 64m=64 microbatches: bubble =15/79≈19%= 15/79 \approx 19\%=15/79≈19% — one fifth of the cluster-seconds spent idle, before any other inefficiency. Double the microbatches to 128: 15/142≈11%15/142 \approx 11\%15/142≈11%. The lever is more microbatches, but each must be large enough to keep matmuls efficient, and the global batch size budget caps the product. Schedulers like 1F1B (one-forward-one-backward), interleaved stages, and DeepSeek’s DualPipe exist precisely to shrink this number — DualPipe overlaps the fill/drain phases with the other direction’s compute and folds communication into the bubble.

Expert parallelism: split the experts

For mixture-of-experts (MoE) models the natural cut is by expert: with 256 experts and expert-parallel degree 64, each GPU hosts 4 experts. The communication is unlike anything above: after the router assigns each token to its experts, tokens must physically travel to the GPUs holding those experts and their outputs must travel back — an all-to-all exchange, twice per MoE layer, whose traffic pattern depends on the router’s whims. This irregular, latency-sensitive chatter is the systems tax MoE pays for its FLOP savings, and taming it (via node-limited routing — DeepSeek-V3 caps each token to at most 4 nodes — and custom communication kernels) is a large fraction of any MoE paper’s systems section.

Context parallelism: split the sequence

The newest member, for long contexts: shard the sequence dimension itself, so GPU 0 holds tokens 1–32k of a document, GPU 1 holds 32k–64k, and so on. MLPs are per-token and need nothing; attention is the hard part, since queries must see keys and values living on other GPUs. Schemes like Ring Attention pass KV blocks around a ring of GPUs, overlapping each hop’s transfer with the attention compute on the block already in hand. Llama 3 switched context parallelism on only for its long-context training stages — at 128k tokens, a single sequence’s activations alone would smother any one GPU.

💡Intuition

One model, five knives. The batch dimension (data parallel), the width of each matrix (tensor parallel), the depth of the stack (pipeline parallel), the expert axis (expert parallel), and the sequence axis (context parallel) are five independent directions in which the computation is separable — and each cut bleeds communication in a different place: once a step, every layer, every stage boundary, every MoE layer, every attention pass. Systems design is choosing which places can afford to bleed, given which wires are fast.

Composing them: 3D and 4D parallelism

No frontier run uses one parallelism; they multiply. Assign every GPU coordinates (t,p,d)(t, p, d)(t,p,d) — its tensor-parallel rank, pipeline stage, and data-parallel replica — and the cluster becomes a 3D grid; add context or expert parallelism for a 4th dimension. The composition rule follows the bandwidth cliff: the chattiest parallelism gets the fastest wires. Tensor parallel inside the node on NVLink; pipeline parallel across nodes (fixed-pair, modest traffic); data parallel across the remaining span of the cluster, its once-per-step all-reduce overlapped with backward compute.

Llama 3 405B is the best-documented example (Grattafiori et al., 2024, figures approximate as reported): 16,384 H100s, 4D parallelism with tensor parallel 8, pipeline parallel 16, context parallel 16 during long-context stages, and FSDP-style sharded data parallelism filling the rest. Trained in bf16 with fp32 gradient accumulation, it achieved 38–43% MFU (defined below) — around 400 TFLOPs per GPU sustained — and burned roughly 31 million H100-hours for its pretraining (the full herd cost closer to 39 million). The paper is refreshingly frank about failure at this scale: in one 54-day window they logged 466 job interruptions, 419 unexpected, the majority traced to GPU and HBM failures. At 16k GPUs, something breaks every few hours, and surviving that is the next chapter’s subject.

DeepSeek-V3 is the counterpoint: 2,048 bandwidth-crippled H800s, and a design that reads like a systems paper wearing a model paper’s clothes. No tensor parallelism at all (the nerfed NVLink made it unattractive); instead, 16-stage pipeline parallelism with the DualPipe schedule, expert parallelism of degree 64 spanning 8 nodes with routing constrained so no token crosses more than 4 nodes, ZeRO stage-1 data parallelism, fp8 matmuls, and about 20 of each node’s 132 SMs permanently dedicated to running communication kernels so that all-to-all traffic hides behind compute. Every choice is the export-control constraint, metabolized into architecture. Constraint bred efficiency; efficiency bred a number that shocked people — which brings us to money.

What it costs

The unit of account is the GPU-hour, and the arithmetic is short. Recall from scaling laws that training a dense transformer costs roughly C≈6NDC \approx 6NDC≈6ND FLOPs for NNN parameters and DDD training tokens — 2 FLOPs per parameter per token in the forward pass (2N2N2N), twice that in the backward.

On 16,384 GPUs that is about 9.5×10¹⁰ / 16,384 ≈ 5.8 million seconds ≈ 67 days. At a bulk rental rate of roughly $2 per H100-hour (2024–2025 ballpark; spot prices have since drifted lower), the pretraining compute bill is on the order of $50–60M — for one training run of one model, excluding every experiment that led to it.

That 40% is the metric labs optimize: MFU, model FLOPs utilization — the fraction of the cluster’s theoretical peak FLOPs that goes into the model’s useful 6ND6ND6ND arithmetic, introduced in the PaLM paper. Everything in this chapter taxes it: pipeline bubbles, communication that fails to hide behind compute, memory-bound layers like normalization and attention softmax, recompute from checkpointing (which by convention does not count as “useful”), stragglers, restarts. Typical honest values in 2025–2026: 35–45% for dense models in bf16, often 20–35% for MoE models (all-to-all is expensive) — meaning the majority of every cluster’s theoretical capacity is structurally unusable, and a 5-point MFU improvement is worth millions of dollars per run.

Now the number that reset expectations. DeepSeek-V3’s report states its full training consumed 2.788 million H800-hours — at their quoted $2/GPU-hour, $5.576M — for a model competitive with frontier systems that were widely assumed to cost tens to hundreds of millions to train. When mainstream attention caught up with the report in January 2025, roughly $600B was briefly wiped off Nvidia’s market value in a day. Three things about that number, all true at once. First, it is real and roughly reproducible from first principles: V3 is a mixture-of-experts model with only 37B of its 671B parameters active per token, so 6NactiveD=6×3.7×1010×1.48×1013≈3.3×10246 N_{\text{active}} D = 6 \times 3.7\times10^{10} \times 1.48\times10^{13} \approx 3.3\times10^{24}6Nactive​D=6×3.7×1010×1.48×1013≈3.3×1024 FLOPs — about 11× less than Llama 3 405B — delivered efficiently via fp8 and the systems work above. Second, it is narrow: the final pretraining run only, excluding — as the report itself says plainly — every ablation, failed experiment, data pipeline, salary, and the cluster itself. The all-in cost of getting to V3 was surely an order of magnitude higher. Third, the panic misread the economics: cheaper training per capability has never yet reduced total compute demand — it raises what labs attempt with the same budget (economists know the pattern as Jevons paradox).

Capex versus rental frames the top of the market. An H100 cost roughly $25–30k to buy; a 100,000-GPU cluster (xAI’s Colossus scale, and comparable 2025-era builds by Meta, Microsoft/OpenAI, and others) runs $2.5–3B in chips alone before servers, networking, buildings, and the 100+ megawatts of power — a gigawatt-scale buildout across the industry whose numbers move too fast to print. Renting converts that capex into $2–6 per GPU-hour opex and is how everyone below the hyperscaler tier — and every reader of this book — touches this hardware. The strategic consequence is worth stating: at frontier scale, compute access — who can finance, power, and cool how many chips — is as much a competitive moat as any algorithm, which is why export controls on GPUs are foreign policy and why the open-vs-closed landscape tilts the way it does.

✎Note

Every dollar figure and spec in this section is a snapshot of a fast-moving market: rental prices fell throughout 2024–2026 as Blackwell supply arrived, cluster sizes and power figures grow quarterly, and per-chip numbers vary by form factor and vendor claim. Trust the method — FLOPs from 6ND6ND6ND, divide by achievable throughput, multiply by a rate — and re-derive with current prices whenever you need a live answer.

Where this leaves us

  • GPUs win at deep learning because matmuls are huge piles of independent multiply-adds, and GPUs trade single-task latency for massive throughput — tensor cores are matmul-specific circuits delivering the headline TFLOPs.
  • Arithmetic intensity (FLOPs per byte moved) against the chip’s ridge point (~295 FLOPs/byte on an H100) tells you whether a computation is compute-bound or memory-bound: matmuls are compute-bound, matvecs are hopelessly memory-bound — the fact that will drive the inference chapter.
  • Floating-point formats trade exponent bits (range) against mantissa bits (precision); bf16 won by keeping fp32’s range, and the mixed-precision recipe (bf16 compute, fp32 master weights and Adam states) pins ~16 bytes per parameter — the ledger that makes even a 7B model overflow one GPU.
  • fp8 training went from curiosity to frontier practice with DeepSeek-V3’s fine-grained scaling; 4-bit training remains a research frontier.
  • Activation checkpointing buys ~10× activation memory for ~33% extra compute; ZeRO/FSDP eliminates redundant copies of optimizer state, gradients, and weights across data-parallel ranks, in three progressively more communication-hungry stages.
  • The five parallelisms cut along five axes — batch, matrix width, layer depth, experts, sequence — and are composed by one rule: the chattiest cut gets the fastest wires (TP on NVLink inside a node, PP between fixed node pairs, DP overlapped across the cluster).
  • You can estimate any run’s cost on a napkin: 6ND6ND6ND FLOPs, divided by peak FLOPs times MFU (~35–45% dense), times the GPU-hour rate — and you know both what DeepSeek’s $5.6M did and did not include.
