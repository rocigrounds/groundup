High-Dimensional Geometry

Five strange but provable facts about spaces with thousands of dimensions — the facts that make embeddings, retrieval, and superposition possible.

In this chapter
  1. The stage: spaces with thousands of axes
  2. Fact one: random vectors are nearly orthogonal
  3. Fact two: the volume is all in the shell
  4. Fact three: Gaussian clouds are soap bubbles
  5. Fact four: distances flatten, and the nearest neighbor barely wins
  6. Fact five: exponentially many almost-orthogonal directions
  7. Where this leaves us

The vectors in the last two chapters had two or three components, because two or three is what we can draw. The vectors inside a real language model have thousands. GPT-3 carried its running representation of the text in a vector with 12,288 components; Llama 3’s largest model uses 16,384; OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-large hands you 3,072 numbers per passage of text. Every claim this book will make about embeddings, attention, and interpretability is a claim about geometry in spaces like that.

Here is the problem: your geometric intuition was trained in three dimensions, and in high dimensions it is systematically wrong, not merely unreliable. Almost all of a high-dimensional orange is peel. Two directions chosen at random are almost certainly perpendicular. A thousand-dimensional bell curve has almost nobody at the peak of the bell. If someone asserted these things without proof you would be right to be suspicious, and an outline that merely lists them teaches nothing. So this chapter does something different: it takes five strange facts, and for each one gives you the plain-English reason, the short derivation, and arithmetic you can check with a pencil (or a ten-line script — several of the tables below come from simulations we ran while writing, with the settings stated so you can reproduce them).

The payoff is not philosophical. Fact one and fact five are the reason embeddings can encode meaning as direction and the reason superposition — a model storing far more concepts than it has dimensions — is possible at all. Facts two and three govern what typical model activations and initial weights look like. Fact four is the famous “curse of dimensionality,” which shapes how retrieval systems are built and why they measure similarity the way they do. By the end you will know not only that high dimensions are weird, but exactly how weird, with numbers.

The stage: spaces with thousands of axes

Nothing new is needed to define the space. From Chapter 1, a vector in Rd\R^dRd is a list of ddd numbers, its length (norm) is ∥x∥=x12+⋯+xd2\lVert x \rVert = \sqrt{x_1^2 + \cdots + x_d^2}∥x∥=x12​+⋯+xd2​​, and the angle between two vectors comes from the dot product:

cos⁡θ  =  x⋅y∥x∥ ∥y∥.\cos\theta \;=\; \frac{x \cdot y}{\lVert x \rVert\, \lVert y \rVert}.cosθ=∥x∥∥y∥x⋅y​.

All of that carries over to d=12,288d = 12{,}288d=12,288 without modification. What changes is which configurations are typical. In three dimensions, if you pick points or directions at random, the results look like your mental picture: some pairs close together, some far apart, angles all over the place. In high dimensions, randomness stops producing variety and starts producing eerie uniformity — nearly every random draw looks like nearly every other. The umbrella name for this phenomenon is concentration of measure, and it is the single technical idea behind everything in this chapter, so it deserves a plain statement now.

💡Intuition

Concentration of measure, in one sentence: a quantity that is built by adding up many independent random contributions barely fluctuates, because the contributions cancel. One coin flip is completely unpredictable; the average of 10,000 coin flips is glued to 50% (it lands within one percentage point of it the overwhelming majority of the time). High-dimensional geometric quantities — angles, lengths, distances — are all sums or averages over ddd coordinates. So when ddd is in the thousands, they behave like the average of thousands of coin flips: nearly constant. “High-dimensional geometry is weird” is mostly this one fact wearing five different costumes.

We will need two pieces of vocabulary from probability, ahead of their full treatment in Chapter 6. The expected value E[X]\E[X]E[X] of a random quantity XXX is its long-run average over many draws. The variance Var⁡(X)=E[(X−E[X])2]\Var(X) = \E[(X - \E[X])^2]Var(X)=E[(X−E[X])2] measures spread — how far a typical draw sits from that average — and its square root, the standard deviation, is spread in the same units as XXX. The one rule we use repeatedly: for independent random quantities, variances add. That rule is why cancellation happens at a predictable rate.

Fact one: random vectors are nearly orthogonal

Pick two directions at random in Rd\R^dRd and measure the angle between them. In d=2d = 2d=2 you get anything from 0° to 180° with no preference. In d=1000d = 1000d=1000 you get 90°, almost every time, to within a couple of degrees. Random directions in high dimensions are, with overwhelming probability, nearly perpendicular.

💡Intuition

Why should this be? The cosine of the angle is a dot product: multiply the vectors coordinate by coordinate, add up ddd products. If the two vectors are random and unrelated, each little product is as likely to be positive as negative — ddd independent contributions with no agenda. They cancel. The sum hovers near zero, and “dot product near zero” is “nearly orthogonal.” The more dimensions, the more terms cancel, the closer to exactly 90°. Orthogonality is not an exceptional configuration in high dimensions; it is the default state of unrelated vectors.

∑The math

Make it concrete with the simplest random vectors there are: random sign vectors, where each coordinate is +1+1+1 or −1-1−1 with equal probability, independently. ⚠️Careful

“Nearly orthogonal” is not “orthogonal,” and the difference will matter later in the book. A cosine of 0.03 sounds like nothing, but if a model reads a vector by dot-producting against a direction (Chapter 49), every one of thousands of nearly-orthogonal stored signals leaks a little bit into the readout. Small crosstalk times many sources equals real interference — the central tension in superposition. Keep both halves of the fact: overlaps shrink like 1/d1/\sqrt{d}1/d​, and they never reach zero.

Fact two: the volume is all in the shell

Take a solid ball in Rd\R^dRd — every point within distance rrr of the center — and ask a homely question: how much of its volume lies in the outer 1% shell, the “peel” between radius 0.99 r0.99\,r0.99r and rrr? In three dimensions, about 3%, matching intuition: thin peel, small volume. In 1000 dimensions, the peel holds 99.996% of the ball. Nearly the entire orange is peel.

∑The math

The derivation is three lines, and it starts from a scaling fact we already own. Scaling every coordinate of a region by sss multiplies lengths by sss in each of ddd independent directions, so it multiplies volume by sds^dsd — the determinant-as-volume-scaling story of Chapter 2 applied to the matrix s Is\,IsI. The inner ball of radius (1−ε)r(1-\varepsilon)r(1−ε)r is a scaled copy of the full ball with s=1−εs = 1-\varepsilons=1−ε, so

vol(inner ball)vol(whole ball)  =  (1−ε)d,shell fraction  =  1−(1−ε)d.\frac{\text{vol(inner ball)}}{\text{vol(whole ball)}} \;=\; (1-\varepsilon)^d , \qquad \text{shell fraction} \;=\; 1 - (1-\varepsilon)^d .vol(whole ball)vol(inner ball)​=(1−ε)d,shell fraction=1−(1−ε)d.

For fixed ε\varepsilonε, the quantity (1−ε)d(1-\varepsilon)^d(1−ε)d dies exponentially as ddd grows (a useful approximation: (1−ε)d≈e−εd(1-\varepsilon)^d \approx e^{-\varepsilon d}(1−ε)d≈e−εd when ε\varepsilonε is small). No formula for the ball’s volume was needed — only the scaling law.

💡Intuition

Why does this happen? Volume in ddd dimensions is a product of ddd extents, and products punish small discounts mercilessly. Shrinking the radius by 1% shrinks the volume by 1% per dimension, compounded a thousand times. The flip side of “the interior is empty” is where the mass went: near the boundary, a randomly chosen point has full radius available in some combination of its thousand coordinates almost surely — it takes a conspiracy of all ddd coordinates being small simultaneously to sit near the center, and conspiracies among a thousand independent coordinates never happen. This is concentration of measure again, in volume form: the radius of a random point, an aggregate over ddd coordinates, concentrates hard against its maximum.

✎Note

A cousin of this fact, worth one sentence because it recalibrates intuition the same way: the largest ball you can inscribe in a ddd-dimensional cube occupies a fraction of the cube that shrinks toward zero astonishingly fast (about 0.52 in d=3d=3d=3, roughly 2×10−702\times10^{-70}2×10−70 in d=100d=100d=100). Nearly all of a high-dimensional cube’s volume is in its corners — there are 2d2^d2d of them, and they are far from the center. Keith Ball’s survey is the classic readable tour of these results.

Fact three: Gaussian clouds are soap bubbles

Facts one and two were about balls and spheres. The random vectors that appear in deep learning are usually Gaussian: each coordinate drawn independently from a bell curve with mean 0 and variance 1 (written x∼N(0,Id)x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)x∼N(0,Id​) — the full story is in Chapter 6). This is how weights are born at initialization, and it is the standard model for “generic” activations and noise. In one dimension, the bell curve matches intuition: most samples near 0, the peak of the bell. In ddd dimensions, that intuition inverts.

∑The math

Ask how far a Gaussian sample sits from the origin. Its squared length is a sum of ddd independent terms:

∥x∥2  =  x12+x22+⋯+xd2.\lVert x \rVert^2 \;=\; x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \cdots + x_d^2 .∥x∥2=x12​+x22​+⋯+xd2​.

Each coordinate has E[xi2]=1\E[x_i^2] = 1E[xi2​]=1 (that is what “variance 1” means), so by adding expectations,

E[∥x∥2]  =  d.\E\big[\lVert x \rVert^2\big] \;=\; d .E[∥x∥2]=d.

How much does ∥x∥2\lVert x \rVert^2∥x∥2 fluctuate around ddd? Each term xi2x_i^2xi2​ has variance E[xi4]−E[xi2]2=3−1=2\E[x_i^4] - \E[x_i^2]^2 = 3 - 1 = 2E[xi4​]−E[xi2​]2=3−1=2 (a standard Gaussian fact — take it on trust until Chapter 6), and independent variances add: Var⁡(∥x∥2)=2d\Var(\lVert x \rVert^2) = 2dVar(∥x∥2)=2d, so the standard deviation of ∥x∥2\lVert x \rVert^2∥x∥2 is 2d\sqrt{2d}2d​. Compare spread to size: 2d/d=2/d→0\sqrt{2d}/d = \sqrt{2/d} \to 02d​/d=2/d​→0. The squared length is ddd give or take a vanishing relative error, which means the length itself is

∥x∥  ≈  d ,with fluctuations of about 12≈0.71 — a constant, independent of d.\lVert x \rVert \;\approx\; \sqrt{d}\,, \quad\text{with fluctuations of about } \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} \approx 0.71 \text{ — a constant, independent of } d.∥x∥≈d​,with fluctuations of about 2​1​≈0.71 — a constant, independent of d.

(The constant comes from linearizing the square root: d±2d≈d±2d/(2d)=d±1/2\sqrt{d \pm \sqrt{2d}} \approx \sqrt d \pm \sqrt{2d}/(2\sqrt d) = \sqrt d \pm 1/\sqrt 2d±2d​​≈d​±2d​/(2d​)=d​±1/2​.)

✏️Worked example

Take d=1000d = 1000d=1000. A sample from N(0,I1000)\mathcal{N}(0, I_{1000})N(0,I1000​) has length 1000≈31.62\sqrt{1000} \approx 31.621000​≈31.62, plus or minus about 0.710.710.71. Not “about 31.62 on average with samples scattered everywhere” — pinned: the overwhelming majority of samples have length between roughly 29.5 and 33.7, a window whose width does not grow with ddd. Relative to the radius, the cloud of samples is a shell of thickness 0.71/31.62≈2%0.71/31.62 \approx 2\%0.71/31.62≈2%. Meanwhile the origin — the single point of highest probability density — has near-zero probability of hosting a sample: a ball of radius 5 around it (huge in everyday terms) captures a fraction of the mass too small to write down without scientific notation.

💡Intuition

This is the Gaussian soap bubble (also called the “Gaussian annulus”): the distribution’s density is highest at the origin, but its mass lives on a thin shell at radius d\sqrt{d}d​, because mass is density times volume and — fact two — volume near the center is nonexistent. Density says “each individual point near the origin beats each individual point far away.” Volume says “there are unfathomably more points at radius 31 than at radius 5.” Volume wins. The resolution of the apparent paradox is that “the most likely single point” and “where samples typically land” are different questions in high dimensions, and the gap between them is the whole story. The set where samples typically land has a name — the typical set — that will return in Chapter 7 when we meet entropy.

⚙️In practice

This fact is load-bearing in at least three places in modern systems. First, initialization: schemes like Kaiming/He initialization (Chapter 12) choose per-weight variances precisely so that the d\sqrt{d}d​-scale norms of activations stay constant from layer to layer instead of exploding or vanishing. Second, the dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ denominator in scaled dot-product attention exists because a dot product of two dkd_kdk​-dimensional roughly-Gaussian vectors has standard deviation dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ — attention is normalizing away exactly the growth this section derived. Third, diffusion models (Chapter 42) generate images by starting from a Gaussian sample; practitioners interpolate between two noise seeds along the sphere (so-called slerp, spherical interpolation) rather than in a straight line, because the straight line’s midpoint would have norm ≈d/2\approx \sqrt{d/2}≈d/2​ — off the soap bubble, in the empty region where the model never saw data.

Fact four: distances flatten, and the nearest neighbor barely wins

Now put many random points in a high-dimensional space and ask about the distances between them. In low dimensions you get variety: near pairs, far pairs. In high dimensions, concentration strikes again — squared distance is a sum of ddd independent per-coordinate terms, so every pairwise distance lands near the same value, with fluctuations that shrink relative to it. The consequence has a name in the database literature: the curse of dimensionality for nearest-neighbor search. If all distances are nearly equal, “nearest” is a technicality.

In d=1000d=1000d=1000, it is 9% farther — every point is nearly the same distance away, clustered around the predicted typical distance d/6≈12.9\sqrt{d/6} \approx 12.9d/6​≈12.9 (each coordinate contributes E[(ai−bi)2]=16\E[(a_i-b_i)^2] = \tfrac16E[(ai​−bi​)2]=61​ for independent uniforms; sum over 1000 coordinates, take the root). This is the phenomenon formalized by Beyer et al. (1999): as d→∞d \to \inftyd→∞, under broad conditions the nearest and farthest neighbor distances converge, and nearest-neighbor queries lose their meaning.

So why does anything built on high-dimensional similarity work at all? Vector search over embeddings is a nearest-neighbor query in 1,000 to 3,000 dimensions, and it is the backbone of every retrieval-augmented generation system in production. The resolution is the fine print in the theorem: distances flatten for points that are random and unstructured — independent coordinates, no relationships. Real embeddings are the opposite of that. They are trained so that related texts are pulled close together and unrelated texts pushed apart, and empirically they occupy a region of much lower intrinsic dimension than the ambient space — a curved, structured sheet inside R3072\R^{3072}R3072, not a uniform haze filling it. On structured data, the gap between “semantically near” and “background typical distance” is exactly the signal the training created, and nearest-neighbor search recovers it. The curse is real, but it curses random data; the entire job of embedding training is to make data non-random.

⚠️Careful

The flattening does not vanish just because meaning exists — it shows up as score compression. Cosine similarities between unrelated passages under a modern embedding model do not spread over [−1,1][-1, 1][−1,1]; they pile up in a narrow band (often somewhere around 0.1–0.3, model-dependent), with relevant pairs only modestly above the pile. Two practical consequences. First, never interpret an absolute similarity score across models, or treat 0.5 as a universal “related” threshold — only comparisons within one model’s scores, on one query, are meaningful. Second, small differences at the top of the ranking are where all the information is, which is why retrieval pipelines routinely re-rank their top candidates with a stronger model (Chapter 45).

⚙️In practice

The brief’s promised payoff, stated explicitly: this fact shapes how similarity is measured in 2026 systems. Embedding pipelines overwhelmingly use cosine similarity — the angle-only comparison from Chapter 1 — rather than Euclidean distance. Two reasons. Geometrically, facts one and three say that in high dimensions norms are noisy near-constants (everything sits near the soap bubble) while direction is where the discriminating information lives; dividing out the norms discards a nuisance variable. Operationally, most embedding models (OpenAI’s text-embedding-3 family among them) ship vectors pre-normalized to length 1, at which point cosine similarity, dot product, and Euclidean distance give the same ranking — related by ∥u−v∥2=2−2cos⁡θ\lVert u - v\rVert^2 = 2 - 2\cos\theta∥u−v∥2=2−2cosθ for unit vectors. The honest caveat: when vectors are not normalized, dot product and cosine differ (the norm can encode confidence or frequency), and some recommendation and retrieval systems deliberately keep the norm. “Cosine by default, dot product on purpose” is a fair summary of current practice.

Fact five: exponentially many almost-orthogonal directions

The last fact is the deepest, and it is the one this book will lean on hardest — it is the geometric fact behind superposition. Start with the obvious ceiling: in Rd\R^dRd you can have at most ddd directions that are exactly, mutually orthogonal (that is what “dimension ddd” means — Chapter 1). Twelve thousand dimensions, twelve thousand perpendicular slots. It seems to follow that a model with ddd-dimensional representations can keep at most ddd concepts cleanly separated. It does not follow — because of what happens when you relax “exactly orthogonal” to “orthogonal within ε\varepsilonε.”

∑The math

Ask: how many unit vectors v1,…,vNv_1, \dots, v_Nv1​,…,vN​ can we place in Rd\R^dRd so that every pair satisfies ∣cos⁡θij∣≤ε\lvert \cos\theta_{ij} \rvert \le \varepsilon∣cosθij​∣≤ε? We do not need a clever construction — fact one says random vectors nearly achieve this on their own. Draw NNN random sign vectors (normalized by d\sqrt dd​). By Hoeffding’s inequality, one fixed pair fails the ε\varepsilonε requirement with probability at most 2e−dε2/22e^{-d\varepsilon^2/2}2e−dε2/2. There are fewer than N2/2N^2/2N2/2 pairs, so by the union bound (the probability that any bad event happens is at most the sum of their probabilities — probability’s bluntest tool), the whole configuration fails with probability less than

N22⋅2e−dε2/2  =  N2e−dε2/2.\frac{N^2}{2}\cdot 2e^{-d\varepsilon^2/2} \;=\; N^2 e^{-d\varepsilon^2/2}.2N2​⋅2e−dε2/2=N2e−dε2/2.

If this quantity is below 1, a valid configuration must exist (indeed a random one works most of the time). Solving N2e−dε2/2<1N^2 e^{-d\varepsilon^2/2} < 1N2e−dε2/2<1:

N  <  edε2/4.N \;<\; e^{d\varepsilon^2/4}.N<edε2/4.

The count of almost-orthogonal directions is exponential in ddd. Not ddd slots. Not d2d^2d2. Exponentially many — and this is only the guarantee from the crudest possible argument; the true optimal packings are larger still (exponential with a better constant), while a matching upper bound shows exponential-in-dε2d\varepsilon^2dε2 is also the ceiling. The constant in the exponent is loose; the shape of the answer is not.

Twenty trillion directions, every pair within 6° of perpendicular, in a space with only 12,288 truly orthogonal slots.

The exponential does not care that it starts behind; by frontier-model widths it has left every polynomial impossibly far below. And remember this is a lower bound obtained by pure chance — no design, no optimization.

💡Intuition

Where does the exponential room come from? A counting argument makes it tangible. Around each direction viv_ivi​, the set of directions “too close” to it (cosine above ε\varepsilonε) is a small spherical cap, and — by the same concentration that drove fact one — that cap’s share of the sphere is exponentially tiny, roughly e−dε2/2e^{-d\varepsilon^2/2}e−dε2/2 of the total surface. Each claimed direction fences off only an exponentially small forbidden zone, so exponentially many claims fit before the sphere is used up. In three dimensions the caps are fat (at ε=0.1\varepsilon = 0.1ε=0.1, each forbidden zone is a wide band) and you run out of room after a handful of directions. In 12,288 dimensions each cap is a 10−2710^{-27}10−27-scale speck. The sphere’s surface behaves like an effectively unlimited parking lot.

This is the fact the brief called “THE fact behind superposition,” and it is worth stating the payoff in full. A transformer’s residual stream (Chapter 16) is a vector with ddd components, and the naive reading is that the model can track at most ddd distinct features of its input. But a model does not need exact orthogonality — it needs to store features so that they can be read back with tolerable crosstalk. Fact five says the geometry permits exponentially many such storage slots, and fact one says the crosstalk per pair is a manageable ∼1/d\sim 1/\sqrt d∼1/d​. Anthropic’s Toy Models of Superposition (Elhage et al., 2022) demonstrated that trained networks use this room: when features are sparse (few active at once — true of language, where a given token is not simultaneously about chemistry, French grammar, and basketball), small models provably pack more features than dimensions, paying a small interference cost for a large capacity gain. That single geometric bargain is why Chapter 50 exists, why individual neurons are polysemantic, and why interpretability needs tools like sparse autoencoders to unmix directions rather than reading neurons one at a time.

🕰History

The mathematics predates deep learning by decades. Concentration of measure on spheres goes back to Paul Lévy in the 1920s and was developed into a general theory by Vitali Milman in the 1970s, who used it to prove Dvoretzky’s theorem. The random-projection result most cited in machine learning is the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma (Johnson & Lindenstrauss, 1984): any nnn points in any high-dimensional space can be mapped down to about 8ln⁡(n)/ε28\ln(n)/\varepsilon^28ln(n)/ε2 dimensions while distorting every pairwise distance by at most a factor 1±ε1\pm\varepsilon1±ε — and a random linear map achieves this. Notice the shape of the statement: it is fact five read backwards. Exponentially many almost-orthogonal directions fit in ddd dimensions   ⟺  \iff⟺ nnn directions need only logarithmically-many-in-nnn dimensions to stay almost orthogonal. The lemma is why random projection is a respectable dimensionality-reduction technique, and the “logarithmic dimensions suffice” intuition recurs from sketching algorithms to the design of embedding sizes.

✎Note

An honest caveat about applying all five facts to real networks: the theorems are about random or worst-case configurations, and trained models are neither. Activations are not isotropic Gaussians (they have outlier directions and anisotropic spectra — a practical headache for quantization), embeddings concentrate on low-intrinsic-dimension regions, and learned features are arranged by training pressure, not by chance. The facts still earn their keep in two ways: they describe the capacity of the space (what geometry permits, as in superposition), and they describe the background against which structure stands out (as in retrieval). When a later chapter says “by the geometry of Chapter 3,” it is invoking one of those two uses.

Where this leaves us

You can now justify, with arithmetic, the strange facts that the rest of the book will use as freely as the triangle inequality:

  • Random directions are nearly orthogonal: the cosine of two random ddd-dimensional vectors has mean 0 and standard deviation 1/d1/\sqrt d1/d​ — 0.577 in d=3d=3d=3, 0.032 in d=1000d=1000d=1000 — because a dot product is ddd coin flips averaging out. Named phenomenon: concentration of measure.
  • Volume lives in the shell: the inner (1−ε)(1-\varepsilon)(1−ε)-ball holds (1−ε)d(1-\varepsilon)^d(1−ε)d of the volume; 0.991000≈4.3×10−50.99^{1000} \approx 4.3\times10^{-5}0.991000≈4.3×10−5, so a 1% peel is 99.996% of a 1000-dimensional orange.
  • Gaussians are soap bubbles: E∥x∥2=d\E\lVert x\rVert^2 = dE∥x∥2=d, so samples from N(0,Id)\mathcal N(0, I_d)N(0,Id​) live at radius d±O(1)\sqrt d \pm O(1)d​±O(1) — at d=1000d=1000d=1000, a shell at 31.6 of thickness about 0.7 — and the high-density origin is empty.
  • Distances flatten: farthest ÷ nearest fell from 33.5 to 1.09 as ddd went from 2 to 1000 in our simulation — the curse of dimensionality for nearest neighbors — which is why absolute similarity scores are untrustworthy, why cosine similarity is the default for embeddings, and why retrieval only works because embeddings are trained to be non-random.
  • Exponentially many almost-orthogonal directions fit: a union-bound argument gives edε2/4e^{d\varepsilon^2/4}edε2/4 directions with pairwise ∣cos⁡θ∣≤ε\lvert\cos\theta\rvert \le \varepsilon∣cosθ∣≤ε — about 2×10132\times10^{13}2×1013 at GPT-3’s width for ε=0.1\varepsilon = 0.1ε=0.1 — the geometric license for superposition, and the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma in reverse.
  • You know the fine print: these are statements about random configurations, used later either as capacity results or as background models.

Geometry now behaves; the next tool is motion. To train a model we must know how its outputs change as its parameters change — in a million-dimensional parameter space, no less, where this chapter’s instincts will again apply. Next: Derivatives and Gradients.
