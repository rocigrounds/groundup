Features, Directions, and Probes

The linear representation hypothesis — why the field bets that concepts live as directions in activation space, the evidence for and against, and how to hunt for them with probes.

The previous chapter made the case for looking inside models. This chapter is about the first thing you see when you do: a tensor of floating-point numbers, billions of them per forward pass, with no labels, no comments, and no obvious structure. An activation vector at some layer of a transformer is a list of a few thousand numbers. Somewhere in those numbers, the model is representing everything it knows about the text so far — that the sentence is in French, that the speaker is being sarcastic, that the code has an unclosed bracket, that the next token is probably “the”. The question this chapter answers is: in what format?

The field’s working answer is almost embarrassingly simple, and it is the single most load-bearing assumption in all of interpretability: concepts are directions. A property of the input — “this is French” — corresponds to a direction in activation space, and the model represents how French the text is by how far the activation vector extends along that direction. If this is true, then reading the model’s mind reduces to the very first operation you learned in Chapter 1: a dot product. And writing to the model’s mind — making it believe the text is French — reduces to vector addition.

That claim is called the linear representation hypothesis, and this chapter examines it properly: what it says, why it might be true, the evidence that convinced the field (including the best case study in interpretability, a model that taught itself a board game), the honest counter-evidence (some features live on circles, not lines), and the workhorse tool for testing it — the probe, a small classifier trained to read concepts out of a frozen model’s activations. Probes come with a famous trap, and we will walk into it deliberately so you recognize it later.

Everything in the coming chapters — superposition, sparse autoencoders, circuits, steering — is built on top of the vocabulary this chapter establishes.

What counts as a feature

Interpretability people say “feature” constantly, and the word is doing a lot of quiet work, so let us pin it down before using it.

A feature is a property of the input (or of the model’s in-progress computation) that the model represents — that is, some function of the input that you could in principle recover from the model’s internal activations, and that the model plausibly computes because it is useful for predicting the next token. Features span an enormous range of abstraction:

  • Concrete, surface-level: this token is a digit; this text is French; we are inside a string literal; the previous word was “not”.
  • Syntactic and structural: this noun is the subject of the sentence; the current bracket depth is 3; this is the second item of a list.
  • Semantic: this passage is about the Golden Gate Bridge; the sentiment is positive; this person being described is a doctor; the year under discussion is in the 1960s.
  • Abstract and unsettling: the user is asking for something harmful; the persona being role-played is dishonest; this claim is false; the assistant is about to refuse; the flattering answer differs from the accurate one.

The last category is why Chapter 48 argued this field matters. If “the model believes this statement is false” is a feature with a findable representation, you can build a lie detector. If “deception of the user” is a feature, you can monitor for it. Whether such abstract properties really are represented as cleanly as “is-French” is an open empirical question — but as we will see in the feature menagerie below, the published record has been steadily moving the answer toward “yes, surprisingly often.”

⚠️Careful

A feature is not the same thing as a neuron. It is tempting to hope that each individual coordinate of the activation vector — each neuron — encodes one feature, and early vision-model work found some interpretable neurons. But in language models most neurons respond to grab-bags of unrelated things (a phenomenon called polysemanticity), and most features are spread across many neurons. The next chapter, superposition, explains why models are forced into this arrangement. For now, keep the concepts separate: neurons are the basis directions hardware happens to give us; features are the directions the model uses, and the two bases generally do not line up.

The linear representation hypothesis

Here is the hypothesis, stated carefully. Let h∈Rdh \in \R^{d}h∈Rd be the activation vector at some layer — for concreteness, a residual-stream vector of dimension d=4096d = 4096d=4096 in a Llama-class model. The linear representation hypothesis claims that for each feature iii the model uses, there is a fixed direction vi∈Rdv_i \in \R^{d}vi​∈Rd (a unit vector, say) such that the activation decomposes, at least approximately, as a weighted sum

h  ≈  ∑ifi vi,h \;\approx\; \sum_{i} f_i \, v_i,h≈i∑​fi​vi​,

where fi∈Rf_i \in \Rfi​∈R is the feature’s activation strength on this particular input — how French the text is, how positive the sentiment is — and viv_ivi​ is the same fixed direction across all inputs. Three consequences follow immediately, and they are the whole reason the hypothesis matters:

  1. Reading is projection. To ask “how much of feature iii is present,” compute the dot product vi⋅hv_i \cdot hvi​⋅h (plus, in practice, a learned bias). This is the payoff of Chapters 1 and 2 arriving all at once: the dot product measures “how much of this direction is in that vector,” and now the direction means something.
  2. Writing is addition. To make the model represent more of feature iii, add αvi\alpha v_iαvi​ to the activation and let the rest of the forward pass run. If the hypothesis holds, downstream layers — which read the stream with their own dot products — cannot tell the difference between an input that really had the feature and one where you injected it. This is the basis of steering.
  3. Interpretability becomes linear algebra. Finding features means finding directions; the entire toolkit of Part I applies.
💡Intuition

Why would a model choose this format? Because the transformer’s own anatomy pushes it there. Recall from the residual stream chapter that a transformer is a sum: each attention head and each multi-layer perceptron (MLP) adds its output vector into a shared running total, and every later component reads that total through a linear map (a WQW_QWQ​, WKW_KWK​, WVW_VWV​, or the MLP’s input matrix) before doing anything nonlinear. If component A wants to send the message “this is French” to component B, and B can only receive messages by taking dot products with the stream, then the natural — arguably the only robust — encoding is: pick a direction, and write along it. The linear representation hypothesis is less a discovery about models than a prediction from their architecture. The discoveries are that it holds as often as it does, and that it sometimes fails.

The clean-sounding phrase “features are directions” hides some subtlety, and Park, Choe & Veitch (2023) is the paper that made the field say precisely what it means. They distinguish the embedding representation (a direction in the residual stream whose presence encodes the concept) from the unembedding representation (a direction in the output space that shifts token probabilities), show these need not coincide under the plain Euclidean dot product, and construct a causal inner product — a reweighting of the geometry — under which reading directions and writing directions line up. The technical details are beyond what we need, but the moral is worth keeping: “direction” is only meaningful relative to an inner product, and the model’s natural geometry may not be the one your coordinates suggest. You met this idea abstractly in Chapter 1; this is it earning rent.

The evidence, assembled

Why does the field believe this? Not because of one experiment — because four independent lines of evidence, from different methods and different eras, keep landing on the same picture.

Word embeddings started it. In 2013, Mikolov and colleagues showed that word2vec embeddings — humble, non-contextual word vectors, from before deep language models — support arithmetic on meaning: the vector for king, minus man, plus woman, lands nearest to queen. The by-then-famous reading is that there is a “gender direction” (roughly vwoman−vmanv_{\text{woman}} - v_{\text{man}}vwoman​−vman​) and a “royalty direction,” and words are approximately sums of their attributes’ directions — the linear representation hypothesis, avant la lettre, in the model’s first layer. Chapter 20 covered this and its caveats (the analogies are noisier than the legend says); what matters here is the template it set. If meaning is linear at the embedding layer, it is natural to ask whether it stays linear deeper in.

Probes succeed. Train a logistic regression — the simplest possible linear read-out — on frozen activations, and it can predict an astonishing range of properties: part of speech, syntax-tree depth, language, sentiment, topic, whether a factual claim is true, whether the model is about to refuse. The methodology section below gives the full recipe. The point for now: if a linear map suffices to read a property out, the property is, by definition, linearly available — evidence (with a trap we will discuss) that the model stores it that way.

Steering works. Run the implication in reverse: if you compute a direction for a concept — often as crudely as averaging activations on examples with the concept minus examples without — and add it to the residual stream at inference time, the model’s behavior shifts toward the concept. Add a “refusal” direction and the model refuses harmless requests; subtract it and (alarmingly) the model complies with harmful ones, a result established for a broad panel of open models by Arditi et al. (2024). The representation engineering line of work scaled this recipe across dozens of concepts — honesty, emotion, harmlessness — and Anthropic’s “Golden Gate Claude” demo made it famous: clamp one bridge feature high, and Claude relates every topic to the Golden Gate Bridge. Steering is Chapter 54’s subject; it appears here because it is causal evidence. A probe shows a direction correlates with a concept. Steering shows the model uses the direction: write to it, and downstream computation treats the concept as present.

Sparse autoencoders decompose activations into directions that make sense. Jumping ahead two chapters: if you take the hypothesis h≈∑ifivih \approx \sum_i f_i v_ih≈∑i​fi​vi​ seriously and ask an optimization process to find the directions — millions of them, with each input using only a few — you get a sparse autoencoder (SAE), and the directions it finds are, to a startling degree, interpretable: a feature that fires on DNA sequences, one for sycophantic praise, one for code with security vulnerabilities, one for the Golden Gate Bridge (Anthropic’s “Scaling Monosemanticity”, 2024). The hypothesis is the prior baked into the SAE architecture, so SAE success is evidence the prior fits the territory — dictionaries built on a linear decomposition would not produce clean features if activations were not approximately linearly decomposable.

Othello-GPT: the case study that convinced people

One experiment deserves to be told properly, because it answers the deepest objection to everything above. The objection: maybe language models are “stochastic parrots” — surface-statistics machines with no internal model of the world — in which case hunting for a “truth direction” is hunting for something that is not there.

Li et al. (2022) built the cleanest test of this that anyone had managed. They trained a small GPT (8 layers, 512 dimensions) on transcripts of the board game Othello — sequences of moves like E3 D3 C4 ..., tokenized one square per token, with no access to the rules, the board, or anything but move sequences. The model learned to predict legal next moves with high accuracy. The question writes itself: does the network track the actual board state — a world model — or has it merely memorized which move-sequence patterns tend to continue which ways?

Their answer came in two parts. First, presence: they trained probes to predict, from the residual stream after a given move, the state of each of the 64 squares (empty / black / white). Linear probes did poorly — hold that thought — but small nonlinear probes (two-layer MLPs) recovered the full board with high accuracy. The board state is in there. Second, and this is the part that moved the field, causality: they used the probes to edit the activations — nudging the internal representation of one square from, say, black to white via gradient steps on the probe’s output — and the model’s legal-move predictions changed to match the edited board, including for board positions that never occur in real games. The representation is not a passive correlate; it is the thing the model consults to decide what is legal. A model trained only on next-token prediction had built a causal world model, and interventions proved it.

Then came the twist. Nanda, Lee & Wattenberg (2023) revisited the result, puzzled by the failure of linear probes — which the linear representation hypothesis said should have worked. Their diagnosis: the original probes asked the wrong question. Othello players alternate turns, so “this square is black” is not a stable concept from the network’s point of view — what stays useful across the game is “this square is mine” versus “theirs,” relative to the player whose move it is. Probe for mine/empty/theirs instead of black/empty/white, and simple linear probes recover the board almost perfectly — and support the same causal edits, more cheaply. The representation was linear all along; the researchers’ ontology, not the model’s encoding, was the nonlinearity.

⚠️Careful

The Othello-GPT probe history is a permanent caution for probing practice: a failed linear probe does not show the feature is nonlinearly encoded — it may show you asked for the wrong feature. The space of concepts you find natural (black/white) and the space of concepts useful for the model’s objective (mine/theirs) need not match. When a linear probe fails, before reaching for a deeper probe, ask whether a reparameterization of the concept — relative instead of absolute, current-player-centric, offset by context — might be what the model computes.

Counter-evidence: not all features are linear

A hypothesis you cannot falsify is a slogan. This one is falsifiable, and in places it is false — the honest version of this chapter has to show you where.

The clearest documented failures are circular features. Engels et al. (2024), in a paper titled exactly for the point — Not All Language Model Features Are Linear — used sparse-autoencoder features from GPT-2 and Mistral 7B to hunt for multi-dimensional structure, and found that concepts like days of the week and months of the year are represented on closed loops: a two-dimensional circle embedded in activation space, with Monday through Sunday arranged in order around it. This is not an incidental curiosity — they showed via intervention experiments that the model uses the circular geometry to do modular arithmetic on tasks like “two days after Monday is”, with the computation behaving like rotation around the loop.

∑The math

Why a circle defeats a single direction: suppose days were encoded along one direction vvv with Monday =0= 0=0, Tuesday =1= 1=1, …, Sunday =6= 6=6. Then “advance one day” would be “add vvv” — but Sunday + 1+\,1+1 must wrap to Monday, which along a line means jumping from 666 back to 000: not addition of any fixed vector. The wrap-around is handled effortlessly by two dimensions. Encode day kkk of 7 as

(cos⁡θk,  sin⁡θk),θk=2πk7,\big(\cos \theta_k,\; \sin \theta_k\big), \qquad \theta_k = \frac{2\pi k}{7},(cosθk​,sinθk​),θk​=72πk​,

so consecutive days are 2π/7≈51.4°2\pi/7 \approx 51.4°2π/7≈51.4° apart around a circle. Now “advance one day” is a rotation by 51.4°51.4°51.4° — a linear map (a rotation matrix, the same machinery as rotary position embeddings — RoPE), applied identically everywhere on the circle, wrap-around included. Concretely: Monday (k=0k=0k=0) sits at (1.00, 0.00)(1.00,\, 0.00)(1.00,0.00); Thursday (k=3k=3k=3) at (cos⁡154.3°, sin⁡154.3°)≈(−0.90, 0.43)(\cos 154.3°,\, \sin 154.3°) \approx (-0.90,\, 0.43)(cos154.3°,sin154.3°)≈(−0.90,0.43). No single projection v⋅hv \cdot hv⋅h can rank the days in cyclic order — whatever direction you pick, some adjacent pair of days lands far apart along it. The concept is two-dimensional by nature, and the model represents it that way.

Beyond circles, the frays continue. Some properties appear to be encoded in onion-like or otherwise nonlinear arrangements — recoverable by simple nonlinear read-outs but not by any single projection. Continuous quantities sometimes occupy curved feature manifolds: a family of related states traced out as a low-dimensional curved surface rather than a straight coordinate. And even for features that are individually linear, the set of directions can carry structure the “bag of independent directions” picture ignores — related features clustering into geometric arrangements that researchers are still mapping.

✎Note

Where does this leave the hypothesis? The field’s honest position circa 2026: mostly true, with documented exceptions, and true enough to build on. The load-bearing version has weakened gracefully from “every feature is one direction” to “features occupy low-dimensional linear subspaces — usually one-dimensional, occasionally two or a few.” The reading-by-projection and writing-by-addition toolkit survives almost intact under that weakening (project onto a subspace instead of a line). But keep the epistemic status straight: this is a working assumption that most tools presuppose, not a theorem. When a linear method fails on some concept, “the concept is not linearly represented” and “my concept was the wrong one” (remember Othello) are both live explanations.

Probing: the methodology

So far “train a probe” has been a black box. Time to open it, because probing is the cheapest instrument in the interpretability toolkit and the one you are most likely to use first — and misuse first.

A probe (also probing classifier, or historically diagnostic classifier) is a small supervised model — nearly always logistic regression — trained to predict some labeled property from a frozen network’s internal activations. The network never changes; the probe is a measurement instrument clipped onto it. The name and the canonical form come from Alain & Bengio (2016), who inserted linear classifiers at every layer of image classifiers to watch class information sharpen with depth; Belinkov’s survey (2021) chronicles the method’s boom in natural language processing (NLP) and its discontents.

The full recipe:

  1. Build a labeled dataset. Pairs of (text, label) for the property you care about: 1,000 statements labeled true/false; 2,000 sentences labeled by language; prompts labeled by whether the model refused. This dataset is where probes are won or lost — more below.
  2. Collect activations. Run the frozen model on each text and cache the activation vector at a chosen hook point — typically the residual stream after layer ℓ\ellℓ, at a chosen token position (often the final token, whose representation must summarize the sequence to predict what comes next). One forward pass per example; no gradients through the model.
  3. Train the probe. Fit logistic regression from activations to labels on a training split. At d=4096d = 4096d=4096 this is a 4097-parameter model — seconds on a laptop.
  4. Sweep layers. Repeat at every layer. The resulting accuracy-vs-depth curve is itself a finding (next subsection).
  5. Run the controls. Compare against the majority-class baseline; train the same probe on a randomly initialized copy of the model (untrained networks support surprisingly good probes — random features plus a trained read-out are powerful, so beating this baseline is the real bar); and run a control task with shuffled or structurally matched nonsense labels (Hewitt & Liang, 2019) — their selectivity measure is the gap between real-task and control-task accuracy, and a probe with high accuracy but low selectivity is telling you about the probe, not the model.
  6. Evaluate out of distribution. A probe trained on one dataset of true and false statements should be tested on differently-formatted, differently-sourced statements. Probes that only work in-distribution have usually latched onto dataset quirks.
∑The math

Everything a linear probe can ever tell you is contained in that one projection.

A worked conceptual example: a truthfulness probe. Assemble a few thousand short factual statements, half true (“The city of Paris is in France”), half false (“The city of Paris is in Denmark”), across several topics — the setup of Marks & Tegmark’s “Geometry of Truth” (2023). Cache the residual stream at the final token of each statement, at every layer of, say, a Llama-class 7B model. Train one probe per layer. The typical published pattern, which their paper and successors report: at the embedding layer and first few blocks, accuracy hovers near chance — “truth” is not a property of the words, so it takes computation to determine. It then climbs steeply through the early-middle layers and plateaus around 90–95% (numbers vary by model, dataset, and topic; treat them as indicative) by the middle of the network. The learned directions generalize across topics — a probe trained on geography statements transfers, imperfectly but far above chance, to statements about other domains — and steering along the direction changes whether the model treats a statement as true. Related unsupervised work (Burns et al., 2022) found truth-like directions without any labels, by searching for a direction that assigns logically consistent probabilities to statements and their negations. None of this makes a finished lie detector — the follow-up literature is a catalog of ways such probes latch onto “plausible” or “widely asserted” rather than “true” — but it is the canonical example of the probing pipeline earning real, causally-validated structure.

What layer sweeps reveal

Step 4 of the recipe — repeating the probe at every layer — produces one of interpretability’s most information-dense plots: probe accuracy versus depth, one curve per feature. The consistent qualitative finding across models and papers:

  • Surface features saturate immediately. Language identity, token case, in-a-string-literal: high accuracy by the first few layers, since they are nearly readable off the embeddings.
  • Syntactic features rise early. Part of speech, dependency structure: strong by the first third of the network.
  • Semantic and abstract features emerge in the middle. Sentiment, topic, truthfulness, refusal-to-come: chance-level early (the information does not exist yet — it has to be computed), then a steep climb, then a plateau through the middle layers.
  • Late layers sometimes dip. Near the output, representations reorganize toward next-token logits, and abstract-property accuracy can decline — information not needed for the immediate prediction gets crowded out.

Each curve is a depth profile of when the model knows what. This is the probing-based cousin of the logit lens — decoding every layer’s residual stream through the output head to watch the next-token prediction refine with depth — which is Chapter 53’s opening tool. Both instruments report the same underlying fact from different angles: a transformer’s forward pass is a pipeline that constructs features in stages, surface to syntax to semantics to output.

The trap: information present is not information used

Now the most important warning in this chapter. A probe with 97% held-out accuracy licenses exactly one conclusion: the property is decodable from these activations. It does not license the conclusion you want to draw, which is that the model represents the property in any sense that matters — that the model computes it, uses it, or would behave differently without it. The gap between those two claims has burned enough researchers that it has a standard name in the folklore: the correlation-versus-use trap.

Three distinct failure modes live inside it:

The probe can do the work itself. Activations are a deterministic function of the input, so any input property is “in” the activations in an information-theoretic sense — the question is how much computation is needed to extract it. An expressive probe (a deep MLP) can compute the property from the activations rather than reading it off them; run the reductio and note that a sufficiently powerful probe can classify sentiment given raw pixel values of rendered text. This is the argument for linear probes as the default: a single projection has almost no capacity to compute, so high linear decodability is at least evidence the model did the work of disentangling the property. It is also the argument for Hewitt & Liang’s selectivity control: if your probe achieves similar accuracy mapping the same activations to matched nonsense labels, its accuracy reflects probe capacity plus memorization, not model structure.

Decodable does not mean consulted. Even a linearly-decodable property may be a fossil — computed incidentally, or a byproduct correlated with something else the model uses — sitting in the stream without any downstream component ever reading it. The probe cannot distinguish a signal the model relies on from one it ignores.

The dataset can leak. If your “true” statements are systematically longer, more famous, or more formulaic than your “false” ones, the probe will happily read those directions instead, and transfer tests to new distributions will expose it. Every embarrassing probing retraction in the literature is some version of this.

The escape from the trap is always the same move: graduate from observation to intervention. If you believe the model uses direction www, edit along www and check whether behavior changes as predicted. The literature’s namecheck here is amnesic probing (Elazar et al., 2020): surgically remove a property from the representations (by repeatedly projecting out every direction a probe can find for it) and measure whether the model’s actual task performance degrades — if scrubbing part-of-speech information barely dents the language model’s loss, then part-of-speech, for all its 95% probe accuracy, was not doing the causal work you assumed. Othello-GPT’s probe-guided board edits and the refusal-direction ablations are the same logic run in the positive direction. This observational-to-causal ladder is the organizing idea of Chapter 53 (activation patching, ablations) and Chapter 54; when you get there, you will recognize every technique as an answer to this section’s trap.

⚠️Careful

When you read a paper — or a press release — claiming “the model has a representation of X,” reflexively check which rung of the ladder the evidence sits on. Probe accuracy alone = X is decodable, nothing more. Probe + selectivity + transfer controls = X is plausibly represented. Probe + successful intervention along the probe’s direction = X is represented and causally used. The three claims differ enormously, and abstracts routinely state the third while the experiments support the first.

The feature menagerie: what has actually been found

A decade of probing, steering, and (lately) SAE work have assembled a real natural history of features in production-scale language models. A partial census, with sources you can open in the side panel — organized roughly by abstraction, because the abstract end is the surprising part:

  • Language, style, and format. Language identity, code vs. prose, markdown structure, rhyme-and-meter features in poetry. Among the easiest probe targets known; multilingual models also show evidence of concept directions shared across languages, with language identity carried separately.
  • Code syntax and state. Indentation depth, being inside a comment or string, variable-type information, and — in SAE work — features for bugs and security vulnerabilities in code (Scaling Monosemanticity, 2024).
  • Sentiment. A lineage with history: OpenAI’s 2017 discovery of a single “sentiment neuron” in a long short-term memory (LSTM) network trained on reviews (Radford et al., 2017) was an early hint that unsupervised sequence models learn abstract, linearly-readable properties nobody asked them for.
  • Space and time. Probes recover the real-world latitude/longitude of place names and the calendar dates of events and people from mid-layer activations, approximately linearly (Gurnee & Tegmark, 2023) — literal maps, learned from text alone.
  • Truth and falsehood. The truthfulness-probe line discussed above (Marks & Tegmark, 2023; Burns et al., 2022), plus adjacent directions distinguishing “the model is answering honestly” from “the model is role-playing a liar.”
  • Refusal. In every open chat model examined by Arditi et al. (2024), the refuse-or-comply decision is mediated by (approximately) a single direction — remove it via projection and the model stops refusing; add it and it refuses benign requests. Probably the single most operationally consequential feature found to date, for attackers and defenders alike.
  • Sycophancy and social features. Directions and SAE features tracking flattery, agreement-with-the-user, and user-stated opinions (Sharma et al., 2023 documents the behavioral side; SAE and steering work supplies the internal handles).
  • Persona and self-model features. Directions that track the character the assistant is playing — “evil,” sycophancy, a propensity to hallucinate — whose activation predicts and controls persona drift (Anthropic’s persona vectors work, 2025), and SAE features active on the assistant’s own dialogue turns. Chapter 56 picks this thread up.
⚙️In practice

How this cashes out in 2026 practice: probes have quietly become production monitoring instruments, not research tools alone. Because a linear probe costs one dot product per token, you can run hundreds of them against a frontier model’s activations at serving time for negligible overhead — no extra forward passes, unlike an LLM-judge classifier reading the transcript. Published and publicly discussed uses include jailbreak/misuse classifiers on activations, probes flagging evaluation-awareness and deception-adjacent features during safety testing (Anthropic and others have described such internal monitoring in frontier safety reports), and probe-based data filtering. The pattern to remember: SAEs and circuit analysis to discover and validate what a direction means; cheap linear probes to watch for it at scale. Treat specific deployment details as fast-moving and partially non-public.

Activations 101: what tensor, which hook, where to tap

This chapter has said “cache the activations” a dozen times; before the methods chapters, let us make that concrete enough to act on, using the vocabulary of TransformerLens — the open-source library (by Neel Nanda and collaborators) that most published mechanistic-interpretability work on small models uses, and whose naming conventions the field talks in.

Run a decoder-only transformer on a prompt of TTT tokens and every layer produces activations shaped [batch, T, d][\text{batch},\, T,\, d][batch,T,d] — one ddd-dimensional vector per token position per layer, the geometry you know from Chapter 25. For GPT-2 small (the standard lab rat: 12 layers, d=768d = 768d=768, 12 heads per layer), a 20-token prompt yields a 12×20×76812 \times 20 \times 76812×20×768 residual-stream history, plus per-component internals. The standard hook points — named places where you can read or modify the forward pass:

  • resid_pre / resid_post — the residual stream entering/leaving each block. The default probing site, because it is the shared communication bus every component reads from and writes to (Chapter 16).
  • attn_out and mlp_out — each sub-block’s contribution before it is added into the stream: what attention (or the MLP) wrote this layer.
  • z (per-head values) and pattern (per-head attention weights) — the head-level internals, shaped [batch,T,nheads,dhead][\text{batch}, T, n_{\text{heads}}, d_{\text{head}}][batch,T,nheads​,dhead​] and [batch,nheads,T,T][\text{batch}, n_{\text{heads}}, T, T][batch,nheads​,T,T] respectively; these become central in Chapter 52.
  • pre / post inside the MLP — the hidden layer around the nonlinearity (4d4d4d-dimensional in classic architectures), where “neurons” in the polysemanticity discussion live.

Practical defaults the literature has converged on: probe the residual stream (component-specific sites answer “who computed it,” the stream answers “is it represented”); for sequence-level properties, take the final token position (causal attention means only late positions can have aggregated the whole sequence); sweep all layers rather than guessing; and expect mid-network layers to be the sweet spot for abstract features. For frontier-scale models, the same ideas run through PyTorch forward hooks or libraries like nnsight; the concepts transfer unchanged even when the tooling does.

Where this leaves us

  • You can state the linear representation hypothesis precisely — activations decompose as h≈∑ifivih \approx \sum_i f_i v_ih≈∑i​fi​vi​ over fixed feature directions — and explain why the residual-stream architecture makes it the natural encoding: components communicate through a shared sum, read by dot products.
  • You can name the four lines of evidence — word2vec arithmetic, probe success, steering success, SAE decompositions — and tell the Othello-GPT story with both of its lessons: models trained on next-token prediction build causal world models, and a failed linear probe may mean your ontology is wrong (black/white vs. mine/theirs), not the model’s encoding.
  • You can also state the counter-evidence: circular features for days-of-week and months (rotation, not addition), onion-like encodings, and feature manifolds — so the working assumption is “low-dimensional linear subspaces, usually 1D,” held with appropriate looseness.
  • You can run the full probing recipe — labeled data, frozen forward passes, per-layer logistic regression, majority/random-model/control-task baselines, out-of-distribution tests — and read a layer-sweep curve as a depth profile of when the model computes what.
  • You can articulate the correlation-versus-use trap — probes certify that information is present, never that it is used — and name the escape ladder: selectivity controls, amnesic probing, and causal interventions.
  • You know where to tap a model: the residual stream at the last token, swept over layers, in TransformerLens vocabulary (resid_post, mlp_out, z) that Chapters 51–53 will use freely.

One large question is left hanging, and it is the hinge of the whole part. If features are directions, and a model has only ddd dimensions but the world has vastly more than ddd features worth representing — how does it fit them all in, and what does that do to the neurons we observe? That is superposition, next.
