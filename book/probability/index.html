Probability for Deep Learning

Distributions, expectation, Bayes, the chain rule of probability, maximum likelihood, and softmax — the exact probability toolkit that language models are built from.

In this chapter

  2. Distributions: the categorical and the Gaussian
  3. Expectation and variance: what to expect, and how wrong to expect to be
  4. Conditional probability, independence, and Bayes
  5. The chain rule of probability — the identity that is language modeling
  6. Maximum likelihood: how distributions are fit to data
  7. Softmax: turning scores into a distribution
  8. Sampling: how a model actually chooses a word
  9. Where this leaves us

Every time a language model produces a word, it is doing probability. Not metaphorically — literally. The final act of a transformer, after all the attention and matrix multiplication you will meet in later chapters, is to output a probability distribution over its entire vocabulary and then draw one token from it. Training the model means adjusting billions of parameters so those distributions place more probability on the words that actually come next in human text. The loss function is a probability formula. The sampling temperature slider in every AI playground is a probability formula. Even the random numbers that initialize a network before training begins are draws from a probability distribution.

So this chapter is not background reading. It is the load-bearing wall. By the end of it you will have seen, in miniature, almost the entire conceptual core of a language model: a distribution over discrete outcomes (the categorical), a rule for chaining conditional distributions into a distribution over sequences (the chain rule of probability), a principle for fitting distributions to data (maximum likelihood), a function for turning a network’s raw scores into a valid distribution (softmax), and a procedure for drawing a sample from it (inverse-CDF sampling). Everything after this chapter is about building a better machine for producing those distributions.

The good news: you need surprisingly little of probability theory’s vast edifice. No measure theory, no exotic distributions, no hypothesis testing. What you need is a small set of ideas used with total fluency, because they appear on nearly every page of the rest of the book. We will build each one from scratch, with numbers you can check by hand.

Two ways to read a probability

Start with the philosophical question, because it matters for how you interpret model outputs. What does P(A)=0.3\P(A) = 0.3P(A)=0.3 mean?

The frequentist reading: probability is long-run frequency. If you could repeat the experiment many times, the event AAA would happen about 30% of the time. This is the natural reading for coin flips and dice, and for statements like “the model assigns 0.3 to the token mat” when what you mean is “over a huge corpus, in contexts like this one, mat was the continuation about 30% of the time.”

The Bayesian reading: probability is degree of belief. P(A)=0.3\P(A) = 0.3P(A)=0.3 means “given everything I know, I’d bet on AAA at 3-to-7 odds.” No repetition required — you can put a probability on a one-off event, like “this particular email is spam” or “the next token of this never-before-seen sentence is mat.”

Deep learning uses both stances without apology, and it helps to know which is in play. Training data is treated frequentist-style: we fit models to observed frequencies in a corpus. But a trained model’s output on a novel prompt is best read as a belief — a calibrated forecast about a sentence that has never existed before. When a model assigns probability 0.87 to a token, there is no ensemble of parallel universes being counted; there is a compressed summary of patterns in training data, expressed as betting odds.

✎Note

“Calibrated” is doing real work in that sentence. A forecaster is calibrated if, among all the times it says “70%”, the event happens about 70% of the time. Base language models straight out of pretraining are famously well-calibrated on many tasks, and post-training (reinforcement learning from human feedback — the RLHF stage) famously damages that calibration; GPT-4’s technical report showed the effect starkly. We return to this in evaluating language models.

Whichever reading you prefer, the mechanics are identical, and they rest on three rules you can treat as the axioms: probabilities are never negative; the probabilities of all mutually exclusive outcomes of an experiment sum to exactly 1; and the probability that either of two mutually exclusive events happens is the sum of their probabilities. Everything in this chapter is consequences of those three rules plus one definition (conditional probability, coming shortly).

Distributions: the categorical and the Gaussian

A random variable is a quantity whose value is determined by chance — the outcome of a die roll, the height of a randomly chosen person, the next token in a sentence. A probability distribution is the complete description of a random variable: the full list of what values it can take and how much probability each one gets. Deep learning runs on many distributions, but two of them do the overwhelming majority of the work, and they conveniently represent the two great families: discrete and continuous.

The categorical: the distribution LLMs live in

A categorical distribution is a distribution over a finite list of kkk outcomes, described by nothing more than a list of kkk probabilities p1,p2,…,pkp_1, p_2, \dots, p_kp1​,p2​,…,pk​ with each pi≥0p_i \ge 0pi​≥0 and ∑i=1kpi=1\sum_{i=1}^{k} p_i = 1∑i=1k​pi​=1. That’s the whole definition. A fair die is a categorical with k=6k=6k=6 and every pi=1/6p_i = 1/6pi​=1/6. A loaded die is a categorical with unequal entries.

This humble object is the distribution of language modeling. A large language model (LLM) with a vocabulary of kkk tokens — around 128,000 for Llama 3, around 200,000 for the tokenizer used by GPT-4o-era OpenAI models (both figures approximate; see tokenization) — outputs, at every step, one categorical distribution over those kkk tokens. The entire multi-billion-parameter network exists to compute the pip_ipi​‘s well.

Notice the long tail in the figure. Real next-token distributions concentrate most mass on a handful of plausible tokens, then spread a thin film of probability over everything else — including tokens that would be bizarre but not impossible. Managing that tail is what the sampling strategies at the end of this chapter (and in inference and serving) are about.

The Gaussian: the distribution of continuous noise

Continuous random variables — heights, weights, neural network activations — can take any real value, so we cannot list probabilities one outcome at a time. Instead we use a probability density function p(x)p(x)p(x): a curve whose area over an interval gives the probability of landing in that interval. The probability of any exact single value is zero (a point has no width); only intervals carry probability.

The king of continuous distributions is the Gaussian (or normal) distribution, written N(μ,σ2)\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)N(μ,σ2):

p(x)  =  12πσ2  e−(x−μ)22σ2,p(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\; e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}},p(x)=2πσ2​1​e−2σ2(x−μ)2​,

where μ\muμ is the mean (the center of the bell) and σ\sigmaσ is the standard deviation (its width; σ2\sigma^2σ2 is called the variance). The messy constant 12πσ2\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}2πσ2​1​ exists only to make the total area equal 1, as every density must. The shape is what matters: values near μ\muμ are likely, and probability dies off extremely fast as you move away — the e−(⋅)2e^{-(\cdot)^2}e−(⋅)2 makes the tails thinner than almost any other common distribution.

Why does this one shape appear everywhere? The deep reason is the central limit theorem: when you add up many small independent random effects, the sum tends toward a Gaussian regardless of what distribution the individual effects had. Heights are Gaussian-ish because they are sums of many small genetic and environmental nudges. Neural network activations early in training are Gaussian-ish because they are sums of many weighted inputs — a fact Chapter 12 turns into engineering.

⚙️In practice

Where you will meet Gaussians in this book:

  • Initialization. Before training, weights are drawn from a Gaussian with mean 0 and a carefully chosen variance — e.g. He initialization, N(0, 2/nin)\mathcal{N}(0,\, 2/n_{\text{in}})N(0,2/nin​) where ninn_{\text{in}}nin​ is the number of inputs to the layer, from He et al. 2015. Get the variance wrong and signals explode or vanish exponentially with depth (Chapter 12).
  • Diffusion models. Image generators like Stable Diffusion are trained by drowning images in Gaussian noise and learning to reverse the process (Ho et al. 2020; Chapter 42).
  • Gradient noise. The randomness that minibatch training injects into gradients is approximately Gaussian by the central limit theorem — it is part of why stochastic gradient descent behaves the way it does (Chapter 10).

Expectation and variance: what to expect, and how wrong to expect to be

If a distribution is the full description of a random variable, the expectation (or expected value, or mean) is its one-number summary: the probability-weighted average of all outcomes.

∑The math

For a discrete random variable XXX taking values x1,…,xkx_1, \dots, x_kx1​,…,xk​ with probabilities p1,…,pkp_1, \dots, p_kp1​,…,pk​:

E[X]  =  ∑i=1kpi xi.\E[X] \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_i \, x_i .E[X]=i=1∑k​pi​xi​.

For a continuous variable, the sum becomes an integral against the density: E[X]=∫x p(x) dx\E[X] = \int x\, p(x)\, dxE[X]=∫xp(x)dx. Expectation is linear — for any constants a,ba, ba,b and any random variables X,YX, YX,Y (independent or not!), E[aX+bY]=a E[X]+b E[Y]\E[aX + bY] = a\,\E[X] + b\,\E[Y]E[aX+bY]=aE[X]+bE[Y]. This innocuous-looking property is used constantly and is worth committing to memory.

The variance measures spread: the expected squared distance from the mean,

Var⁡(X)  =  E ⁣[(X−E[X])2]  =  E[X2]−E[X]2,\Var(X) \;=\; \E\!\left[(X - \E[X])^2\right] \;=\; \E[X^2] - \E[X]^2,Var(X)=E[(X−E[X])2]=E[X2]−E[X]2,

where the second form follows by expanding the square and using linearity. The standard deviation σ=Var⁡(X)\sigma = \sqrt{\Var(X)}σ=Var(X)​ puts spread back in the same units as XXX.

✏️Worked example

A fair six-sided die. Each face has pi=1/6p_i = 1/6pi​=1/6, so

E[X]=16(1+2+3+4+5+6)=216=3.5.\E[X] = \tfrac{1}{6}(1+2+3+4+5+6) = \tfrac{21}{6} = 3.5 .E[X]=61​(1+2+3+4+5+6)=621​=3.5.

Note that 3.5 is not a possible outcome — expectation is a center of mass, not a prediction of any single roll. For the variance we first need E[X2]=16(1+4+9+16+25+36)=916≈15.17\E[X^2] = \tfrac{1}{6}(1+4+9+16+25+36) = \tfrac{91}{6} \approx 15.17E[X2]=61​(1+4+9+16+25+36)=691​≈15.17, so

Var⁡(X)=15.17−3.52=15.17−12.25=2.92,σ≈1.71.\Var(X) = 15.17 - 3.5^2 = 15.17 - 12.25 = 2.92, \qquad \sigma \approx 1.71 .Var(X)=15.17−3.52=15.17−12.25=2.92,σ≈1.71.

A typical roll lands about 1.7 away from 3.5 — which matches intuition, since the outcomes are spread evenly from 1 to 6.

The estimator view: expectations you can’t compute, sample means you can

Here is the move that makes expectation the workhorse of deep learning. Most expectations we care about are over distributions we cannot enumerate — “the expected loss over all possible English text” involves a sum with more terms than atoms in the universe. But we can always estimate an expectation by sampling: draw nnn independent samples x(1),…,x(n)x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(n)}x(1),…,x(n) and average them,

Xˉn  =  1n∑j=1nx(j).\bar{X}_n \;=\; \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} x^{(j)} .Xˉn​=n1​j=1∑n​x(j).

The law of large numbers says Xˉn→E[X]\bar{X}_n \to \E[X]Xˉn​→E[X] as nnn grows. Better, we can say how fast. If the samples are independent with variance σ2\sigma^2σ2, then by linearity E[Xˉn]=E[X]\E[\bar{X}_n] = \E[X]E[Xˉn​]=E[X] (the estimate is unbiased — right on average), and because variances of independent variables add,

Var⁡(Xˉn)=1n2∑j=1nσ2=σ2n,sotypical error≈σn.\Var(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \sigma^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \text{typical error} \approx \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} .Var(Xˉn​)=n21​j=1∑n​σ2=nσ2​,sotypical error≈n​σ​.

The error shrinks like 1/n1/\sqrt{n}1/n​: quadruple the samples to halve the error. For our die, averaging n=100n = 100n=100 rolls gives a typical error of 1.71/100≈0.171.71/\sqrt{100} \approx 0.171.71/100​≈0.17 — the average will usually land between about 3.3 and 3.7.

💡Intuition

Hold onto this pattern, because it is minibatch training. The “true” training objective is an expectation of the loss over the whole dataset (or, idealistically, over all text ever). Nobody computes it. Instead, each step samples a minibatch of a few hundred or thousand examples and averages their loss gradients — a sample mean standing in for an expectation, correct on average, noisy with standard error shrinking like 1/n1/\sqrt{n}1/n​. Every gradient a neural network is trained on, ever, is an estimator of this kind. The full story is in gradient descent and backpropagation.

⚠️Careful

The σ2/n\sigma^2/nσ2/n formula required independence. If your samples are correlated — say, 100 consecutive sentences from the same document — the effective sample size is much smaller than 100 and the error shrinks far more slowly than 1/n1/\sqrt{n}1/n​. This is precisely why training pipelines shuffle data aggressively (Chapter 31): correlated batches produce misleading gradient estimates.

Conditional probability, independence, and Bayes

Everything so far treated one random variable in isolation. The action starts when variables interact — because language is nothing but interaction: the probability of mat depends utterly on the words before it.

Conditioning and the product rule

The conditional probability of AAA given BBB is defined as

P(A∣B)  =  P(A,B)P(B),\P(A \mid B) \;=\; \frac{\P(A, B)}{\P(B)},P(A∣B)=P(B)P(A,B)​,

where P(A,B)\P(A, B)P(A,B) is the joint probability that both happen. In words: restrict attention to the worlds where BBB occurred, and ask what fraction of those worlds also have AAA. The vertical bar reads “given.”

Rearranging the definition gives the product rule, which we will use relentlessly:

P(A,B)  =  P(B) P(A∣B).\P(A, B) \;=\; \P(B)\, \P(A \mid B) .P(A,B)=P(B)P(A∣B).

To have both things happen: first BBB happens (probability P(B)\P(B)P(B)), then AAA happens in a world where BBB already has (probability P(A∣B)\P(A \mid B)P(A∣B)).

Two events are independent when conditioning changes nothing: P(A∣B)=P(A)\P(A \mid B) = \P(A)P(A∣B)=P(A), or equivalently P(A,B)=P(A)P(B)\P(A, B) = \P(A)\P(B)P(A,B)=P(A)P(B). Independence is a modeling assumption, and knowing when it is being assumed matters. The standard assumption that training examples are i.i.d. — independent and identically distributed — is what justified the σ2/n\sigma^2/nσ2/n argument above. But within a sentence, tokens are spectacularly dependent, and that dependence is the entire object of study. A model that treated tokens as independent would generate word salad drawn from overall word frequencies: “the of and a to in…” A language model is precisely a machine for capturing the dependence.

Bayes’ rule

The product rule can be written in two orders — P(A,B)=P(B)P(A∣B)\P(A,B) = \P(B)\P(A \mid B)P(A,B)=P(B)P(A∣B) and P(A,B)=P(A)P(B∣A)\P(A,B) = \P(A)\P(B \mid A)P(A,B)=P(A)P(B∣A) — and setting them equal gives Bayes’ rule:

P(A∣B)  =  P(B∣A) P(A)P(B).\P(A \mid B) \;=\; \frac{\P(B \mid A)\,\P(A)}{\P(B)} .P(A∣B)=P(B)P(B∣A)P(A)​.

Its power is that it reverses the direction of conditioning: from “how likely is the evidence given the hypothesis” (usually easy to model) to “how likely is the hypothesis given the evidence” (what you want).

✏️Worked example

The rare-disease problem — the canonical demonstration of why the reversal is not intuitive. A disease affects 1 in 1,000 people. A test catches 99% of true cases (P(+∣sick)=0.99\P(+ \mid \text{sick}) = 0.99P(+∣sick)=0.99) but also fires falsely on 5% of healthy people (P(+∣healthy)=0.05\P(+ \mid \text{healthy}) = 0.05P(+∣healthy)=0.05). You test positive. How worried should you be?

Most people say ~95–99%. Bayes says: first compute the total probability of testing positive, splitting by whether you’re sick (this denominator trick is called the law of total probability):

P(+)=0.99×0.001⏟sick and caught+0.05×0.999⏟healthy, false alarm=0.00099+0.04995=0.05094.\P(+) = \underbrace{0.99 \times 0.001}_{\text{sick and caught}} + \underbrace{0.05 \times 0.999}_{\text{healthy, false alarm}} = 0.00099 + 0.04995 = 0.05094 .P(+)=sick and caught0.99×0.001​​+healthy, false alarm0.05×0.999​​=0.00099+0.04995=0.05094.

Then

P(sick∣+)=0.99×0.0010.05094=0.000990.05094≈0.019.\P(\text{sick} \mid +) = \frac{0.99 \times 0.001}{0.05094} = \frac{0.00099}{0.05094} \approx 0.019 .P(sick∣+)=0.050940.99×0.001​=0.050940.00099​≈0.019.

About 1.9%. The intuition repair: imagine 1,000 people. About 1 is sick and (almost surely) tests positive. Of the 999 healthy, about 50 also test positive. Your positive result puts you in a room with 51 people, only one of whom is sick. The rarity of the disease — the prior P(sick)\P(\text{sick})P(sick) — dominates the accuracy of the test.

💡Intuition

Bayes’ rule is the mathematics of updating beliefs on evidence: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. You will meet it again wearing different costumes: maximum likelihood (below) is Bayes with the prior ignored; regularization (Chapter 13) is Bayes with the prior restored; and the spam-filter framing — P(spam∣words)∝P(words∣spam)P(spam)\P(\text{spam} \mid \text{words}) \propto \P(\text{words} \mid \text{spam})\P(\text{spam})P(spam∣words)∝P(words∣spam)P(spam) — was the workhorse of practical machine learning for a decade before deep learning.

The chain rule of probability — the identity that is language modeling

Now the centerpiece. Apply the product rule not to two events but to a whole sequence. For three variables:

P(w1,w2,w3)=P(w1,w2) P(w3∣w1,w2)=P(w1) P(w2∣w1) P(w3∣w1,w2),\P(w_1, w_2, w_3) = \P(w_1, w_2)\,\P(w_3 \mid w_1, w_2) = \P(w_1)\,\P(w_2 \mid w_1)\,\P(w_3 \mid w_1, w_2),P(w1​,w2​,w3​)=P(w1​,w2​)P(w3​∣w1​,w2​)=P(w1​)P(w2​∣w1​)P(w3​∣w1​,w2​),

where each step is one application of the product rule, peeling off the last variable. Repeating the peel nnn times gives the chain rule of probability:

P(w1,w2,…,wn)  =  ∏t=1nP(wt∣w1,…,wt−1)  =  ∏t=1nP(wt∣w<t),\P(w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^{n} \P(w_t \mid w_1, \dots, w_{t-1}) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^{n} \P(w_t \mid w_{<t}),P(w1​,w2​,…,wn​)=t=1∏n​P(wt​∣w1​,…,wt−1​)=t=1∏n​P(wt​∣w<t​),

where w<tw_{<t}w<t​ is shorthand for “all the tokens before position ttt” (and the t=1t=1t=1 factor is the unconditional P(w1)\P(w_1)P(w1​)).

Read it slowly, because this single identity is language modeling. The left side is the probability of an entire sentence — a monstrous object, a distribution over all possible token sequences, of which there are more than atoms in the observable universe even for modest lengths. The right side says that monster factors exactly — no approximation, no assumption, this is pure algebra — into a product of small, manageable pieces: one categorical distribution per position, each conditioned on the tokens so far. To model all of language, it suffices to get good at one question, asked over and over: given the text so far, what comes next?

Three remarks the factorization deserves.

First, it is exact. Older approaches (the nnn-gram models of the 1990s–2000s) made it tractable by truncating the condition — “assume wtw_twt​ depends only on the previous 2 tokens” — which is a genuine, damaging approximation. Modern transformers keep the full condition w<tw_{<t}w<t​, up to their context window. The chain rule itself was never the approximation; the truncation was.

Second, it turns generation into a loop. To sample a sentence: sample w1w_1w1​ from P(w1)\P(w_1)P(w1​), then w2w_2w2​ from P(w2∣w1)\P(w_2 \mid w_1)P(w2​∣w1​), then w3w_3w3​ from P(w3∣w1,w2)\P(w_3 \mid w_1, w_2)P(w3​∣w1​,w2​), and so on — each step a draw from one categorical. This is autoregressive generation, and it is why LLMs produce text one token at a time: the factorization is the algorithm.

Third, it turns training into next-token prediction. Making the left side big on real text is the same as making every factor on the right big — reward the model for assigning high probability to each actual next token. The idea of learning these conditionals with a neural network dates to Bengio et al. 2003; the full modern story is in what a language model is.

Maximum likelihood: how distributions are fit to data

We now have distributions and data. The missing piece is a principle for choosing a distribution’s parameters from data. The dominant answer, and the one that trains every LLM, is maximum likelihood estimation (MLE): choose the parameters under which the observed data would have been most probable.

∑The math

Fit a coin. Let θ\thetaθ be the unknown probability of heads. You flip 10 times and see 7 heads and 3 tails, in some particular order. If flips are independent, the product rule gives the probability of that exact sequence:

L(θ)  =  θ7(1−θ)3.L(\theta) \;=\; \theta^7 (1-\theta)^3 .L(θ)=θ7(1−θ)3.

LLL is the likelihood: the data is fixed (it already happened), and we view the probability as a function of the parameter. MLE says: maximize it. Products are awkward to differentiate, so take the logarithm — legal because log⁡\loglog is monotonic, so it moves the maximum’s value but not its location:

log⁡L(θ)  =  7log⁡θ  +  3log⁡(1−θ).\log L(\theta) \;=\; 7 \log\theta \;+\; 3 \log(1-\theta) .logL(θ)=7logθ+3log(1−θ).

Differentiate with respect to θ\thetaθ (recall from Chapter 4 that ddθlog⁡θ=1/θ\frac{d}{d\theta}\log\theta = 1/\thetadθd​logθ=1/θ, and the chain rule puts a −1-1−1 on the second term) and set to zero:

dlog⁡Ldθ  =  7θ−31−θ  =  0    ⟹    7(1−θ)=3θ    ⟹    θ^=710.\frac{d \log L}{d\theta} \;=\; \frac{7}{\theta} - \frac{3}{1-\theta} \;=\; 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; 7(1-\theta) = 3\theta \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \hat\theta = \frac{7}{10} .dθdlogL​=θ7​−1−θ3​=0⟹7(1−θ)=3θ⟹θ^=107​.

The maximum-likelihood estimate is the observed frequency, 0.70.70.7. In general, kkk heads in nnn flips gives θ^=k/n\hat\theta = k/nθ^=k/n — the principle recovers the answer you would have guessed, which is exactly what you want from a principle: it agrees with intuition on easy cases so you can trust it on hard ones, where intuition is silent and the same recipe (write the likelihood, take logs, ascend the gradient) still runs.

Why logs are not optional

Taking the log was presented as a convenience. At deep-learning scale it is a survival requirement, for two reasons.

Products become sums. The chain rule of probability writes a document’s probability as a product of thousands of factors. Sums are what calculus and computers handle gracefully: the derivative of a sum is the sum of derivatives, and a sum over tokens can be split across minibatches and GPUs. The log turns ∏tP(wt∣w<t)\prod_t \P(w_t \mid w_{<t})∏t​P(wt​∣w<t​) into ∑tlog⁡P(wt∣w<t)\sum_t \log \P(w_t \mid w_{<t})∑t​logP(wt​∣w<t​) — a per-token quantity you can average.

Underflow is certain, not hypothetical. Suppose a model is doing quite well on a 250-token passage, averaging probability 0.050.050.05 per token (for a 100k-token vocabulary, that is good). The passage’s probability is

0.05250  =  10250log⁡100.05  ≈  10−325,0.05^{250} \;=\; 10^{250 \log_{10} 0.05} \;\approx\; 10^{-325},0.05250=10250log10​0.05≈10−325,

which is below the smallest positive normal number in float64 (≈10−308\approx 10^{-308}≈10−308). The computed probability is literally zero, and every comparison and gradient computed from it is garbage. In log space the same quantity is 250×ln⁡0.05≈−749250 \times \ln 0.05 \approx -749250×ln0.05≈−749 — an utterly ordinary number. Probabilities of sequences are only ever represented as log-probabilities. When you see “logprobs” in an inference API, this is why.

💡Intuition

Here is the preview that ties this chapter to the next three. Maximizing log-likelihood over a dataset is the same as minimizing its negative, −∑tlog⁡P(wt∣w<t)-\sum_t \log \P(w_t \mid w_{<t})−∑t​logP(wt​∣w<t​) — the quantity called cross-entropy loss. The loss function that trains GPT-5-class and Claude-class models is maximum likelihood on the chain-rule factorization, and nothing more exotic than that. Information theory will reinterpret the same expression as a compression cost measured in bits, and loss functions will differentiate it.

🕰History

Maximum likelihood was championed by R. A. Fisher in the 1920s, building on earlier least-squares ideas of Gauss — who introduced the Gaussian distribution precisely as the noise law under which least squares is maximum likelihood. That neural language models should be trained this way was settled by Bengio et al. 2003; a century later, the training objective of frontier AI is still Fisher’s principle applied at planetary scale.

Softmax: turning scores into a distribution

One gap remains between neural networks and everything above. A network’s final layer produces a vector of raw real-valued scores — logits — one per vocabulary token: some positive, some negative, summing to nothing in particular. A categorical distribution needs non-negative numbers summing to 1. The bridge is softmax, which you glimpsed in Chapter 0 and now meet properly.

softmax⁡(z)i  =  ezi∑j=1kezj\softmax(z)_i \;=\; \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k} e^{z_j}}softmax(z)i​=∑j=1k​ezj​ezi​​

where z=(z1,…,zk)z = (z_1, \dots, z_k)z=(z1​,…,zk​) are the logits. Exponentiate (now everything is positive), then divide by the total (now everything sums to 1). Because exe^xex is strictly increasing, the ranking of tokens by logit is preserved exactly; softmax changes the scale of preferences, never their order.

Why eee?

A fair question — the definition looks arbitrary. Why exponentials at all, and why base eee specifically? The requirements select the function almost uniquely. We need a map from scores to probabilities that is:

  1. Positive everywhere, so no outcome gets negative probability even for very negative logits;
  2. Monotonic, so a higher score always means higher probability;
  3. Differentiable, so gradients can flow through it during training (Chapter 10 explains why this is non-negotiable).

Exponentials bzb^zbz satisfy all three for any base b>1b > 1b>1. And the choice of base turns out to be no choice at all: since bz=ezln⁡bb^z = e^{z \ln b}bz=ezlnb, softmax with base bbb equals softmax base-eee with all logits scaled by ln⁡b\ln blnb — which, as we will see in a moment, is exactly a temperature change. The family is forced; the base is a unit convention.

So why the convention eee? Because ddxex=ex\frac{d}{dx}e^x = e^xdxd​ex=ex — the exponential is its own derivative, with no stray constant. That single fact makes the calculus downstream astonishingly clean: when softmax is combined with the log-likelihood loss above, the gradient of the loss with respect to logit ziz_izi​ collapses (after cancellations you will perform in Chapter 9) to

∂L∂zi  =  pi−yi,\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_i} \;=\; p_i - y_i,∂zi​∂L​=pi​−yi​,

“predicted probability minus actual outcome” — and with any other base it would carry a ln⁡b\ln blnb everywhere.

🕰History

Physicists met this function first. The Boltzmann distribution (also called the Gibbs measure, 1870s) says a physical system at temperature TTT occupies a state with energy EiE_iEi​ with probability proportional to e−Ei/Te^{-E_i / T}e−Ei​/T (in suitable units). Softmax is precisely Boltzmann with Ei=−ziE_i = -z_iEi​=−zi​: logits are negative energies, likely tokens are low-energy states, and the “temperature” terminology in AI is a direct inheritance. The name “softmax” itself came into machine learning via John Bridle (1989), because the function is a soft, differentiable version of picking the argmax.

Shift invariance and the trick that prevents overflow

Softmax has a symmetry with practical teeth: adding the same constant ccc to every logit changes nothing.

softmax⁡(z+c)i=ezi+c∑jezj+c=ec eziec∑jezj=softmax⁡(z)i.\softmax(z + c)_i = \frac{e^{z_i + c}}{\sum_j e^{z_j + c}} = \frac{e^{c}\, e^{z_i}}{e^{c} \sum_j e^{z_j}} = \softmax(z)_i .softmax(z+c)i​=∑j​ezj​+cezi​+c​=ec∑j​ezj​ecezi​​=softmax(z)i​.

The ece^cec factors out of every term and cancels. Only differences between logits matter — a logit has no absolute meaning, which is worth remembering when you inspect model internals later in Part VII.

This symmetry is also a numerical lifesaver. Logits from a real network can be large; suppose z=[1000, 1001, 1002]z = [1000,\, 1001,\, 1002]z=[1000,1001,1002]. Computing e1000e^{1000}e1000 overflows instantly — float32 gives up around e89e^{89}e89, float64 around e710e^{710}e710 — and the naive softmax returns a useless ∞/∞\infty/\infty∞/∞. But shift invariance lets us subtract the maximum logit first, at zero cost to the answer:

z−max⁡(z)=[−2, −1, 0]    ⟹    e−2≈0.135,e−1≈0.368,e0=1.z - \max(z) = [-2,\, -1,\, 0] \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; e^{-2} \approx 0.135,\quad e^{-1} \approx 0.368,\quad e^{0} = 1 .z−max(z)=[−2,−1,0]⟹e−2≈0.135,e−1≈0.368,e0=1.

The sum is 1.5031.5031.503, and dividing gives p≈[0.090, 0.245, 0.665]p \approx [0.090,\, 0.245,\, 0.665]p≈[0.090,0.245,0.665]. This is the log-sum-exp trick (the name comes from the companion identity log⁡∑jezj=max⁡(z)+log⁡∑jezj−max⁡(z)\log\sum_j e^{z_j} = \max(z) + \log\sum_j e^{z_j - \max(z)}log∑j​ezj​=max(z)+log∑j​ezj​−max(z), used when you need the log-normalizer itself, as the cross-entropy loss does). ⚠️Careful

If you ever implement softmax or cross-entropy by hand and see NaN losses a few hundred steps into training, this is overwhelmingly the likely cause: logits grew until eze^{z}ez overflowed. Subtract the max. Relatedly, prefer fused log_softmax over log(softmax(x)) — the latter can round a tiny probability to exactly zero and then take log⁡0=−∞\log 0 = -\inftylog0=−∞.

Temperature

Divide all logits by a temperature T>0T > 0T>0 before the softmax:

pi  =  ezi/T∑jezj/T.p_i \;=\; \frac{e^{z_i / T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j / T}} .pi​=∑j​ezj​/Tezi​/T​.

Sampling: how a model actually chooses a word

One step remains to close the loop: given the categorical p=(p1,…,pk)p = (p_1, \dots, p_k)p=(p1​,…,pk​) that softmax produced, how does a computer — a deterministic machine — draw a random token from it? The standard answer is inverse-CDF sampling (CDF: cumulative distribution function), and it is pleasingly simple.

Lay the probabilities end to end as segments of the interval [0,1][0, 1][0,1]: token 1 owns [0,p1)[0, p_1)[0,p1​), token 2 owns [p1,p1+p2)[p_1, p_1 + p_2)[p1​,p1​+p2​), and so on — each token gets a stretch of the line whose length is its probability, and the running totals ci=p1+⋯+pic_i = p_1 + \cdots + p_ici​=p1​+⋯+pi​ are the CDF. Now draw a single uniform random number uuu between 0 and 1 (the one primitive every computer provides) and return the token whose segment contains uuu. A token with probability 0.420.420.42 owns 42% of the line, so it is chosen 42% of the time. That is the entire algorithm.

✏️Worked example

Our running distribution: mat 0.6650.6650.665, floor 0.2450.2450.245, couch 0.0900.0900.090. The cumulative sums are c=[0.665, 0.910, 1.000]c = [0.665,\, 0.910,\, 1.000]c=[0.665,0.910,1.000]. Suppose the random draw is u=0.734u = 0.734u=0.734. Scan for the first cumulative value that reaches uuu: 0.665<0.734≤0.9100.665 < 0.734 \le 0.9100.665<0.734≤0.910, so the sample is floor — the second-choice token, this time. With u=0.31u = 0.31u=0.31 it would have been mat; with u=0.95u = 0.95u=0.95, couch. Run the sentence again and you may get a different word; that is all the “creativity” of a language model is.

Wrap this in the chain-rule loop and you have, end to end, the complete generation algorithm of a language model: run the network on the context, get logits, softmax (with temperature) into a categorical, draw uuu, pick the segment, append the token, repeat. Everything else — the transformer itself — is machinery for making the pip_ipi​‘s good.

⚙️In practice

Production systems rarely sample from the raw categorical, because the long tail bites: with ~130,000 tokens each carrying a sliver of probability, the tail’s total mass is substantial, so a tail token — occasionally an absurd one — gets picked every few dozen draws, and one absurd token can derail all subsequent conditioning. Holtzman et al. 2019 diagnosed this and proposed nucleus (top-p) sampling: keep only the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds ppp (say 0.9), renormalize to sum to 1, and sample from that truncated categorical. Its cousin top-k keeps the kkk highest-probability tokens instead. Both are standard knobs in every 2026 inference API, layered on exactly the inverse-CDF procedure above — see inference and serving.

✎Note

Where does the “random” uuu itself come from? A pseudorandom number generator — a deterministic sequence that passes statistical tests of uniformity, started from a seed. Fix the seed and temperature and the whole pipeline is reproducible in principle, though at scale, floating-point non-determinism across GPU batches means real APIs are shakier about exact reproducibility than this chapter’s math would suggest.

Where this leaves us

  • You can read P(A)=0.3\P(A)=0.3P(A)=0.3 two ways — long-run frequency or degree of belief — and you know a trained model’s outputs are best read as (hopefully calibrated) beliefs.
  • You know the two distributions that carry deep learning: the categorical (a list of kkk probabilities summing to 1 — the object an LLM emits at every step) and the Gaussian (initialization, gradient noise, diffusion), including its 68/95/99.7 geometry.
  • You can compute expectations and variances by hand, and you know the estimator view: a sample mean approximates an expectation with error σ/n\sigma/\sqrt{n}σ/n​ — the statistical fact underneath minibatch training.
  • You can run the product rule forwards (joint = chain of conditionals) and backwards (Bayes’ rule), and you have felt the prior dominate a 99% accurate test.
  • You can derive the chain rule of probability, P(w1..n)=∏tP(wt∣w<t)\P(w_{1..n}) = \prod_t \P(w_t \mid w_{<t})P(w1..n​)=∏t​P(wt​∣w<t​), and you understand why this exact identity — not an approximation — is the definition of language modeling.
  • You can carry out a maximum-likelihood derivation with logs, you know the two reasons logs are mandatory (products→sums; a demonstrated 10−32510^{-325}10−325 underflow), and you know the LLM training loss is MLE in disguise.
  • You can explain softmax from requirements (positive, monotonic, differentiable), justify base eee, prove shift invariance, apply the log-sum-exp trick numerically, and predict what temperature does before touching the slider.
