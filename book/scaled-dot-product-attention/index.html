Scaled Dot-Product Attention

The complete anatomy of softmax(QKᵀ/√dₖ)V — where queries, keys, and values come from, why the √dₖ is there, and a full forward pass you can check with a pencil.

The previous chapter ended with a powerful but slightly ramshackle idea: instead of forcing a whole sentence through one fixed-size bottleneck vector, let the model look back at every position and take a weighted average of what it finds, with weights computed on the fly. That idea rescued machine translation in 2014. But the version we saw there was bolted onto a recurrent network, used a small learned scoring network to compare positions, and still processed text one token at a time.

This chapter is about the cleaned-up, industrial-strength version — the one in “Attention Is All You Need” that every frontier model since 2017 has been built on. It fits in one line:

Attention(Q,K,V)  =  softmax⁡ ⁣(QK⊤dk)V.\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) \;=\; \softmax\!\left(\frac{QK\T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V.Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(dk​​QK⊤​)V.

One line, five symbols, and an enormous amount packed inside. Our plan is to dismantle it completely: where QQQ, KKK, and VVV come from and why there are three of them; what each matrix multiplication means geometrically; why the mysterious dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ is there (with a real derivation, not a hand-wave); how a triangular mask turns this into a text generator that trains in parallel; and what all of it costs. By the end you will compute an entire attention layer by hand for a three-token sentence, and read the equation the way you read a sentence — left to right, knowing what every part does.

Everything here is the single-head story. The next chapter runs many of these in parallel; this one makes sure the thing being parallelized is fully understood.

Queries, keys, and values

Carry over the library analogy from the previous chapter. You walk into a library with a need — “something about 19th-century whaling voyages”. Every book has a catalog card describing what it contains. You compare your need against every card, find the best matches, and walk out with the books — not the cards. Three distinct objects were involved:

  • a query: what I am looking for,
  • a key: what I contain, written in a form that can be matched against,
  • a value: what I hand over if you pick me.

Attention gives every token all three roles at once. Each token broadcasts a query (“here is what I need from the rest of the sentence”), a key (“here is what I can offer, advertised for matching”), and a value (“here is what I will contribute if someone attends to me”). Every token then compares its query against every token’s key, and collects a weighted blend of values.

Where do these three vectors come from? From the token’s embedding, via three learned linear maps. Let XXX be the input matrix — nnn rows, one per token, each row a ddd-dimensional embedding (in real models, the current residual-stream vector — see residual connections). The layer owns three weight matrices, WQW_QWQ​, WKW_KWK​ (each d×dkd \times d_kd×dk​) and WVW_VWV​ (d×dvd \times d_vd×dv​), and computes

Q=XWQ,K=XWK,V=XWV.Q = X W_Q, \qquad K = X W_K, \qquad V = X W_V .Q=XWQ​,K=XWK​,V=XWV​.

Each is an ordinary matrix multiplication: row iii of QQQ is token iii‘s embedding pushed through the map WQW_QWQ​, and likewise for KKK and VVV. The projections typically map down: ddd might be 4,096 while dkd_kdk​ is 64 or 128. Each token’s roles are compressed summaries of its embedding, specialized for their jobs.

Why three separate matrices?

A reasonable objection: tokens already live in an embedding space where the dot product measures similarity. Why not score token iii against token jjj with a raw xi⋅xjx_i \cdot x_jxi​⋅xj​ and skip the projections?

Because asking is not the same as offering. Consider the pronoun “it” in “The trophy didn’t fit in the suitcase because it was too big.” When “it” attends over the sentence, its query should say something like seeking: a singular, inanimate noun that could be my antecedent. But “it” should absolutely not advertise itself as a good antecedent — its key should say I am a pronoun, which is nearly the opposite. What a token wants and what it offers are different things, so they need different maps. With raw xi⋅xjx_i \cdot x_jxi​⋅xj​ similarity, “it” would attend most strongly to… other pronouns, the tokens most similar to itself. That is almost never what the model needs.

The same asymmetry separates keys from values. The catalog card that gets you matched is not the book you take home: a token might be matched because its key says “I am a recent noun”, but what it contributes — its value — might be “the concept trophy, singular, physical object”. Matching information and payload information are different, so WK≠WVW_K \ne W_VWK​=WV​.

💡Intuition

One clarifying way to see what WQW_QWQ​ and WKW_KWK​ buy you: the score between tokens iii and jjj is qi⋅kj=xiWQ(xjWK)⊤=xi (WQWK⊤) xj⊤q_i \cdot k_j = x_i W_Q (x_j W_K)\T = x_i \,(W_Q W_K\T)\, x_j\Tqi​⋅kj​=xi​WQ​(xj​WK​)⊤=xi​(WQ​WK⊤​)xj⊤​. The two projections fuse into a single learned d×dd \times dd×d matrix sandwiched between the raw embeddings. So attention does not measure whether xix_ixi​ and xjx_jxj​ are similar — it measures whether they are related under a learned, asymmetric notion of relevance. “Adjective” can be made close to “the noun after me”; “verb” close to “my subject”. The geometry is bent to order, then the dot product does its usual work. (Interpretability researchers call this fused matrix the QK circuit; we will meet it again in Part VII.)

The shapes, tracked obsessively

Attention bugs are shape bugs, so let us fix the shapes now and re-check them at every step. Throughout: nnn is the number of tokens, ddd the embedding (model) dimension, dkd_kdk​ the query/key dimension, dvd_vdv​ the value dimension.

Two things to notice before we move on. Queries and keys must share a dimension (dkd_kdk​) because they meet in a dot product; values need not (dvd_vdv​ can differ, though in practice it usually equals dkd_kdk​). And the output has one row per token, same as the input — attention is a sequence-to-sequence map, which is what lets us stack it in layers.

The formula, term by term

Attention(Q,K,V)  =  softmax⁡ ⁣(QK⊤dk⏟score every pair)⏟rows become budgets  V⏟blend the values\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) \;=\; \underbrace{\softmax\!\Bigg(\underbrace{\frac{QK\T}{\sqrt{d_k}}}_{\text{score every pair}}\Bigg)}_{\text{rows become budgets}}\;\underbrace{V}_{\text{blend the values}}Attention(Q,K,V)=rows become budgetssoftmax(score every pairdk​​QK⊤​​​)​​blend the valuesV​​

Three operations, reading inside out. We take each in turn.

QKᵀ: score every pair at once

K⊤K\TK⊤ flips KKK from n×dkn \times d_kn×dk​ to dk×nd_k \times ndk​×n, so QK⊤QK\TQK⊤ is (n×dk)(dk×n)=n×n(n \times d_k)(d_k \times n) = n \times n(n×dk​)(dk​×n)=n×n. Recall what matrix multiplication is: entry (i,j)(i, j)(i,j) of QK⊤QK\TQK⊤ is the dot product of row iii of QQQ with row jjj of KKK:

Sij  =  qi⋅kj  =  ∑m=1dkqim kjm.S_{ij} \;=\; q_i \cdot k_j \;=\; \sum_{m=1}^{d_k} q_{im}\, k_{jm}.Sij​=qi​⋅kj​=m=1∑dk​​qim​kjm​.

So this single multiplication computes all n2n^2n2 pairwise scores — every token’s query against every token’s key — in one shot. Row iii of the score matrix answers one question: how relevant is each token in the sequence to what token iii is currently seeking? Large positive score: strong match. Around zero: irrelevant. Negative: actively anti-relevant.

This is the moment the previous chapter’s scoring loop became a matrix multiply — the operation a graphics processing unit (GPU) does fastest of anything it does — and it is also where the infamous quadratic cost enters: an n×nn \times nn×n matrix has n2n^2n2 entries no matter how you compute it. Hold that thought; we will put numbers on it at the end of the chapter.

Row-wise softmax: each token gets an attention budget

Raw scores are unbounded real numbers. To use them as mixing weights we want each row converted into a probability distribution — all entries positive, summing to one. That is exactly what softmax does, and here it is applied to each row independently:

Aij  =  eSij∑j′=1neSij′.A_{ij} \;=\; \frac{e^{S_{ij}}}{\sum_{j'=1}^{n} e^{S_{ij'}}}.Aij​=∑j′=1n​eSij′​eSij​​.

Think of it as a budget: token iii has exactly 1.0 unit of attention to spend, and softmax decides how to split it across the nnn tokens it could look at. Because exponentials exaggerate differences, a score gap of even 2–3 points concentrates most of the budget on the winner — attention tends to be soft but peaked, a few tokens getting most of the mass.

⚠️Careful

The softmax runs across keys (across each row), never across queries. Each row of AAA sums to 1; the columns sum to nothing in particular. A column sum tells you how much total attention a token received from everyone — it can be far more than 1 (popular tokens are attended to by many queries) or nearly 0 (ignored tokens). Mixing up the axis is the most common implementation bug in hand-rolled attention, and it fails silently: the shapes still work, the numbers are still positive, and the model quietly refuses to learn.

Multiplying by V: outputs are convex combinations of values

The last step, O=AVO = AVO=AV, has shape (n×n)(n×dv)=n×dv(n \times n)(n \times d_v) = n \times d_v(n×n)(n×dv​)=n×dv​. Row iii of the output is

oi  =  ∑j=1nAij vj,o_i \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{n} A_{ij}\, v_j ,oi​=j=1∑n​Aij​vj​,

a weighted average of all the value vectors, with weights from row iii of the attention matrix. Since the weights are non-negative and sum to 1, each output is a convex combination of value vectors — geometrically, oio_ioi​ lies inside the shape spanned by the points v1,…,vnv_1, \dots, v_nv1​,…,vn​ (their convex hull). Attention never invents content; it mixes content that the values already carry. If token iii spends 90% of its budget on token jjj, then oio_ioi​ is 90% “whatever vjv_jvj​ says,” and the layer has effectively moved information from position jjj to position iii.

💡Intuition

The whole mechanism in one sentence: each token asks a question (qqq), every token’s advertisement (kkk) is scored against it, the scores become a spending plan (softmax row), and the answer is a blend of what the winners were carrying (vvv). It is a soft, differentiable version of a dictionary lookup — instead of retrieving the single best match, you retrieve a weighted average of everything, weighted by match quality. Softness is not a compromise; it is the feature that makes the whole thing trainable by gradient descent, because a hard argmax lookup has no useful gradient.

🕰History

Nothing in QK⊤QK\TQK⊤ was obvious in advance. Bahdanau et al. (2014) scored query–key pairs with a small learned network (a one-hidden-layer multi-layer perceptron (MLP)), so-called additive attention. Luong et al. (2015) showed a bare dot product worked about as well and cost far less. Vaswani et al. (2017) kept the dot product, added the 1/dk1/\sqrt{d_k}1/dk​​ correction (their justification is a single sentence and a footnote — the derivation below is the missing steps), and made the radical move: use attention within one sequence, as the only mixing mechanism, dropping recurrence entirely. That last move — self-attention as the backbone — is what the next few chapters are about.

Why divide by √dₖ

Here is the part of the formula everyone memorizes and few can derive. The claim: without the scaling, dot products between queries and keys grow with the dimension dkd_kdk​, the softmax saturates, and learning stalls. Let us show it, using the variance rules from Chapter 6.

∑The math

Model the entries of a query qqq and key kkk as independent random variables with mean 000 and variance 111 — a reasonable picture of a well-initialized network (see initialization), where activations are kept roughly zero-mean and unit-variance on purpose. The score is a sum of dkd_kdk​ products:

q⋅k  =  ∑m=1dkqmkm.q \cdot k \;=\; \sum_{m=1}^{d_k} q_m k_m .q⋅k=m=1∑dk​​qm​km​.

Mean. By independence, E[qmkm]=E[qm] E[km]=0⋅0=0\E[q_m k_m] = \E[q_m]\,\E[k_m] = 0 \cdot 0 = 0E[qm​km​]=E[qm​]E[km​]=0⋅0=0, so E[q⋅k]=0\E[q\cdot k] = 0E[q⋅k]=0: scores are centered.

Variance of one term. Using Var⁡(Z)=E[Z2]−(E[Z])2\Var(Z) = \E[Z^2] - (\E[Z])^2Var(Z)=E[Z2]−(E[Z])2 with Z=qmkmZ = q_m k_mZ=qm​km​:

Var⁡(qmkm)  =  E[qm2km2]−0  =  E[qm2] E[km2]  =  1⋅1  =  1,\Var(q_m k_m) \;=\; \E[q_m^2 k_m^2] - 0 \;=\; \E[q_m^2]\,\E[k_m^2] \;=\; 1 \cdot 1 \;=\; 1,Var(qm​km​)=E[qm2​km2​]−0=E[qm2​]E[km2​]=1⋅1=1,

again splitting the expectation by independence.

Variance of the sum. The dkd_kdk​ terms are independent of one another, and variances of independent terms add:

Var⁡(q⋅k)  =  ∑m=1dkVar⁡(qmkm)  =  dk.\Var(q \cdot k) \;=\; \sum_{m=1}^{d_k} \Var(q_m k_m) \;=\; d_k .Var(q⋅k)=m=1∑dk​​Var(qm​km​)=dk​.

So the score has standard deviation dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ — typical scores are of size ±dk\pm\sqrt{d_k}±dk​​, growing with the square root of the head dimension. Dividing by dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ rescales them back to standard deviation 1, independent of dkd_kdk​. That is the entire trick: we divide by the standard deviation, which is why it is dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ and not dkd_kdk​.

The growth matters because softmax feeds scores through exponentials. When typical scores are ±8\pm 8±8 instead of ±1\pm 1±1, the largest exponential dwarfs all others and the output row collapses to nearly one-hot — the distribution’s entropy crashes toward zero. And a saturated softmax is a gradient dead zone: the derivative of a softmax output with respect to its own score is p(1−p)p(1-p)p(1−p), which vanishes as p→1p \to 1p→1 or p→0p \to 0p→0. Every weight upstream of a saturated attention row receives almost no learning signal — the same vanishing-gradient disease we dissected in Chapter 12, contracted at birth.

⚙️In practice

The at-initialization assumptions in the derivation are honest but temporary: training moves the distributions, and in large models the QK⊤QK\TQK⊤ logits can drift upward anyway (“attention-logit growth”), occasionally to the point of numerical trouble in low precision. Modern responses, as of 2026: computing the softmax in float32 even when the model runs in bfloat16 (near-universal); QK-norm — applying root-mean-square normalization (RMSNorm) to queries and keys right before the dot product — used by open models such as OLMo 2 and Gemma 3; and Gemma 2-style logit soft-capping. All are patches on the same wound the dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ treats at birth: keep attention scores in softmax’s responsive range. Head dimensions themselves have stayed stable: GPT-2 used dk=64d_k = 64dk​=64; Llama-class and most 2024–2026 open models use 64 or 128.

Self-attention, cross-attention, and the causal mask

So far, QQQ, KKK, and VVV all came from the same sequence XXX — every token attending over its own sentence. That configuration is self-attention, and it is the backbone of modern language models. But nothing in the formula requires it. Compute queries from one sequence and keys/values from another — Q=XtargetWQQ = X_{\text{target}} W_QQ=Xtarget​WQ​, K,VK, VK,V from XsourceX_{\text{source}}Xsource​ — and you have cross-attention: one sequence interrogating another. That is exactly the translation attention of the previous chapter in modern clothes, and it survives today wherever two different streams meet: encoder–decoder models like T5 and Whisper (decoder text attending to encoded audio), and text conditioning in diffusion image models, where image patches query the prompt’s tokens. The score matrix is then ntarget×nsourcen_{\text{target}} \times n_{\text{source}}ntarget​×nsource​ — rectangular, and nothing breaks.

The causal mask

Self-attention as defined has a problem if you want to generate text. A language model predicts token i+1i{+}1i+1 from tokens 1…i1 \dots i1…i. But our score matrix lets position 2 attend to position 7 — the model would be peeking at the future it is supposed to predict. Training on that is worthless: the network learns to copy the answer sitting in plain view.

The fix is almost embarrassingly direct: before the softmax, overwrite every score where a query would look at a later position — the upper triangle of SSS, all (i,j)(i,j)(i,j) with j>ij > ij>i — with −∞-\infty−∞:

Sijmasked  =  {Sijj≤i−∞j>i.S^{\text{masked}}_{ij} \;=\; \begin{cases} S_{ij} & j \le i \\[2pt] -\infty & j > i . \end{cases}Sijmasked​={Sij​−∞​j≤ij>i.​

Why −∞-\infty−∞, and why before the softmax? Because of how softmax treats it: e−∞=0e^{-\infty} = 0e−∞=0, so a masked position contributes zero to the numerator and zero to the row’s normalizing sum. The remaining, legal positions renormalize among themselves automatically, and each row is still an exact probability distribution — over the past only. Masked positions get weight exactly zero, not approximately zero: no information leaks, and no gradient flows backward through the forbidden connections.

✏️Worked example

Rerun the worked example with a causal mask. Row 1 (“the”) may only see itself: its masked scores are [0,−∞,−∞][0, -\infty, -\infty][0,−∞,−∞], softmax gives [1,0,0][1, 0, 0][1,0,0], and o1=v1=[1,0]o_1 = v_1 = [1, 0]o1​=v1​=[1,0] — with no past, it receives its own value, untouched. Row 2 (“cat”): scaled scores [0.707,0.707,−∞][0.707, 0.707, -\infty][0.707,0.707,−∞] → weights [0.5,0.5,0][0.5, 0.5, 0][0.5,0.5,0] → o2=0.5 [1,0]+0.5 [0,−1]=[0.5,−0.5]o_2 = 0.5\,[1,0] + 0.5\,[0,-1] = [0.5, -0.5]o2​=0.5[1,0]+0.5[0,−1]=[0.5,−0.5]. ⚠️Careful

Two classic masking bugs. First, masking after the softmax by zeroing weights: the surviving weights no longer sum to 1, so outputs shrink for early positions and the “distribution” is broken — if you must zero afterwards, you would have to renormalize each row, at which point you have reimplemented the −∞-\infty−∞ trick with extra steps. Second, using a “large negative number” that is not large enough for your precision: the popular −109-10^9−109 works in float32, but float16 cannot even represent it (its most negative value is about −65,504-65{,}504−65,504), so the mask silently becomes NaN (not-a-number) values or a leak. Real implementations use the dtype’s most negative finite value or a genuine −∞-\infty−∞.

The payoff: training in parallel

Here is the consequence that made transformers take over, and it is worth slowing down for. During training we already know the whole target text. With the causal mask in place, one forward pass over an nnn-token document computes every position’s prediction simultaneously: row 1 of the output predicts token 2 while seeing only token 1; row 2 predicts token 3 while seeing tokens 1–2; and so on — nnn separate prediction problems, each correctly blinded to its own future, all answered by the same three matrix multiplications. You get nnn cross-entropy losses per document for the price of one pass.

Contrast the recurrent networks this replaced: a recurrent neural network (RNN) must process token 1 before token 2, because token 2’s hidden state depends on token 1’s. Its computation is a chain — inherently sequential, the worst possible shape for a GPU that wants to do millions of identical operations at once. Attention has no chain: the mask enforces the ordering of information without imposing an ordering of computation. That single property — total train-time parallelism at the cost of an n×nn \times nn×n matrix — is arguably why the transformer, and not some improved long short-term memory (LSTM) network, absorbed the scaling decade that followed.

✎Note

One more property, easy to miss: attention is permutation-equivariant. Shuffle the input rows and the outputs shuffle identically — nothing in QK⊤QK\TQK⊤ knows position 5 from position 50; the causal mask imposes a direction but the scores themselves are order-blind. A bag of words in, a bag of enriched words out. Since word order carries much of language’s meaning, real models inject positional information separately — the subject of Chapter 26.

Two more lenses: message passing and learned patterns

The equation rewards being seen from more than one angle. Here are two that will pay off later in the book.

💡Intuition

Attention as message passing. Picture the nnn tokens as nodes of a graph. Each attention layer sends a message along every edge — the value vector of the sender — and each node aggregates its incoming messages, weighted by the attention weights on its incoming edges. Two things make this unusual as graph computation: the graph is complete (every token may talk to every other — or, with a causal mask, a complete directed acyclic graph pointing pastward), and the edge weights are not stored anywhere — they are recomputed from content, freshly, at every layer. A convolutional neural network (CNN) passes messages on a fixed local grid; an RNN on a chain; attention on a fully-connected graph with data-dependent, disposable edge weights. When you later meet efficient-attention variants, most of them are exactly this graph with edges deleted.

The second lens: look at the attention matrices of a trained model. The rows of AAA are interpretable objects — for each token, a probability distribution over what it looked at — and real models learn structured, recurring patterns. Some heads attend almost entirely to the previous token (a “previous-token head” — useful for building local phrases). Many heads park most of their budget on the first token or a delimiter when they have nothing better to do — the “attention sink” pattern, a kind of learned no-op, since attending to a near-constant token adds a near-constant vector. Others track syntax: heads where a verb attends to its subject, or a relative pronoun to its antecedent. And some implement genuine algorithms — the induction heads of Olsson et al. (2022), which find the previous occurrence of the current token and attend to what came after it, implementing “this happened before; predict the same continuation”. We will dissect those mechanisms properly in Part VII; for now the point is that the n×nn \times nn×n matrix you computed by hand above is one of the few places a large model’s behavior is directly visible.

✎Note

A caution that Part VII will sharpen: attention weights show where information could flow, not necessarily what the model used, and heads interact in ways a single map cannot show (what gets moved depends on the values — the OV circuit of Elhage et al. (2021) — not the weights alone). Treat attention maps as evidence, not explanation.

What it costs, and the code

The score matrix has n2n^2n2 entries, and there is no getting around computing them in exact attention: both time and memory scale quadratically in sequence length. Concrete numbers make the problem vivid. At a modest n=2,048n = 2{,}048n=2,048 (GPT-3-era context), the matrix has about 4.2 million entries per head — fine. At n=128,000n = 128{,}000n=128,000, a routine context length in 2026, it has 128,0002≈1.64×1010128{,}000^2 \approx 1.64 \times 10^{10}128,0002≈1.64×1010 — about 16 billion scores per head per layer. Stored naively in bfloat16 (2 bytes each) that is roughly 33 GB — for one head of one layer, before you multiply by tens of heads and dozens of layers. Doubling the context quadruples all of it.

Nobody materializes that matrix. FlashAttention computes the exact same mathematics tile by tile, keeping only small blocks in fast on-chip memory and never writing the full n×nn \times nn×n matrix anywhere — same numbers out, memory now linear in nnn (compute is still quadratic). And at generation time, a different observation saves the day: as you emit token n+1n{+}1n+1, the keys and values of tokens 1…n1 \dots n1…n have not changed, so you cache them and compute one new row of attention instead of the whole matrix. That is the KV (key–value) cache, and together with attention variants that share or shrink keys and values it gets a chapter of its own.

Finally, the payoff for all the shape-tracking: the entire chapter is about a dozen lines of code. This runs, and reproduces the worked example’s tables to rounding:

import numpy as np

def attention(Q, K, V, causal=False):
    d_k = Q.shape[-1]
    S = Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(d_k)              # [n, n] pairwise scores
    if causal:
        n = S.shape[0]
        future = np.triu(np.ones((n, n), dtype=bool), k=1)
        S = np.where(future, -np.inf, S)    # mask BEFORE softmax
    S = S - S.max(axis=-1, keepdims=True)   # stability: shift each row
    A = np.exp(S)
    A = A / A.sum(axis=-1, keepdims=True)   # row-wise softmax
    return A @ V                            # [n, d_v] blended values

The one line we have not discussed — subtracting each row’s maximum before exponentiating — changes nothing mathematically (softmax is invariant to adding a constant to a row; numerator and denominator pick up the same factor) but keeps eSe^{S}eS from overflowing when scores are large. Every serious implementation does it.

⚙️In practice

In real 2026 code you will rarely write those lines yourself: PyTorch’s torch.nn.functional.scaled_dot_product_attention (and equivalents in JAX) dispatches to fused FlashAttention-class kernels that handle scaling, masking, numerical stability, and tiling in one call. The math is byte-for-byte the math above; the engineering around memory movement is where the years of work went. When you read a model’s source — Llama’s, DeepSeek’s, nanoGPT’s — you will find this function at the core, wrapped in the multi-head plumbing of the next chapter.

Where this leaves us

  • You can build Q=XWQQ = XW_QQ=XWQ​, K=XWKK = XW_KK=XWK​, V=XWVV = XW_VV=XWV​ and say why the three maps must be separate: asking, advertising, and delivering are different jobs (“it” queries for an antecedent without offering itself as one).
  • You can read softmax⁡(QK⊤/dk) V\softmax(QK\T/\sqrt{d_k})\,Vsoftmax(QK⊤/dk​​)V term by term: all n2n^2n2 pairwise scores in one multiplication, each row softmaxed into an attention budget summing to 1, each output a convex blend of value vectors.
  • You can derive the dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​: independent unit-variance coordinates make Var⁡(q⋅k)=dk\Var(q \cdot k) = d_kVar(q⋅k)=dk​, so scores grow like dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ and would saturate the softmax into a vanishing-gradient regime (dk=64d_k = 64dk​=64 → logits 8× too large) — dividing by the standard deviation fixes the scale.
  • You can run the whole layer by hand — projections, nine dot products, scaling, three softmaxes, three blends — and verify it against twelve lines of NumPy.
  • You know the causal mask’s mechanics (−∞-\infty−∞ above the diagonal, applied before softmax, giving exact zeros after) and its payoff: nnn correctly blinded predictions from one parallel forward pass — the property that let transformers out-train RNNs.
  • You can view the same equation as message passing on a complete graph with content-computed edge weights, and you have seen the kinds of patterns (previous-token, syntactic, induction) that trained heads learn.
  • You know the price — O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2), about 16 billion scores per head at 128k context — and the names of the escape hatches (FlashAttention, the KV cache) waiting in Chapter 28.

One head, though, can only spend one attention budget — it cannot look at the subject and the previous token and the matching bracket at once. The fix is delightfully unsubtle: run many small heads in parallel and let them specialize. Next: Multi-Head Attention.
