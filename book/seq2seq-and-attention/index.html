From seq2seq to Attention

How a translation bottleneck in 2014 led to attention — a differentiable lookup that became the core operation of every modern language model.

Every architecture in the rest of this book — GPT-5-class models, Claude, Llama, DeepSeek — is built around one operation, and that operation was invented to patch a specific, measurable engineering failure in 2014 machine translation systems. This chapter tells that story properly, because the fastest way to understand attention is to watch it being invented: to feel the problem it solved, see the first version of the math, and then watch three years of researchers slowly realize that the patch mattered more than the thing it was patching.

The failure was this: the best neural translation systems of 2014 read an entire sentence, squeezed everything they had learned about it into one fixed-size vector, and then generated the translation from that vector alone. For short sentences this worked startlingly well. For long ones it fell apart, and it fell apart in exactly the way you would predict: the model ran out of room. The fix — proposed by an intern, in a paper that never uses the word “attention” in its title — was to let the model look back at the whole input, at every step of writing the output, and take a weighted average of what it finds there, with the weights learned end to end.

That weighted lookup turned out to be one of the most consequential ideas in the history of computing. By 2017, researchers at Google asked what would happen if you deleted everything except the lookup — no recurrence, no convolutions, attention all the way down — and the transformer was born. This chapter takes you from the bottleneck to that moment. The next chapter, scaled dot-product attention, then rebuilds the modern version of the operation with full rigor. By the end of this one, you will have already derived its soul.

Two RNNs and a bottleneck

Recall the setup from CNNs and RNNs. A recurrent neural network (RNN) reads a sequence one token at a time, maintaining a hidden state vector that it updates at each step — a running summary of everything read so far. In 2014, two groups (Sutskever, Vinyals & Le at Google, and Cho et al. in Yoshua Bengio’s lab, whose paper also introduced the gated recurrent unit (GRU)) independently proposed the sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) recipe for translation:

  1. An encoder RNN reads the source sentence token by token — “the”, “cat”, “sleeps” — updating its hidden state at each step.
  2. When the sentence ends, the encoder’s final hidden state is taken as a summary of the entire sentence: one vector, often called the context or thought vector.
  3. A decoder RNN is initialized from that vector and generates the target sentence one token at a time — “le”, “chat”, “dort” — each step conditioned on the summary vector and on its own previous outputs, exactly the autoregressive factorization you met in What a Language Model Is.

This was a radical design at the time: a single neural network, trained end to end by gradient descent on pairs of sentences, with no hand-built dictionaries, no explicit grammar, no separate alignment step. And it worked — Sutskever’s system, a stack of four long short-term memory (LSTM) layers with 1,000 units each, came close to the heavily engineered statistical systems that had dominated translation for a decade.

But look at step 2 again. The encoder’s final hidden state has a fixed size — say 1,000 numbers — chosen before training and identical for every input. A six-word sentence gets 1,000 numbers of summary. A sixty-word sentence about nested legal obligations, with three subclauses and a dangling pronoun, gets the same 1,000 numbers. Everything the decoder will ever know about the source must fit through this aperture. That is the bottleneck.

💡Intuition

The bottleneck is a closed-book exam. The encoder reads the sentence, then the book is taken away, and the decoder must write the translation entirely from memory — from whatever it managed to cram into one fixed-size vector. Short sentence, no problem. Long sentence, and by the time the decoder is writing word 40 it has half-forgotten word 3. Attention, when it arrives, will turn this into an open-book exam: the source stays on the desk, and the decoder can look up exactly the part it needs, at every step.

The failure was not hypothetical, and it was measured before it was fixed. Cho et al. (2014) plotted translation quality — measured in BLEU (bilingual evaluation understudy, an n-gram overlap score where higher is better) — against source sentence length, and found that the fixed-vector encoder–decoder degraded sharply once sentences passed roughly 20 words, while the older statistical systems did not. The information argument is straightforward: as covered in Information Theory, a fixed-size representation has fixed capacity, but the information content of a sentence grows with its length. Sooner or later, lossy compression becomes too lossy.

There were also symptoms in the tricks people used. Sutskever’s team found that reversing the source sentence — feeding “sleeps cat the” instead of “the cat sleeps” — improved BLEU substantially. Why would that help? Because it puts the beginning of the source right next to the beginning of the target, shortening the path the earliest (and often most important) words must survive inside the recurrent state. It is a clever hack, and like most clever hacks it is a confession: the architecture was losing information over distance, and everyone knew it. You have seen the underlying disease before, in Initialization and Gradient Pathologies — gradients and information both struggle to propagate through long chains of repeated transformations.

Bahdanau’s fix: look back at everything

The fix appeared in September 2014: “Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate” by Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho, and Yoshua Bengio. The proposal has three moves, each of which sounds almost too reasonable to be a breakthrough.

Move 1: keep everything. Do not throw away the encoder’s intermediate hidden states. If the source has TTT tokens, keep all TTT vectors h1,h2,…,hTh_1, h_2, \dots, h_Th1​,h2​,…,hT​ — one per source position, each a representation of that token in context. (Bahdanau’s encoder was bidirectional — one RNN reading left-to-right, another right-to-left, with the two states concatenated — so each hih_ihi​ summarizes the whole sentence from the perspective of position iii. The paper calls these vectors annotations.)

Move 2: score relevance. At each decoder step ttt, before producing the next word, compare the decoder’s current state st−1s_{t-1}st−1​ against every hih_ihi​ and produce a scalar score et,ie_{t,i}et,i​: “how relevant is source position iii to what I am about to write?” The scoring function is a tiny neural network, learned along with everything else — nobody tells the model what “relevant” means.

Move 3: take a weighted average. Push the scores through a softmax so they become positive weights summing to 1, then use them to blend the annotations into a single context vector ctc_tct​ — a custom summary of the source, rebuilt from scratch for this one decoder step. Feed ctc_tct​ into the decoder update alongside the previous output token.

That is the whole mechanism. The decoder still generates left to right, still maintains a recurrent state — but its window onto the source is no longer one frozen vector. It is a fresh, targeted lookup at every step: writing the verb? Reweight toward the source verb. Writing an adjective? Reweight toward the noun phrase it modifies.

The results vindicated the design immediately. Bahdanau’s attention model (named RNNsearch in the paper) matched the fixed-vector baseline on short sentences and demolished it on long ones — the quality-versus-length curve, which had collapsed after 20 words, went nearly flat out to 60 words. Neural translation became competitive with the best hand-engineered phrase-based systems, and within two years (with attention as a standard ingredient) Google’s production translation system was neural end to end.

🕰History

Dzmitry Bahdanau was a masters student visiting from Jacobs University Bremen, working as an intern in Yoshua Bengio’s Montreal lab with Kyunghyun Cho, when he proposed the mechanism. The paper never calls it “attention” in the title — the framing is “jointly learning to align and translate”, because the natural reference point in 2014 was the explicit word-alignment models of statistical machine translation. The word “attention” appears in the text (“the decoder decides parts of the source sentence to pay attention to”) and stuck. It is one of several near-simultaneous inventions of learned soft addressing: the Neural Turing Machine (Graves et al., October 2014) and Memory Networks (Weston et al., October 2014) both built differentiable memory reads around the same softmax-weighted-average idea, aimed at algorithms and question answering rather than translation.

Soft alignment: watching the model translate

The scores are learned with no alignment supervision, so a natural question is whether they learn anything interpretable. Bahdanau’s Figure 3 answered with one of the most reproduced images in deep learning: plot αt,i\alpha_{t,i}αt,i​ as a grid — target words down the side, source words across the top, cell darkness showing weight — and you can watch the model align the two languages.

Three things in this picture are worth staring at. First, most of the mass hugs the diagonal — as it should, since English and French mostly share word order. Second, where the languages disagree on order, the weights break off the diagonal and follow the meaning: French puts adjectives after the noun, so “European Economic Area” becomes “zone économique européenne” and the attention forms a reversed block. Third, the alignments are soft: the French passé composé “a été signée” needs three output words for the two English words “was signed”, and the corresponding rows spread their weight across both source positions rather than picking one.

Statistical machine translation had spent 20 years building explicit alignment models to capture exactly these patterns, trained with their own objectives and algorithms. Here they emerged as a side effect of asking a differentiable network to translate well. This was many researchers’ first visceral demonstration that “let the gradient figure it out” could replace an entire subfield’s worth of hand engineering.

⚠️Careful

Do not over-read attention maps. The heatmap shows where the model put weight, and for translation those weights often coincide with human intuitions about alignment — but weights are not guaranteed explanations of why the model produced an output. Each hih_ihi​ is already a mixture of information from the whole sentence (the encoder is bidirectional), so weight on position iii is not purely weight on word iii. Whether attention weights constitute explanations became a genuine research dispute later — see “Attention is not Explanation” (Jain & Wallace, 2019) and the rebuttal “Attention is not not Explanation” (Wiegreffe & Pinter, 2019). The field’s mature answer, which Part VII develops, is that raw attention maps are a clue, not a verdict — causal tests are needed before “the model attended to X” becomes “the model used X”.

Luong’s simplification: scoring with a dot product

Bahdanau’s scoring function — project both vectors, add, tanh⁡\tanhtanh, project to a scalar — works, but it is a little machine with three weight tensors of its own. A year later, Luong, Pham & Manning (2015) at Stanford asked how much of that machinery is necessary, and tried a menu of scoring functions:

et,i  =  {st⊤hidotst⊤ W higeneralva⊤tanh⁡(Wa[st;hi])concat (≈ Bahdanau)e_{t,i} \;=\; \begin{cases} s_t\T h_i & \text{dot} \\[4pt] s_t\T\, W\, h_i & \text{general} \\[4pt] v_a\T \tanh\big(W_a [s_t; h_i]\big) & \text{concat (≈ Bahdanau)} \end{cases}et,i​=⎩⎨⎧​st⊤​hi​st⊤​Whi​va⊤​tanh(Wa​[st​;hi​])​dotgeneralconcat (≈ Bahdanau)​

The first option should look familiar from Vectors and Spaces: it is a plain dot product — no learned parameters in the score at all. Relevance is measured as geometric similarity between the decoder state and the annotation, in whatever representation space the rest of the network has learned. The “general” form inserts one learned matrix WWW, which lets the model ask about a transformed version of the annotation — a freedom that will bloom into queries and keys in the next chapter.

Luong found the simple forms worked as well as the elaborate one, and the paper cleaned up the surrounding architecture too (scoring against the current state sts_tst​ rather than the previous one, a simpler unidirectional stacked LSTM encoder). The lasting lesson: you do not need a neural network to compute relevance — a dot product in a learned space is enough. Modern attention is dot-product attention; Bahdanau’s additive form survives mostly in textbooks and in a scaling footnote you will meet in the next chapter.

What matters is the shared skeleton: score, softmax, weighted sum.

Attention is a differentiable dictionary

Step back from translation. Strip away the RNNs, the French, the BLEU scores, and look at the bare operation Bahdanau introduced, because it has a clean computational identity: attention is a lookup in a dictionary — done softly, so that it is differentiable.

A dictionary (a hash map, a Python dict) stores key–value pairs. You bring a query, it is compared against the keys, and the value whose key matches is returned. That comparison is hard: exact match or nothing, one value returned, all others ignored. Attention performs the same ritual with every hard edge rounded off:

  • The query is what you are looking for — in Bahdanau’s model, the decoder state st−1s_{t-1}st−1​: “I am about to write the next French word; what do I need?”
  • The keys are what each stored item advertises about itself — here, the encoder annotations hih_ihi​, matched against the query by the score function.
  • The values are what each item hands over when selected — here, also the annotations hih_ihi​.
  • The match is by similarity, not equality — every key matches a little, to the degree the score says.
  • And the return is a blend, not a selection — the softmax-weighted average of all values, weighted by how well their keys matched.

In Bahdanau’s version, keys and values are the same vectors, and the query lives in a different network (the decoder) from the keys (the encoder). The transformer will cut these ties: it computes queries, keys, and values as three separate learned projections of the same token representations, so that “what I seek”, “what I advertise”, and “what I deliver” can each be tuned independently. Hold onto this query–key–value (QKV) vocabulary — it is the language of the next five chapters, and it was born here, as a dictionary metaphor for a translation hack.

💡Intuition

A hash map asks “which entry is this?” and returns exactly one value. Attention asks “how much is each entry like this?” and returns a weighted cocktail of all of them. Blurring the lookup costs precision but buys the one property deep learning cannot live without: the output now changes smoothly as the query changes, so the whole retrieval process has gradients and can be learned. Attention is what a dictionary looks like after calculus gets hold of it.

Why soft beats hard

The alternative — hard attention — would pick one position: take the arg max⁡\argmaxargmax of the scores, or sample a position from the softmax distribution, and return that single hih_ihi​. It sounds cleaner and more efficient, and people tried it seriously: Xu et al. (2015) built both soft and hard attention for image captioning (“Show, Attend and Tell”) and their comparison is the classic reference.

The problem is training. arg max⁡\argmaxargmax is a step function: nudge the scores a little and the selected index either does not change at all (gradient zero — no learning signal) or jumps discontinuously (gradient undefined). As established in Derivatives and Gradients, backpropagation needs the output to respond smoothly to the parameters. Hard selection breaks the chain, and training it requires the high-variance score-function estimators from reinforcement learning (Xu et al. used exactly that), which are far noisier than a clean gradient.

∑The math

The second term is the interesting one: it teaches the scoring pathway. ⚠️Careful

“Soft” does not mean “probabilistic.” The weights αi\alpha_iαi​ come out of a softmax, so they are positive and sum to 1 and look like probabilities — but in soft attention nothing is sampled. The model does not choose position 2 with probability 0.72; it deterministically mixes 72% of h2h_2h2​ into a blend. Conflating the two makes hard attention (which really does select) and soft attention (which never selects anything) sound like the same mechanism at different temperatures, and hides the actual difference: one is differentiable, the other is not.

Attention escapes translation, 2015–2017

Good abstractions travel light, and “score, softmax, weighted sum” needs nothing translation-specific. Between 2015 and 2017 it colonized one task after another. Xu et al. attached a caption-writing decoder to a convolutional image encoder, attending over image regions instead of source words — writing “a bird flying over water,” with the weights visibly moving across the bird and then the water. Others applied attention over supporting facts for question answering (the end-to-end memory networks line), over documents for summarization, over speech frames for recognition.

Then came a subtler shift. In all the above, a decoder attends over an encoder’s output — two different networks, two different sequences. In 2016, papers began letting a sequence attend over itself. Cheng, Dong & Lapata (2016) had a reader attend over its own earlier states (“intra-attention”) while reading; Parikh et al. (2016) showed a recurrence-free model where two sentences attend to each other could match far heavier LSTMs on entailment; Lin et al. (2017) built sentence embeddings from attention over a sentence’s own tokens. This is self-attention: each position’s query is scored against every other position’s key in the same sequence, so each token rebuilds its representation as a weighted blend of the tokens around it. “Which earlier words matter for understanding this one?” turns out to be most of what sentence understanding is.

Meanwhile, in the systems that mattered commercially, a quiet inversion was underway. Google’s 2016 production system (Google Neural Machine Translation, GNMT) was an eight-layer LSTM encoder–decoder with attention in the middle — attention as accessory. But researchers kept noticing that the accessory was doing much of the work, while the recurrence was the reason training was slow: an RNN must process tokens in order, each step waiting for the last, which is exactly the access pattern that graphics processing units (GPUs) — machines built to do enormous batched matrix multiplications in parallel — are worst at. Attention has no such dependency: every score in step 1 and every product in step 3 can be computed simultaneously. The question was becoming audible: if attention connects everything to everything in one parallel step, what is the recurrence still for?

2017: subtraction as invention

The answer arrived in June 2017 from eight researchers at Google: “Attention Is All You Need” (Vaswani et al.), the title a thesis statement. The transformer is best understood not as an invention from nothing but as a subtraction with consequences — and the consequences each got a repair, which is where the rest of the architecture comes from.

What was removed: recurrence (and convolutions — the paper positions itself against convolutional sequence models too). No hidden state carried step to step, no sequential dependency in the layer. Processing token 50,000 no longer requires having finished token 49,999.

What was kept: attention — promoted from accessory to the entire mechanism of interaction between positions, in three roles that are all the same operation with different query and key sources: self-attention in the encoder (source tokens attend to source tokens), masked self-attention in the decoder (each target token attends only to earlier target tokens, preserving the autoregressive rule from Chapter 18), and encoder–decoder attention (target attends to source — Bahdanau’s original, still recognizable in the middle of the stack).

What was added — the repairs:

  • Positional encodings. Remove recurrence and you remove the model’s only sense of word order: a weighted average over a set of vectors returns the same answer under any shuffling of that set. Order must be injected back, by adding position-dependent patterns to the token embeddings — a fix important enough to get its own chapter.
  • Multi-head attention. One softmax produces one blend — one retrieval per step. The transformer runs many attentions in parallel (“heads”) in different learned subspaces, so one head can track syntax while another tracks coreference. Chapter 24 is devoted to this.
  • Depth without recurrence. Each attention layer is paired with a position-wise feed-forward network (an MLP applied to each position independently), and the whole stack is held together by residual connections and layer normalization — the stability toolkit from Part II, without which a 6-layer (let alone 100-layer) attention stack would not train.

Why did this happen in 2017 and not 2015? Because the subtraction’s payoff was hardware-shaped, and the hardware and data had arrived. An RNN’s training time is a chain of TTT dependent steps; the transformer’s per-layer work is a handful of large matrix multiplications with constant sequential depth — precisely what a GPU wants. Equally important, any two positions are now one attention step apart, versus up to TTT recurrent steps: the long-range gradient pathologies of Chapter 12 shrink from “path length TTT” problems to “path length 1” problems. Bigger datasets (the English–French corpus from WMT, the Workshop on Statistical Machine Translation benchmark, had 36 million sentence pairs) meant the winner would be whichever architecture could eat the most data per unit time. The transformer was that architecture by a wide margin.

The paper’s actual results, in two sentences: on WMT 2014 English→German the big transformer scored 28.4 BLEU, more than 2 BLEU above the previous best including ensembles, and on English→French 41.8 BLEU, a new single-model state of the art. It reached those numbers after 3.5 days on eight P100 GPUs — a small fraction of the training cost of the systems it beat (and the base model needed only about twelve hours).

🕰History

Within the paper, the authors could not fully justify why self-attention worked so well — Section 4 gives the honest engineering arguments (per-layer computation, parallelism, path length between positions) rather than grand claims. Nobody in 2017, the authors included, predicted the full trajectory: that scaling this architecture on internet text would dominate the following decade. The paper was aimed at translation benchmarks. The generality was discovered afterwards, empirically, by GPT, BERT, and everything since — a recurring pattern in this field: the architecture arrived years before the understanding of what it could do.

⚙️In practice

What survived into 2026, from each era of this story: from Bahdanau, the core loop — learned relevance scores, softmax, weighted sum — is unchanged in every frontier model; additive scoring itself is retired. From Luong, dot-product scoring is the scoring function (scaled by dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ — next chapter). From Vaswani, nearly everything: GPT-5-class models, Claude, Gemini, Llama 4, and DeepSeek-V3 are all transformers, though most frontier LLMs dropped the encoder half and kept only the masked-self-attention decoder (Chapter 27 covers why). Bahdanau’s original decoder-attends-to-encoder configuration survives too, renamed cross-attention: it is how multimodal models attend from text to image features and how diffusion models condition images on prompts. What changed most is scale and plumbing: hundreds of layers and heads, attention over hundreds of thousands of tokens, and a serving-cost structure dominated by attention’s memory traffic — the subject of the KV cache chapter.

The road through Part IV

You now hold the two ideas this entire part elaborates: attention is differentiable retrieval (query against keys, blend the values), and the transformer is attention made load-bearing (subtract recurrence, repair the consequences). Here is how the elaboration unfolds:

Scaled dot-product attention makes this chapter’s mechanism exact and modern — the QKV projections as matrices, the dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ scaling and why softmax saturates without it, causal masking, and the full worked matrix arithmetic. Multi-head attention splits the operation into parallel subspace-sized lookups. The Transformer, Piece by Piece assembles the complete block — attention, MLP, residuals, normalization — and traces one token’s path through it. Positional Information and RoPE solves order, from the 2017 sinusoids to the rotary encodings modern models use. Then the consequences: architecture families (why decoder-only won), the KV cache (why attention dominates serving costs and what FlashAttention and friends do about it), mixture-of-experts (scaling the MLPs without scaling the compute), and state-space models — the current attempt to answer a question this chapter should leave you primed to ask: recurrence was removed for training-parallelism reasons in 2017; with different hardware tricks, could something recurrence-like come back?

Where this leaves us

  • You can state the seq2seq bottleneck precisely: a fixed-size encoder vector has fixed capacity, so translation quality collapses with sentence length — and you know the 2014 evidence (Cho’s length curves, Sutskever’s sentence-reversal trick) that diagnosed it.
  • You can compute Bahdanau attention by hand: additive scores, softmax to weights, weighted sum of annotations to a per-step context vector — and you have done it, with real numbers, twice.
  • You can read an alignment heatmap and explain why soft alignments emerge with no alignment supervision — and why attention maps are clues, not explanations.
  • You know how dot-product scoring entered (Luong, 2015) and why the simplest scorer won.
  • You can explain attention as a differentiable dictionary — query, keys, values, similarity instead of equality, blending instead of selection — and why soft selection trains (gradients reach every position and every score) where hard selection starves.
  • You can describe the transformer as a subtraction: recurrence removed, attention kept in three roles, positions and multi-head and the residual stack added as repairs — and you know the timing was about GPUs, path lengths, and data appetite.
