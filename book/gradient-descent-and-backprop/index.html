Gradient Descent and Backpropagation

The learning loop itself: how a loss is turned into parameter updates, why minibatches work, and a full backward pass computed by hand through a tiny network.

In this chapter

  2. The learning rate: too small, too big, and the cliff in between
  3. Batch, stochastic, minibatch
  4. Backpropagation, by hand, slowly
  5. Now do it 70 billion times in parallel: autodiff
  6. Where this leaves us

The last two chapters built a machine and a scorekeeper. A multi-layer perceptron turns inputs into predictions, and a loss function turns predictions into a single number measuring how wrong they are. What we still lack is the part that makes it learning rather than a fixed formula: a procedure that changes the parameters so the loss goes down.

This chapter is that procedure, and it comes in two pieces that are often blurred together but are separate ideas. Gradient descent is the outer loop: nudge every parameter a small step in the direction that reduces the loss, then do it again, millions of times. Backpropagation is the inner subroutine: an efficient way to compute, for every one of the parameters, which direction that is — the gradient — at a cost of roughly one extra pass through the network, no matter how many parameters there are. Gradient descent is fifty lines of ideas; backpropagation is the reason those ideas are affordable for a model with 70 billion knobs.

Nothing in this chapter is exotic. It is the derivatives and chain rule machinery from Part I, finally doing the job we promised it would do. If you follow the worked example in the middle of this chapter with a pencil — and it is small enough that you can — you will have personally executed the same algorithm that trains GPT-5-class models, minus about 102210^{22}1022 repetitions.

The downhill idea

Collect every parameter of the model — every weight and bias, from Chapter 8 — into one enormous vector θ\thetaθ (theta). For a modern language model, θ\thetaθ has tens of billions of entries, but conceptually it is one point in one very high-dimensional space. The loss L(θ)L(\theta)L(θ), averaged over training data, assigns a number to every such point. That defines a loss landscape: a surface over parameter space whose height at θ\thetaθ is how badly the model with those parameters performs.

Training is now a geography problem: we are standing somewhere on this surface (wherever initialization dropped us) and we want to get lower. We cannot see the whole landscape — evaluating LLL even once means running the entire dataset through the model — but we can sense the local slope under our feet. Chapter 4 gave us exactly the right instrument: the gradient ∇L(θ)\nabla L(\theta)∇L(θ) is the vector that points in the direction of steepest increase of the loss, and its negation points down the steepest descent. So: take a small step downhill. Repeat.

∑The math

Gradient descent is the update rule

θ  ←  θ  −  η ∇L(θ),\theta \;\leftarrow\; \theta \;-\; \eta \, \nabla L(\theta),θ←θ−η∇L(θ),

where θ\thetaθ is the full parameter vector, ∇L(θ)\nabla L(\theta)∇L(θ) is the gradient of the loss with respect to every parameter (a vector the same size as θ\thetaθ: entry iii is ∂L/∂θi\partial L / \partial \theta_i∂L/∂θi​), and η\etaη (eta) is the learning rate — a small positive number controlling the size of the step. The rule is applied simultaneously to all parameters: each θi\theta_iθi​ moves opposite to its own partial derivative, scaled by the same η\etaη.

Why is downhill the locally best move? The first-order Taylor expansion from Chapter 4 says that for a small step δ\deltaδ,

L(θ+δ)  ≈  L(θ)+∇L(θ)⊤δ.L(\theta + \delta) \;\approx\; L(\theta) + \nabla L(\theta)\T \delta .L(θ+δ)≈L(θ)+∇L(θ)⊤δ.

Among all steps δ\deltaδ of a fixed small length, the dot product ∇L⊤δ\nabla L\T \delta∇L⊤δ is most negative when δ\deltaδ points exactly opposite the gradient — that is the dot-product geometry of Chapter 1. Setting δ=−η∇L\delta = -\eta \nabla Lδ=−η∇L gives a predicted decrease of −η∥∇L∥2-\eta \|\nabla L\|^2−η∥∇L∥2, which is strictly negative whenever the gradient is nonzero. Small enough steps downhill always help.

💡Intuition

Picture standing on a foggy mountainside at night with a flashlight that only illuminates the ground at your feet. You cannot see the valley. You can see which way the ground tilts right here. Gradient descent is the strategy of repeatedly stepping in the locally steepest downhill direction and trusting that enough local steps add up to global progress. The fog matters: nothing in the algorithm knows where the minimum is, whether a better valley exists one ridge over, or how far there is to go. Everything a trillion-dollar training run knows about where it is heading is contained in that circle of light.

Two honest caveats before we go further. First, nothing guarantees this finds the global minimum — the loss landscape of a deep network is wildly non-convex, full of valleys, ridges, and saddle points. The empirical miracle of deep learning, which we take up in the regularization chapter, is that for large networks the local minima gradient descent finds are almost always good enough. Second, the rule above says nothing about how to get ∇L\nabla L∇L. For now, assume an oracle hands it to us; the second half of this chapter builds the oracle.

The learning rate: too small, too big, and the cliff in between

The single number η\etaη deserves its own section, because it is the hyperparameter that most often decides whether training works at all. The Taylor argument above only holds for small steps — the landscape is only approximately linear near your feet — so η\etaη is a bet on how far the local slope can be trusted. Too small and you waste compute crawling; too large and each step overshoots the valley floor, landing on the far wall higher than you started, and the loss explodes.

We can see all three regimes exactly, with no simulation, on the simplest possible landscape.

✏️Worked example

Take the one-parameter loss f(x)=x2f(x) = x^2f(x)=x2, whose gradient is f′(x)=2xf'(x) = 2xf′(x)=2x and whose minimum is at x=0x = 0x=0. Gradient descent reads

x  ←  x−η⋅2x  =  (1−2η) x.x \;\leftarrow\; x - \eta \cdot 2x \;=\; (1 - 2\eta)\, x .x←x−η⋅2x=(1−2η)x.

Every step multiplies xxx by the constant factor (1−2η)(1 - 2\eta)(1−2η) — so the whole future of training is visible in that one factor. Starting from x0=1x_0 = 1x0​=1:

  • η=0.05\eta = 0.05η=0.05 — factor 0.90.90.9. Iterates: 1→0.9→0.81→0.729→…1 \to 0.9 \to 0.81 \to 0.729 \to \dots1→0.9→0.81→0.729→… Converging, but after 50 steps we are still at 0.950≈0.0050.9^{50} \approx 0.0050.950≈0.005. Slow, safe, expensive.
  • η=0.3\eta = 0.3η=0.3 — factor 0.40.40.4. Iterates: 1→0.4→0.16→0.064→0.0261 \to 0.4 \to 0.16 \to 0.064 \to 0.0261→0.4→0.16→0.064→0.026. Four steps do what the small rate needed thirty-five for.
  • η=0.75\eta = 0.75η=0.75 — factor −0.5-0.5−0.5. Iterates: 1→−0.5→0.25→−0.1251 \to -0.5 \to 0.25 \to -0.1251→−0.5→0.25→−0.125. It overshoots past the minimum every step, oscillating side to side — yet the magnitude still halves each time, so it converges. Twitchy but fine.
  • η=1.1\eta = 1.1η=1.1 — factor −1.2-1.2−1.2. Iterates: 1→−1.2→1.44→−1.728→2.0741 \to -1.2 \to 1.44 \to -1.728 \to 2.0741→−1.2→1.44→−1.728→2.074. Each overshoot lands higher than the last. The loss diverges to infinity.

The boundary is exactly where ∣1−2η∣=1|1 - 2\eta| = 1∣1−2η∣=1, i.e. η=1\eta = 1η=1: below it any rate converges, above it every rate diverges. Divergence is not a gradual degradation — it is a cliff.

The stability threshold is not arbitrary — it is curvature, the second-derivative idea from Chapter 5. For the loss f(x)=λ2x2f(x) = \tfrac{\lambda}{2} x^2f(x)=2λ​x2, which has second derivative f′′=λf'' = \lambdaf′′=λ, the update factor is (1−ηλ)(1 - \eta\lambda)(1−ηλ) and stability requires η<2/λ\eta < 2/\lambdaη<2/λ: the sharper the curvature, the smaller the largest safe step. In many dimensions the same analysis applies direction by direction, and the binding constraint comes from the most curved direction — which is why a single global learning rate is a compromise, and why the entire optimizers chapter is about doing better than one global η\etaη (per-parameter step sizes in Adam, warmup and decay schedules, and the rest of the modern toolkit).

⚠️Careful

The most common failure mode in practice is not the slow crawl — it is quiet divergence. With too large a learning rate, the loss may fall for a while, then produce a spike, then NaNs (not-a-number values). When you see a loss curve blow up, the learning rate (or a bad interaction between learning rate and initialization) should be your first suspect, not a bug in your data pipeline. Frontier training runs treat loss spikes as routine operational events with runbooks — more on that in the training run.

Batch, stochastic, minibatch

So far we wrote ∇L(θ)\nabla L(\theta)∇L(θ) as if the loss were one function. But recall from Chapter 9 that the training loss is an average over examples: for a dataset of NNN pairs (xi,yi)(x_i, y_i)(xi​,yi​) and a per-example loss ℓ\ellℓ,

L(θ)  =  1N∑i=1Nℓ(f(xi;θ), yi).L(\theta) \;=\; \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \ell\big(f(x_i; \theta),\, y_i\big).L(θ)=N1​i=1∑N​ℓ(f(xi​;θ),yi​).

Because differentiation is linear, the gradient of the average is the average of the per-example gradients. That gives us a choice about how many examples to look at before taking a step, and the three canonical answers have names worth defining precisely, because the terminology is used loosely everywhere:

  • Batch (full-batch) gradient descent computes the gradient over all NNN examples before every single parameter update. The gradient is exact, the update is deterministic — and each step costs a full pass over the dataset. For NNN in the trillions of tokens, one step would cost an entire epoch. Nobody trains large models this way.
  • Stochastic gradient descent (SGD), in its original strict sense, uses one example per step: θ←θ−η∇θℓ(f(xi;θ),yi)\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \nabla_\theta \ell(f(x_i;\theta), y_i)θ←θ−η∇θ​ℓ(f(xi​;θ),yi​) for a randomly chosen iii. Each step is cheap but the gradient estimate is extremely noisy, and — worse for modern hardware — it wastes the massive parallelism of a graphics processing unit (GPU), which would rather process thousands of examples at once than one.
  • Minibatch gradient descent is the compromise everyone uses: sample a batch B\mathcal{B}B of BBB examples (the batch size), average their per-example gradients, and step on that average. Confusingly, in 2026 almost everyone calls this “SGD” too; when a paper says SGD, it nearly always means minibatch.
∑The math

The minibatch gradient is the average of the per-example gradients over the BBB examples in the sampled batch B\mathcal{B}B:

g^  =  ∇LB(θ)  =  1B∑i∈B∇θ ℓ(f(xi;θ), yi),θ  ←  θ−η g^.\hat{g} \;=\; \nabla L_{\mathcal{B}}(\theta) \;=\; \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} \nabla_\theta\, \ell\big(f(x_i; \theta),\, y_i\big), \qquad \theta \;\leftarrow\; \theta - \eta\, \hat{g}.g^​=∇LB​(θ)=B1​i∈B∑​∇θ​ℓ(f(xi​;θ),yi​),θ←θ−ηg^​.

Each term ∇θℓ(f(xi;θ),yi)\nabla_\theta \ell(f(x_i;\theta), y_i)∇θ​ℓ(f(xi​;θ),yi​) is a full-size gradient vector — one partial derivative per parameter, computed from example iii alone — and g^\hat gg^​ averages these BBB vectors entrywise. Two properties make this work. First, if the batch is sampled uniformly, g^\hat gg^​ is an unbiased estimate of the true gradient: E[g^]=∇L(θ)\E[\hat g] = \nabla L(\theta)E[g^​]=∇L(θ) (Chapter 6’s linearity of expectation). On average, the minibatch points exactly downhill. Second, averaging BBB independent estimates shrinks the noise: the variance of g^\hat gg^​ falls like 1/B1/B1/B, so its standard deviation falls like 1/B1/\sqrt{B}1/B​. Bigger batches buy you a cleaner compass — but with diminishing returns: quadrupling the batch only halves the noise.

Why tolerate noise at all, if the full-batch gradient is exact? Three reasons, in increasing order of subtlety. First, economics: with a dataset of trillions of tokens, the exact gradient is astronomically expensive, and an estimate from a few million tokens already points almost exactly the same way — computing the rest is paying for precision you cannot use. Second, hardware: a minibatch is exactly the shape of work GPUs love — the same computation over thousands of independent examples, which becomes the batched matrix multiplies of Chapter 2. Third, and most interesting, the noise may actively help: gradient noise lets the iterate rattle out of saddle points and, on one influential account (Keskar et al., 2016), biases training toward “flat” minima that generalize better than sharp ones. Be warned that this third claim is contested — the flat-minima story has known counterexamples and the field has not settled it — but the empirical headline is uncontroversial: noisy minibatch training reliably finds solutions that generalize, and no one trains any other way.

⚙️In practice

For a large language model (LLM), batch size is denominated in tokens, not examples. A “batch” is a stack of token sequences — say 1,024 sequences of 8,192 tokens each — and every token position contributes its own next-token loss term (Chapter 18), so that stack is a batch of roughly 8.4 million loss terms averaged into one update. Real numbers, approximate and from the published reports: Llama 3 405B pretrained with batches of about 4M tokens early in training, raised to 8M and later 16M tokens as training progressed (Grattafiori et al., 2024); GPT-3 used about 3.2M tokens per batch. Ramping batch size upward mid-run is common because early training benefits from fast noisy steps while late training benefits from cleaner gradients. Batches this large are far beyond one GPU’s memory — they are split across thousands of accelerators whose per-example gradients are averaged with a collective communication step, which is the data-parallelism story of Chapter 33.

One more piece of vocabulary, because it trips up everyone returning to the field. Progress through training is measured in three different currencies:

TermMeaningTypical LLM-scale value (approximate)
Step (= iteration, update)One parameter update from one minibatchHundreds of thousands to ~1M steps per pretraining run
Batch sizeExamples — for LLMs, tokens — averaged into one step~1M–16M tokens per step
EpochOne complete pass through the entire training setOften ~1 or less: frontier runs may see most data about once
Tokens seenSteps × batch size; the field’s preferred x-axis~15T tokens for Llama 3 405B

Classic machine learning thought in epochs (“train for 90 epochs on ImageNet”). LLM training mostly abandoned the epoch: with near-unbounded data, you rarely finish even one full pass, so papers report tokens seen instead, and scaling laws are written in that currency.

Backpropagation, by hand, slowly

Now for the oracle. Gradient descent needs ∂L/∂θi\partial L / \partial \theta_i∂L/∂θi​ for every parameter. For a 70-billion-parameter model, computing each partial derivative independently — say, by nudging each parameter and re-running the model — would take 70 billion forward passes per step. Backpropagation gets all 70 billion partials in roughly the cost of two forward passes. It is not an approximation; it is the chain rule from Chapter 5, organized so that no piece of work is ever done twice.

We will run the entire algorithm on the smallest network that shows the mechanism:

y^  =  w2⋅ϕ(w1⋅x),L  =  12(y^−y)2,\hat{y} \;=\; w_2 \cdot \phi(w_1 \cdot x), \qquad L \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2} (\hat{y} - y)^2,y^​=w2​⋅ϕ(w1​⋅x),L=21​(y^​−y)2,

where ϕ\phiϕ is the rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation ϕ(z)=max⁡(0,z)\phi(z) = \max(0, z)ϕ(z)=max(0,z) from Chapter 8 and the loss is squared error from Chapter 9.

Let’s be completely explicit about what is on the table, because this is where readers most often lose track of the bookkeeping. This is one single training example — one pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) — flowing through the entire network, which has exactly two trainable parameters, w1w_1w1​ and w2w_2w2​. It is not a minibatch: in the language of the previous section, it computes one per-example gradient gig_igi​, and a minibatch step would repeat this whole calculation for BBB examples and average the results. And it is not “one layer”: w1w_1w1​ and w2w_2w2​ live in different layers (a one-unit hidden layer and a one-unit output layer), which is exactly what makes the chain rule necessary — the gradient for w1w_1w1​ has to pass through the rest of the network to reach the loss. Scalars throughout, so every step is pencil-sized; the real thing replaces each scalar with a matrix and each product with a matrix product, and changes nothing conceptual.

The prediction is too low by 0.20.20.2, and the loss is 0.020.020.02. Intuition says both weights should grow (a bigger w1w_1w1​ or w2w_2w2​ raises y^\hat yy^​ toward the target). Backprop will confirm this and tell us by exactly how much each matters.

The backward pass. The forward pass built a computation graph: a chain of small operations, each of which we know how to differentiate locally. The chain rule says the derivative of LLL with respect to any earlier quantity is the product of the local derivatives along the path from that quantity to LLL. Backprop evaluates those products right to left, so each intermediate derivative is computed once and reused.

∑The math

Start at the end and walk backwards, one edge at a time. Each line multiplies the “gradient so far” by one local derivative — the derivative of a single operation, using values saved from the forward pass.

Seed. The derivative of the loss with respect to itself: ∂L∂L=1\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial L} = 1∂L∂L​=1.

Loss node. L=12(y^−y)2L = \tfrac{1}{2}(\hat y - y)^2L=21​(y^​−y)2, so the local derivative is ∂L∂y^=y^−y=−0.2\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}} = \hat{y} - y = -0.2∂y^​∂L​=y^​−y=−0.2. The gradient flowing backward out of the loss node is −0.2-0.2−0.2: raising y^\hat yy^​ by a tiny ε\varepsilonε would change LLL by about −0.2 ε-0.2\,\varepsilon−0.2ε — the loss falls when the (currently too-low) prediction rises. The sign is doing real work.

Output weight. y^=w2h\hat{y} = w_2 hy^​=w2​h has two inputs, and the gradient flows to both:

∂L∂w2=∂L∂y^⋅∂y^∂w2=(−0.2)⋅h=(−0.2)(1.0)=−0.2,∂L∂h=∂L∂y^⋅w2=(−0.2)(0.8)=−0.16.\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial w_2} = (-0.2) \cdot h = (-0.2)(1.0) = \mathbf{-0.2}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial h} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}} \cdot w_2 = (-0.2)(0.8) = -0.16 .∂w2​∂L​=∂y^​∂L​⋅∂w2​∂y^​​=(−0.2)⋅h=(−0.2)(1.0)=−0.2,∂h∂L​=∂y^​∂L​⋅w2​=(−0.2)(0.8)=−0.16.

Note both local derivatives use saved forward values (hhh and w2w_2w2​). The first is a finished answer — the gradient for parameter w2w_2w2​. The second keeps flowing backward.

(Had zzz been negative, this local derivative would be 000 and the gradient for w1w_1w1​ would be exactly zero — the “dead ReLU” issue that Chapter 15 takes up.)

Input weight. z=w1xz = w_1 xz=w1​x, so

∂L∂w1=∂L∂z⋅∂z∂w1=(−0.16)⋅x=(−0.16)(2.0)=−0.32.\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial w_1} = (-0.16) \cdot x = (-0.16)(2.0) = \mathbf{-0.32}.∂w1​∂L​=∂z∂L​⋅∂w1​∂z​=(−0.16)⋅x=(−0.16)(2.0)=−0.32.

Done. The full gradient is ∇L=(∂L/∂w1, ∂L/∂w2)=(−0.32, −0.2)\nabla L = (\partial L/\partial w_1,\, \partial L/\partial w_2) = (-0.32,\, -0.2)∇L=(∂L/∂w1​,∂L/∂w2​)=(−0.32,−0.2). Both partials are negative, so gradient descent — which steps against the gradient — will increase both weights, as intuition predicted. And it learned something intuition alone did not: w1w_1w1​ matters more here (its partial is 1.6×1.6\times1.6× larger), because its influence is amplified by the input x=2x = 2x=2 on its way to the loss.

Close the loop with one gradient-descent step at learning rate η=0.1\eta = 0.1η=0.1:

w1←0.5−0.1×(−0.32)=0.532,w2←0.8−0.1×(−0.2)=0.82.w_1 \leftarrow 0.5 - 0.1 \times (-0.32) = 0.532, \qquad w_2 \leftarrow 0.8 - 0.1 \times (-0.2) = 0.82 .w1​←0.5−0.1×(−0.32)=0.532,w2​←0.8−0.1×(−0.2)=0.82.

The loss dropped from 0.02000.02000.0200 to 0.00810.00810.0081 in one step — the prediction moved from 0.80.80.8 to about 0.870.870.87, toward the target 1.01.01.0. That is the entire learning loop: forward, backward, step. Everything else in deep learning training is this loop with more numbers.

💡Intuition

Two mental models for the backward pass, both worth keeping. Blame assignment: the loss says “I’m 0.2 too low,” and backprop propagates that complaint upstream, with each operation translating it into terms its inputs understand — “for the loss to fall, y^\hat yy^​ should rise; for y^\hat yy^​ to rise, hhh should rise (scaled by w2w_2w2​); for hhh to rise, zzz should rise (ReLU’s gate is open)…” — until every parameter has received a precise, quantified share of the blame. Sensitivity accounting: each backward value ∂L/∂(⋅)\partial L / \partial(\cdot)∂L/∂(⋅) answers “if this quantity were nudged up by a tiny ε\varepsilonε, how much would the loss change?” — and the chain rule says sensitivities along a path multiply.

⚠️Careful

Notice that the backward pass consumed hhh, w2w_2w2​, ϕ′(z)\phi'(z)ϕ′(z), and xxx — all values from the forward pass. This is the defining resource trade of backpropagation: you must keep the forward activations around until the backward pass has used them. In our toy example that is four scalars. In a transformer processing a 4-million-token batch through 80 layers, it is the dominant consumer of GPU memory during training — a point we return to below.

🕰History

The chain rule is 17th-century calculus, and reverse-mode differentiation was discovered several times — notably by Seppo Linnainmaa in 1970 in the context of accumulated rounding error, and by Paul Werbos in his 1974 thesis in the context of neural networks. It became the training algorithm when Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams (1986) showed in a three-page Nature paper that networks trained this way learn useful internal representations — hidden-layer features nobody hand-designed. Forty years later that is still the punchline: backprop is not only how models fit data, it is where all the structure that interpretability later digs for comes from.

Now do it 70 billion times in parallel: autodiff

You have now done by hand what frameworks call automatic differentiation (autodiff, or “autograd” after PyTorch’s implementation of it). It is worth seeing precisely what generalizes from our two-parameter walk-through to a 70-billion-parameter transformer, because the answer is “everything, with the scalars fattened into matrices.”

A framework like PyTorch or JAX watches your forward computation as it runs and records the computation graph — every operation, plus the saved intermediate values (“activations”) its derivative will need, exactly the values our forward-pass table kept. Calling loss.backward() replays that graph in reverse, applying each operation’s local derivative rule, exactly as we did edge by edge. Here is our entire worked example, verbatim, in PyTorch-flavored code:

import torch

x, y = torch.tensor(2.0), torch.tensor(1.0)
w1 = torch.tensor(0.5, requires_grad=True)   # the two parameters
w2 = torch.tensor(0.8, requires_grad=True)

z = w1 * x                     # 1.0   (graph node recorded)
h = torch.relu(z)              # 1.0
y_hat = w2 * h                 # 0.8
loss = 0.5 * (y_hat - y) ** 2  # 0.0200

loss.backward()                # the backward pass we did by hand
print(w1.grad, w2.grad)        # tensor(-0.3200)  tensor(-0.2000)

Those two printed numbers are our hand-computed −0.32-0.32−0.32 and −0.2-0.2−0.2. Nothing symbolic happened, and nothing was approximated by finite differences: the framework ran the same right-to-left chain of local-derivative multiplications we ran, to floating-point precision. Scale the tensors up from scalars to matrices, swap the toy loss for cross-entropy over a vocabulary, and this eleven-line program is the inner loop of frontier pretraining.

The matrix version has one conceptual upgrade, and it is the payoff of the Jacobian machinery from Chapter 5. When the quantities flowing through the network are vectors, each local derivative is a Jacobian matrix, and the chain rule becomes a product of Jacobians. Backprop’s right-to-left order means we always multiply a row vector (the gradient so far — the loss is a scalar, so its derivative with respect to a ddd-dimensional activation is a 1×d1 \times d1×d row) into the next Jacobian: a vector–Jacobian product (VJP). That ordering is the entire efficiency trick. Materializing a layer’s full Jacobian (d×dd \times dd×d, with ddd in the thousands) is unaffordable; the VJP costs about the same as the layer’s forward computation, and the Jacobians never need to be formed explicitly. Each operation only needs a rule for “given the gradient at my output, produce the gradient at my inputs,” which for a linear layer z=Whz = Whz=Wh is a matrix multiply by W⊤W\TW⊤.

This is also why reverse mode wins for deep learning specifically. Autodiff comes in two flavors (Baydin et al., 2015): forward mode costs one pass per input you differentiate with respect to, and reverse mode costs one pass per output. Training has the most lopsided shape imaginable — billions of inputs (the parameters), exactly one output (the scalar loss) — so reverse mode gets the entire gradient in a single backward sweep costing roughly two to three forward passes, while forward mode would need billions of sweeps. Had loss functions been vector-valued and parameters few, deep learning would be built on forward mode; the design of everything downstream flows from “one scalar loss, many parameters.”

⚙️In practice

What this costs, concretely, in a 2026 training run. Compute: the rule of thumb from the scaling-laws literature is that training costs about 6ND6ND6ND floating-point operations for NNN parameters and DDD tokens — 2ND2ND2ND for the forward pass and roughly 4ND4ND4ND for the backward, since the backward pass computes gradients with respect to both activations and weights. The backward pass is about two-thirds of all pretraining compute. Memory: saved activations scale with (batch size × sequence length × width × depth) and routinely exceed the memory of the weights themselves. The standard remedy is gradient checkpointing (also called activation recomputation, Chen et al., 2016): save activations only at every few layers, then recompute the intermediate ones on the fly during the backward pass — trading roughly one extra forward pass worth of compute for a large memory reduction. Virtually every large-model training stack — Megatron-style trainers, DeepSeek’s, Meta’s — uses some version of this, and Chapter 33 covers how it composes with parallelism and mixed precision.

✎Note

A distinction worth keeping crisp: backpropagation is not an optimizer. Backprop computes the gradient; gradient descent (or Adam, or Muon) decides what to do with it. When the next chapter modifies the update rule — adding momentum, per-parameter scaling, weight decay — the backward pass is untouched. Likewise, backprop is not specific to neural networks: it differentiates any composition of differentiable pieces, which is why the same machinery trains diffusion models, fine-tunes with LoRA, and computes the policy gradients of RLHF.

Where this leaves us

  • You can state the learning loop in one line — θ←θ−η∇L(θ)\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \nabla L(\theta)θ←θ−η∇L(θ) — name every symbol in it, and derive from a Taylor expansion why the negative gradient is the locally best direction.
  • You can predict a learning rate’s fate on a quadratic exactly (the (1−2η)(1 - 2\eta)(1−2η) factor), and you know the general lesson: curvature sets the largest stable step, and divergence is a cliff, not a slope.
  • You can define batch, stochastic, and minibatch gradient descent precisely, write the minibatch gradient as an unbiased average of BBB per-example gradients, and translate between steps, epochs, batch size, and tokens seen — including why LLM batch sizes are quoted in millions of tokens.
  • You have executed a complete forward and backward pass by hand on y^=w2 ϕ(w1x)\hat y = w_2\,\phi(w_1 x)y^​=w2​ϕ(w1​x) — one example, two parameters — and verified that one gradient step lowers the loss.
  • You can explain what autodiff frameworks record (the graph and its activations), why reverse mode with vector–Jacobian products gets every parameter’s gradient in one sweep, why activation memory scales with depth, and what gradient checkpointing trades to fix it.
  • You know that loss.backward() is not magic: it is your pencil calculation, industrialized.

The gradient is computed; the question becomes what to do with it. Raw gradient descent turns out to be a fragile way to steer 70 billion parameters, and the fixes — momentum, Adam, AdamW, learning-rate schedules, and 2026’s newer entrants like Muon — are the subject of the next chapter: Optimizers: From SGD to AdamW and Muon.
