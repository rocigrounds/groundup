Residual Connections and the Residual Stream

Why y = x + F(x) — one added arrow — made 100-layer networks trainable, and why the running sum it creates is the single most important object in transformer interpretability.

In this chapter
  1. The degradation problem
  2. Learning corrections instead of remappings
  3. The gradient highway
  4. The reframe: the residual stream
  5. Iterative refinement: the stream is always mid-thought
  6. In practice: residual streams in 2026
  7. Where this leaves us

This chapter is about one added arrow. In 2015, researchers at Microsoft Research took the standard deep-network layer — input goes in, transformed output comes out — and added a shortcut: the input gets added back to the output. In symbols, instead of computing y=F(x)y = F(x)y=F(x), the layer computes y=x+F(x)y = x + F(x)y=x+F(x). That is the entire idea. It fits in a tweet with room to spare.

It is also, arguably, the most consequential architectural decision in modern deep learning. Before it, networks deeper than roughly twenty layers trained worse than shallow ones — not from overfitting, but from a failure of optimization so strange that the field needed a name for it. After it, depth stopped being a problem: 152 layers that year, over 1,000 in experiments soon after, and every transformer since — GPT-5-class, Claude-class, Llama, DeepSeek, all of them — is a residual network from top to bottom.

And there is a second story hiding inside the first, which is why this short chapter earns its place as the finale of Part II. When every layer computes “input plus a correction,” the network stops looking like an assembly line where each station replaces the product with a new one. It starts looking like a single persistent object — a vector per token, called the residual stream — that dozens of components read from and write to, like colleagues editing a shared document. That reframe is the foundation of mechanistic interpretability, the subject of Part VII. Learn it now, while the math is small, and half of the interpretability chapters will feel like coming home.

The degradation problem

By 2015 the field knew that depth was the active ingredient. Each layer of a network composes another function onto the stack, and we saw in the multi-layer perceptron (MLP) chapter that composition is where expressive power comes from: deeper networks can represent functions that shallow ones need exponentially more neurons to match. The ImageNet leaderboard told the same story — the 2012 winner had 8 layers, the 2014 winners had 19 and 22 — so the obvious move was to keep stacking.

It didn’t work. He, Zhang, Ren, and Sun’s ResNet paper opens with the cleanest possible demonstration: on the CIFAR-10 image dataset, a plain 56-layer network performs worse than a plain 20-layer network. Not slightly worse on test data, which you could blame on overfitting — worse on the training data itself. The deeper model, with strictly more parameters and strictly more expressive capacity, could not even fit the training set as well as its shallow sibling. They called this the degradation problem.

Stop and feel how weird that is. There is a construction proof that the deeper network should never lose: take your trained 20-layer network, then stack 36 more layers on top and set every one of them to compute the identity function — output equals input, change nothing. The resulting 56-layer network computes exactly what the 20-layer one does. So a solution at least as good as the shallow network exists in the deeper network’s parameter space. The deeper model doesn’t lack capacity; gradient descent cannot find that solution, or anything close to it.

⚠️Careful

The degradation problem is not vanishing gradients in the classic sense, and the ResNet authors say so explicitly. Their plain networks used batch normalization, and they verified the gradients had healthy magnitudes. The failure is subtler: it is an optimization landscape problem. A stack of many nonlinear layers has no easy way to represent “do nothing,” so even when the identity is the right answer for a layer, the optimizer must steer a pile of weights through nonlinearities to approximate it — and in a 56-layer-deep composition, dozens of layers must all get this right simultaneously. The loss surface of such a network is a maze. Making the identity hard to express made deep networks hard to train even when nothing was numerically wrong.

Why is the identity hard for an ordinary layer? Consider one layer y=σ(Wx+b)y = \sigma(Wx + b)y=σ(Wx+b), where WWW is the weight matrix, bbb the bias, and σ\sigmaσ a nonlinearity like the rectified linear unit (ReLU). To make this compute y=xy = xy=x, the weights must both invert the nonlinearity’s distortion and land exactly on the identity matrix’s behavior — a delicate, precisely coordinated configuration far from where standard initialization puts you (random weights near zero). “Do nothing” is, for a plain layer, a hard function to learn. That single observation is the whole motivation for what comes next.

Learning corrections instead of remappings

Here is the ResNet move, in plain English first. Instead of asking each layer to learn the full mapping from its input to its desired output, give the layer the input for free and ask it to learn only the correction — the delta between input and desired output. If the desired output is the input, the correction is zero, and “output zero” is the easiest thing a neural layer can learn: push the weights toward zero, done.

∑The math

Suppose the mapping we want some stack of layers to implement is H(x)H(x)H(x). A plain block tries to learn HHH directly. A residual block instead defines

y  =  x+F(x),y \;=\; x + F(x),y=x+F(x),

where FFF — the residual function — is a small stack of ordinary layers (in the original ResNet, two convolutions; in a transformer, an attention block or an MLP). The block as a whole still represents H(x)=x+F(x)H(x) = x + F(x)H(x)=x+F(x); we have only re-parameterized what the learnable part must express: F(x)=H(x)−xF(x) = H(x) - xF(x)=H(x)−x, the residual (the “leftover”) — hence the name.

Two immediate consequences:

  1. The identity is free. If the optimal HHH is the identity, the optimal FFF is the zero function, reachable by driving weights toward 000 — the easiest target in the space.
  2. The network starts near the identity. At initialization, FFF‘s weights are small random values, so F(x)≈0F(x) \approx 0F(x)≈0 and each block computes y≈xy \approx xy≈x. A freshly initialized 100-block residual network is approximately the identity function end to end — a gentle, well-behaved starting point from which training carves out corrections. A plain 100-layer network at initialization is 100 composed random nonlinear maps: a scrambler.
💡Intuition

A plain deep network is a bucket brigade in the dark: each layer takes what the previous layer hands it, transforms it completely, and passes on the result. One clumsy layer garbles the message for everyone downstream, and no layer can choose to pass the bucket along unchanged. A residual network changes the contract: the bucket travels on a conveyor belt, and each layer may add something to it — or add nothing. Layers become optional editors rather than mandatory relays. Depth stops being a chain of single points of failure and becomes an accumulation of refinements.

✏️Worked example

A one-dimensional toy you can do on paper. Suppose the mapping a block should learn is nearly the identity: H(x)=1.05xH(x) = 1.05xH(x)=1.05x, a 5% stretch.

A plain linear block y=wxy = wxy=wx must learn w=1.05w = 1.05w=1.05. This “start near the identity, learn deltas” logic should feel familiar: it is the same philosophy behind the initialization schemes of Chapter 12, which fight to keep signal magnitudes stable through depth, and behind normalization, which re-standardizes activations at every layer. All three are attacks on the same enemy — compounding distortion across depth — and modern architectures use all three together.

The gradient highway

The forward-pass story (identity is easy to express) is half the explanation. The backward-pass story is the other half, and it is where residual connections earn the nickname gradient highway.

Recall from the backpropagation chapter that the gradient reaching an early layer is a product of Jacobians — one factor per layer between it and the loss. Products of many factors are unstable: if the factors tend to shrink signals, the product vanishes exponentially; if they amplify, it explodes. Now watch what the skip connection does to each factor.

∑The math

Differentiate one residual block y=x+F(x)y = x + F(x)y=x+F(x) with respect to its input:

∂y∂x  =  I+∂F∂x,\frac{\partial y}{\partial x} \;=\; I + \frac{\partial F}{\partial x},∂x∂y​=I+∂x∂F​,

where III is the identity matrix and ∂F/∂x\partial F / \partial x∂F/∂x is the Jacobian of the learned correction. That "I+I +I+" is the whole trick: whatever FFF‘s Jacobian looks like, the block’s Jacobian is the identity plus a perturbation. Gradients flowing backward through the block are passed through unchanged, plus a correction term — never multiplied into oblivion by the block alone.

Now stack two blocks and expand. With x1=x0+F1(x0)x_1 = x_0 + F_1(x_0)x1​=x0​+F1​(x0​) and x2=x1+F2(x1)x_2 = x_1 + F_2(x_1)x2​=x1​+F2​(x1​), the chain rule gives

  • III: skip both blocks (the pure highway),
  • J1J_1J1​: go through F1F_1F1​, skip F2F_2F2​,
  • J2J_2J2​: skip F1F_1F1​, go through F2F_2F2​,

For LLL blocks the product (I+JL)⋯(I+J1)(I + J_L)\cdots(I + J_1)(I+JL​)⋯(I+J1​) expands into 2L2^L2L paths — every subset of blocks — and exactly one of those paths is the bare identity III. Deep supervision reaches shallow layers no matter what the FFF‘s are doing.

0.850≈1.4×10−50.8^{50} \approx 1.4 \times 10^{-5}0.850≈1.4×10−5

Almost all the gradient arrives via short paths: the highway itself, plus paths that dip into only a handful of blocks.

That last observation was turned into an empirical claim by Veit et al. (2016): a deep residual network behaves less like one 110-layer function and more like an ensemble of many shallow networks — the 2L2^L2L paths — with most of the gradient carried by paths only 10–30 layers long. Consistent with this, they showed you can delete an individual block from a trained ResNet at test time and accuracy barely moves, whereas deleting a layer from a plain network is catastrophic. Layers in a residual network are contributors, not load-bearing links in a chain. Hold onto that; it returns below as “iterative refinement,” and again in Part VII as the reason ablation experiments are even possible.

🕰History

Skip connections were not born in 2015. The idea of shortcut links goes back decades, and the immediate precursor was Highway Networks (Srivastava, Greff, and Schmidhuber, earlier in 2015), which — inspired by the gates of LSTMs, the memory cells you may remember from 2017-era recurrent networks — let each layer learn a gate deciding how much input to pass through versus how much to transform: y=t⋅F(x)+(1−t)⋅xy = t \cdot F(x) + (1-t)\cdot xy=t⋅F(x)+(1−t)⋅x. ResNet’s contribution was partly the simplification — drop the gates, always add the input at full strength — and partly the demonstration that this simpler version scales spectacularly. A 2016 follow-up by the ResNet authors showed why the ungated version wins: any scaling, gating, or nonlinearity placed on the skip path degrades training, because it taxes the highway. The identity must stay an identity. That paper’s “pre-activation” ordering — do your normalization inside the residual branch, never on the skip path — is the direct ancestor of the pre-LN (pre-layer-normalization) transformer layout you met in the normalization chapter.

ResNet’s empirical debut was the stuff of legend. At ImageNet 2015, a 152-layer ResNet — 8× deeper than the 19-layer VGG of the year before — took first place with a top-5 error of 3.57% (ensembled), comfortably below the oft-quoted (approximate) human-performance line of roughly 5%, and swept the detection and segmentation competitions for good measure. On CIFAR-10 the authors trained a 1,202-layer network — not because it was better (it overfit), but because it trained at all, which was the point: the degradation problem was dead. Within two years, “add skip connections” was not a technique but an assumption; when the transformer arrived in 2017, residual connections around every sublayer were included without ceremony, cited as standard practice.

The reframe: the residual stream

Everything so far treats the skip connection as a training aid — a trick that makes optimization work. Now change the camera angle, because the same arrow, viewed differently, reorganizes how you think about what a deep network is. This second view, developed most explicitly in Anthropic’s Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits, is the conceptual backbone of modern interpretability.

Look at the equations of a residual network again, written as a running state:

xℓ+1=xℓ+Fℓ(xℓ),ℓ=0,1,…,L−1.x_{\ell+1} = x_\ell + F_\ell(x_\ell), \qquad \ell = 0, 1, \dots, L-1 .xℓ+1​=xℓ​+Fℓ​(xℓ​),ℓ=0,1,…,L−1.

Unroll the recursion:

xL  =  x0  +  F0(x0)  +  F1(x1)  +  ⋯  +  FL−1(xL−1).x_L \;=\; x_0 \;+\; F_0(x_0) \;+\; F_1(x_1) \;+\; \cdots \;+\; F_{L-1}(x_{L-1}).xL​=x0​+F0​(x0​)+F1​(x1​)+⋯+FL−1​(xL−1​).

The final state is the sum of the original input and every block’s contribution. Nothing is ever overwritten; the network’s state is an accumulating total. In the transformer context, this running sum has a name: the residual stream. Per token, it is a single vector of dimension dmodeld_{\text{model}}dmodel​ (a few thousand dimensions in frontier models — Llama-3-70B uses 8,192) that:

  • starts as the token’s embedding (plus positional information),
  • is read by each attention block and each MLP block — every block’s input is the current stream value, accessed through learned linear projections,
  • is written to by each block — the block’s output is added into the stream, never substituted for it,
  • and ends by being unembedded: multiplied by the output matrix and softmaxed into next-token probabilities.
💡Intuition

The residual stream is a shared workspace — a whiteboard, one per token. The embedding writes the opening note (“the token here is ‘bank’, position 7”). Then dozens of specialists file past: each attention head reads whatever notes it cares about (a low-rank linear read — a projection onto the subspace it attends to), computes something, and appends its own note (“given ‘river’ two tokens back, this ‘bank’ is a riverbank”). Each MLP does the same. No one erases; the board is additive. By the last layer the whiteboard must contain, in some direction, the answer to “what token comes next?” — because the unembedding will read it off with one linear map. The blocks are not stations in an assembly line; they are incremental editors of a persistent state, communicating with each other through the stream: a layer-9 head can consume what a layer-2 MLP wrote, because the layer-2 note is still sitting in the sum.

Three consequences of this picture do heavy lifting later in the book.

Features are directions in the stream. Back in Chapter 1 we promised that “meaning lives in directions of high-dimensional space.” Here is the payoff. Since every write is additive, and reads are linear projections, the natural format for information in the stream is a direction: a block writes “this token refers to a place” by adding a vector component along some place-direction, and any later block that cares applies a projection with a large dot product along that direction. Chapter 49 makes this precise with probes; Chapter 50 asks the uncomfortable question of how a few thousand dimensions can host millions of features (short answer: they get crammed in at slight angles to each other — superposition — which is possible because high-dimensional spaces have room for exponentially many almost-orthogonal directions).

The stream is a bandwidth-limited bus. Every pair of layers that wants to communicate must route its message through the same dmodeld_{\text{model}}dmodel​ dimensions, shared with everyone else’s messages. The framework paper’s authors describe residual-stream bandwidth as a scarce resource in high demand — some directions act like allocated memory, some components even use dimensions as scratch space and other components appear to erase spent information (writing a vector that cancels a previous write — the closest an additive medium comes to deletion). This bus metaphor is why interpretability researchers talk about models the way systems engineers talk about computers.

The whole network is (piecewise) linear-algebra-transparent. Because writes add and reads project, you can meaningfully decompose the final logits into per-block contributions: unembed each block’s write separately and ask “which component pushed the model toward this token?” That decomposition — impossible in a plain network, where each layer’s effect is entangled with every subsequent nonlinearity rewriting the state — is the mechanical basis of attribution and circuit analysis.

A miniature residual stream, d=3d = 3d=3, one token. The embedding writes x0=[1.0, −0.5, 0.2]x_0 = [1.0,\ -0.5,\ 0.2]x0​=[1.0, −0.5, 0.2]. Writes are superimposed, not substituted — and any block’s contribution to the final answer can be isolated by unembedding its Δ\DeltaΔ alone.

✎Note

Is the stream picture exactly true? Almost. Real transformers put normalization at the entrance of each block (pre-LN/pre-RMSNorm), which rescales what a block reads — so reads are not perfectly linear, though the write path stays purely additive, which is what the decompositions rely on. And “features are directions” is a working hypothesis with strong evidence, not a theorem — the field’s live debates about it are the subject of Chapter 49 and Chapter 50. The stream picture is a lens, and an unreasonably productive one; treat it as the default mental model, held with appropriately loose fingers.

Iterative refinement: the stream is always mid-thought

If every layer only nudges a running state, a testable prediction follows: the intermediate states should be partially formed versions of the final answer, getting steadily better with depth — not inscrutable intermediate codes that only make sense at the end.

This is what we observe. The classic demonstration is the logit lens (nostalgebraist, 2020): take the residual stream at layer 15 of a 48-layer GPT-2, apply the final unembedding matrix to it — as if the network ended there — and you get a legible probability distribution over next tokens. Often it is a coarse draft of the final prediction: the right part of speech by the early-middle layers, plausible candidates by the middle, the specific choice sharpening near the end. The stream is never in a foreign format; it is always denominated in “current best guess plus working notes.” (The technique has sharper descendants — the tuned lens fits a small per-layer correction first — and both are tools you will use in Chapter 53.)

Three more strands of evidence, briefly:

  • Layer deletion and reordering. As Veit et al. showed for ResNets — and later work confirmed for transformers — dropping a single middle layer from a trained model degrades output only mildly. Adjacent middle layers can sometimes even be swapped with modest damage. This is what “each layer applies a small refinement” predicts, and what “each layer is stage kkk of a 50-stage pipeline” flatly forbids.
  • Early exit works. Because intermediate streams are already partial predictions, a model can stop computing when the prediction has stabilized — spending fewer layers on an easy token (“the”) than a hard one. A line of “early-exit” and adaptive-depth systems exploits exactly this, checking a confidence signal at intermediate layers and unembedding early when it clears a threshold.
  • Layers specialize by depth, softly. Probing studies across model families find broad (approximate, much-debated) regularities: early layers resolve token identity and local syntax, middle layers do the semantic heavy lifting and move information between positions, late layers concentrate on converting accumulated evidence into the specific output distribution. The boundaries are fuzzy, and circuits routinely span many layers — but the drift of labor with depth is consistent with a document being progressively edited toward a deadline.
💡Intuition

A useful slogan for all of this: depth is iteration, not distance. A plain network’s layers are like miles a message must travel — each one a chance to garble. A residual network’s layers are like passes over a draft — each one a chance to improve, with the draft itself never at risk. That is why more depth kept helping once residual connections existed, and why the network degrades so gracefully when you skip a pass.

In practice: residual streams in 2026

⚙️In practice

Every modern transformer is a residual network. In a decoder-only large language model (LLM), each of the LLL layers contributes two writes to the stream — one from attention, one from the MLP — as two consecutive residual updates: x′=x+Attn(Norm(x))x' = x + \mathrm{Attn}(\mathrm{Norm}(x))x′=x+Attn(Norm(x)), then x′′=x′+MLP(Norm(x′))x'' = x' + \mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{Norm}(x'))x′′=x′+MLP(Norm(x′)), the pre-norm arrangement that Chapter 14 covered and that nearly all frontier models (Llama, DeepSeek, Qwen, GPT- and Claude-class systems) use. Typical scales, as of 2026 and approximate: LLL from ~30 layers (7–8B models) to ~100+ (largest dense models), stream width dmodeld_{\text{model}}dmodel​ from 4,096 (Llama-3-8B) to 8,192 (Llama-3-70B) to 16,384 (Llama-3-405B) — so a frontier model’s “whiteboard” per token is a vector of several thousand numbers receiving a few hundred writes.

Residual scaling at initialization. With 2L2L2L writes accumulating, the stream’s variance grows with depth even when each write is well-scaled — the sum of nnn independent writes grows like n\sqrt{n}n​ (Chapter 12’s variance arithmetic). GPT-2 introduced the standard countermeasure: scale each residual branch’s output weight matrix by 1/N1/\sqrt{N}1/N​ at init, where NNN is the number of residual layers, so the initial network stays close to the identity in a calibrated way. Most modern stacks do some version of this, via init scaling or normalization placement.

Depth-stability research. DeepNet (Microsoft, 2022) pushed the analysis further — deriving how much to up-weight the skip path and down-scale the residual branch as a function of depth — and trained 1,000-layer transformers. Related tricks (ReZero-style learned per-branch scalars initialized at zero; maximal-update-parameterization (μP) width/depth scaling rules covered in Chapter 34) share the same north star: begin as close to the identity as possible, and let training grow the corrections. In 2026 this remains active engineering at frontier scale, where a single divergent run costs millions of GPU-hours — but it is tuning around the residual idea, never a replacement for it.

⚠️Careful

A common misreading, worth killing early: the residual stream is per token. A transformer processing 1,000 tokens maintains 1,000 parallel streams, one whiteboard per position. The only mechanism that moves information between streams is attention — an attention head at position 40 can read from the stream at position 7 and write what it found into stream 40. MLPs, by contrast, edit each stream in isolation. This division of labor — attention moves information across positions, MLPs process it within a position — is the organizing fact of transformer architecture, and it only makes sense once you have the stream picture.

Where this leaves us

  • You can state the degradation problem — deeper plain networks underperform shallow ones on training data — and explain why it is an optimization failure, not a capacity or overfitting failure: the identity solution exists but plain layers make “do nothing” hard to express.
  • You can write the residual block y=x+F(x)y = x + F(x)y=x+F(x) and articulate the re-parameterization: FFF learns the correction H(x)−xH(x) - xH(x)−x, so near-zero initialization means the whole deep network starts near the identity.
  • You can derive ∂y/∂x=I+∂F/∂x\partial y/\partial x = I + \partial F/\partial x∂y/∂x=I+∂F/∂x, expand a 2-block stack into its exact sum of 2L2^L2L paths, and explain why the bare-identity path delivers the loss gradient to every layer untouched — the gradient highway that ended the vanishing-through-depth era.
  • You know the empirical history: Highway Networks’ gated skips, ResNet’s ungated simplification, ImageNet 2015, and the follow-up lesson that the skip path must remain a clean identity.
  • You can redraw a transformer as a residual stream: a per-token running sum that attention heads and MLPs read from (linearly) and write to (additively), with features living as directions in the stream and layers acting as incremental editors — depth as iteration, not distance.
  • You can cite the evidence for iterative refinement: the logit lens showing intermediate states are already draft predictions, graceful layer deletion, early exit, and soft depth-specialization.
  • You know the 2026 practice: pre-norm residual stacks everywhere, 1/N1/\sqrt{N}1/N​-style residual init scaling, and DeepNet-style depth stabilization.

Part II is complete: you now hold every anatomical piece of a trainable deep network — neurons, losses, gradients, optimizers, initialization, regularization, normalization, activations, and the residual spine that ties arbitrary depth together.
