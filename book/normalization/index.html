Normalization: BatchNorm to RMSNorm

Why activations drift during training, how BatchNorm, LayerNorm, and RMSNorm re-standardize them on the fly, and why every modern LLM settled on pre-norm RMSNorm — with worked examples you can check by hand.

In the initialization chapter we did careful variance bookkeeping: choose the initial weights so that signals keep roughly the same scale as they pass through each layer, and gradients keep roughly the same scale as they flow back. That bookkeeping has a weakness we glossed over: it holds at step 0 and then immediately starts to rot. The moment training begins, the optimizer changes the weights, the weights change the statistics of every layer’s output, and the delicate scale balance we engineered at initialization drifts — slowly at first, then sometimes catastrophically. A 30-layer network whose activations grow by even 10% per layer ends up multiplying its input scale by 1.130≈171.1^{30} \approx 171.130≈17; at 5% shrinkage per layer it divides by 0.95−30≈4.60.95^{-30} \approx 4.60.95−30≈4.6. Either way, layers deep in the stack see inputs whose scale keeps changing under their feet.

Normalization layers are the field’s blunt, effective answer: stop hoping the scales stay put, and instead re-standardize the activations continuously, inside the network, as part of the forward pass. A normalization layer measures the mean and spread of the numbers flowing through it right now and rescales them to a standard size — every step, every input, forever. Because the operation is differentiable, gradients flow through it and the network learns around it. Initialization sets the scales once; normalization holds them in place for the next trillion tokens.

This chapter builds the three normalizers that matter, in the order history built them. Batch normalization (2015) made deep vision networks trainable and made normalization a default; it also has a structural flaw — dependence on the batch — that makes it nearly unusable for language models. Layer normalization (2016) fixed the flaw by normalizing across a single token’s features, and powered the original transformer and the GPT series. RMSNorm (2019) then noticed that half of LayerNorm’s work can be skipped, and became the choice of nearly every frontier large language model (LLM) since Llama. Along the way we settle the question of where the normalizer goes — pre-norm versus post-norm, one of the most consequential architecture decisions in the transformer’s history — and meet QK-norm, the newest member of the family, which keeps attention itself from blowing up.

The problem: distributions that refuse to sit still

Consider one layer in the middle of a deep network. Its job is to take whatever the previous layer emits and compute something useful from it. During training, this layer faces a moving target: it is trying to learn a function of its inputs while the distribution of those inputs keeps changing, because every layer below it is also being updated. Layer 20 spends the early steps of training learning to handle activations of size 1; then layers 1–19 shift, and suddenly layer 20’s inputs are twice as large and centered somewhere else, and the weights it learned are miscalibrated. It adapts; the layers below shift again.

💡Intuition

Picture an assembly line where every station is being retrained at once. Station 20’s worker learns to install a part on chassis that arrive at a certain height — but stations 1 through 19 are also changing what they do, so the chassis keep arriving at different heights. Much of station 20’s “learning” is wasted re-adapting to upstream changes rather than getting better at its actual job. A normalization layer is a jig bolted to the line in front of each station: no matter what upstream does, the chassis is clamped to a standard height before the worker touches it. Upstream can now be retrained freely without invalidating downstream skills.

The BatchNorm paper named this moving-target problem internal covariate shift — “covariate shift” being the statistics term for a change in input distribution — and proposed normalization as the cure. The name stuck; the explanation, interestingly, did not hold up. Santurkar et al. (2018) ran experiments where they deliberately re-injected distribution shift after BatchNorm layers (adding noise with changing mean and variance) and found the networks trained almost as well anyway. Their alternative account: normalization makes the loss landscape dramatically smoother — the loss and its gradients change less abruptly as you move through parameter space — which lets the optimizer take larger, more confident steps without careening off a cliff. The honest 2026 summary is that normalization works extraordinarily well and the field is still not entirely settled on why; the smoothness story has the better experimental support, and it connects directly to what the optimizers chapter said about why loss-surface curvature limits step sizes.

Whatever the mechanism, the practical effects were immediate and dramatic: networks with normalization train with learning rates 10× larger, are far less sensitive to initialization, converge in many fewer steps, and — the change that mattered most for everything after 2015 — can be made much deeper without dying. The figure below shows the failure mode normalization removes.

One more framing before the mechanics, because it is the one that explains the design details: normalization is a standardization the optimizer doesn’t have to fight. You could imagine achieving stable scales by adding a loss penalty that punishes large activations, and hoping gradient descent keeps things tidy. Normalization instead builds the tidiness into the architecture as a hard constraint: the output of a normalization layer has standard scale by construction, no matter what the weights do. The optimizer is then free to move weights around aggressively — scale errors it introduces are erased in the very same forward pass.

Batch normalization

Ioffe and Szegedy’s 2015 paper asks: what is the most direct way to give a neuron standardized inputs? Answer: standardize each feature the way a statistician would standardize a dataset column — subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation. But the true mean and standard deviation of a feature depend on the whole data distribution, which we never see at once. BatchNorm’s move is to estimate them from the current minibatch, on the fly, every training step.

The mechanics

∑The math

Take one feature (one neuron’s pre-activation) and a minibatch of mmm examples, giving values x1,…,xmx_1, \dots, x_mx1​,…,xm​. BatchNorm computes, in order:

μ=1m∑i=1mxiσ2=1m∑i=1m(xi−μ)2\mu = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} x_i \qquad\qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} (x_i - \mu)^2μ=m1​i=1∑m​xi​σ2=m1​i=1∑m​(xi​−μ)2

— the batch mean and batch variance of this feature. Then it standardizes each value:

x^i=xi−μσ2+ϵ\hat{x}_i = \frac{x_i - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}}x^i​=σ2+ϵ​xi​−μ​

where ϵ\epsilonϵ is a small constant (typically 10−510^{-5}10−5) that prevents division by zero when the batch variance happens to be tiny. After this step the feature has mean 0 and variance ≈ 1 within the batch. Finally, two learnable parameters per feature — a scale γ\gammaγ (gamma, initialized to 1) and a shift β\betaβ (beta, initialized to 0) — produce the output:

yi=γ x^i+β.y_i = \gamma\, \hat{x}_i + \beta.yi​=γx^i​+β.

Every feature gets its own μ,σ2,γ,β\mu, \sigma^2, \gamma, \betaμ,σ2,γ,β; the whole procedure runs independently per feature (per channel, in a convolutional network). All four steps are differentiable, so backpropagation treats the layer like any other — with the subtle twist that μ\muμ and σ2\sigma^2σ2 depend on every example in the batch, so each example’s gradient flows partly through every other example’s forward pass.

One feature, minibatch of m=4m = 4m=4 with values x=[1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 6.0]x = [1.0,\ 2.0,\ 3.0,\ 6.0]x=[1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 6.0].

Scale and shift: suppose the network has learned γ=1.5\gamma = 1.5γ=1.5, β=0.5\beta = 0.5β=0.5. Then y=1.5 x^+0.5=[−1.104, −0.302, 0.5, 2.906]y = 1.5\,\hat{x} + 0.5 = [-1.104,\ -0.302,\ 0.5,\ 2.906]y=1.5x^+0.5=[−1.104, −0.302, 0.5, 2.906] — a distribution with mean 0.50.50.5 and standard deviation 1.51.51.5, but chosen by learnable parameters rather than inherited accidentally from upstream drift.

Why the learnable scale and shift don’t undo the point

A fair question: if we standardize to mean 0, variance 1, and then immediately let γ\gammaγ and β\betaβ move the mean and variance somewhere else — haven’t we built a machine that does nothing? The network could even learn γ=σ\gamma = \sigmaγ=σ, β=μ\beta = \muβ=μ and exactly reverse the normalization.

The resolution is that γ\gammaγ and β\betaβ restore expressiveness, not the old dynamics. Without them, every normalized feature would be forced to mean 0 and variance 1 forever — a real restriction (a sigmoid fed only mean-zero, unit-variance inputs is stuck in its near-linear middle region; a ReLU (rectified linear unit) would always zero out about half its inputs). With them, the layer can represent any mean and variance it wants. But how that mean and variance are controlled has completely changed. Before BatchNorm, a feature’s scale was the tangled product of every upstream weight — thousands of parameters, all shifting at once, none of them “in charge” of scale. After BatchNorm, the scale is controlled by exactly one parameter, γ\gammaγ, with a direct, clean gradient. Upstream weights are freed from scale bookkeeping entirely: multiply an upstream weight matrix by 100 and the normalization erases the change (the batch statistics scale by the same 100 and cancel it). Scale has been moved from an emergent global property into a single learnable knob per feature — and in the rare event that undoing the normalization really is optimal, the network can still learn to do it.

✎Note

This scale-invariance has a curious side effect on regularization: if scaling a weight matrix doesn’t change the function, why does weight decay on that matrix do anything? The answer is that shrinking scale-invariant weights changes the size of relative gradient steps — smaller weights mean each update rotates the weight vector further — so weight decay works as an effective learning-rate control rather than a function penalty. This interaction between normalization and weight decay is a genuine subtlety of modern training recipes.

Training vs inference: the running statistics

BatchNorm as described has an obvious problem at deployment time: the model is supposed to answer for one input, but the layer needs a batch to compute μ\muμ and σ2\sigma^2σ2. Worse, even with a batch available you wouldn’t want predictions for one input to depend on which other inputs happened to share its batch — the model would be nondeterministic.

The standard fix is to keep running estimates of each feature’s mean and variance during training, via an exponential moving average (EMA) updated at every step:

μrun←(1−α) μrun+α μbatch,σrun2←(1−α) σrun2+α σbatch2,\mu_{\text{run}} \leftarrow (1 - \alpha)\,\mu_{\text{run}} + \alpha\,\mu_{\text{batch}}, \qquad \sigma^2_{\text{run}} \leftarrow (1 - \alpha)\,\sigma^2_{\text{run}} + \alpha\,\sigma^2_{\text{batch}},μrun​←(1−α)μrun​+αμbatch​,σrun2​←(1−α)σrun2​+ασbatch2​,

where α\alphaα is a momentum-like constant (PyTorch’s default is α=0.1\alpha = 0.1α=0.1). At inference, the layer stops computing batch statistics and uses the frozen running values instead: every input is normalized by the same μrun,σrun2\mu_{\text{run}}, \sigma^2_{\text{run}}μrun​,σrun2​, making the layer a fixed affine transformation — deterministic, batch-independent, and cheap.

⚠️Careful

BatchNorm is the reason the model.train() / model.eval() distinction exists in every deep learning framework, and forgetting it is one of the classic bugs in the field. Evaluate with the network still in training mode and your “predictions” use statistics computed from the test batch — accuracy changes with batch composition and batch size, and a batch of one produces garbage (variance 0 across a single example). Train with the network accidentally in eval mode and the running statistics never update while gradients assume they do. If a normalization-bearing model behaves wildly differently between training and evaluation, check the mode flag first.

Why batch dependence became a liability

For 2015-era image classifiers — big batches of independent photos — BatchNorm was close to free lunch, and it powered ResNets and years of vision progress. But the batch dependence that makes it work is a structural liability that grew worse as the field moved toward sequences and scale:

  • Small batches break it. The batch statistics are estimates of distribution statistics, and with 2–4 examples per device the estimates are noisy enough to hurt badly. Accuracy of BatchNorm networks degrades sharply as batch size shrinks — a problem for memory-hungry models that can only fit a few examples per graphics processing unit (GPU). (Group normalization, Wu & He 2018, was invented for exactly this regime in vision.)
  • Sequences make the statistics weird. In a language model the “batch” dimension of an activation tensor is batch × sequence position. Pooling statistics across positions mixes tokens at position 3 with tokens at position 3,000; padding tokens in variable-length batches pollute the statistics; and during autoregressive generation the model sees one token at a time, so no meaningful batch statistics exist at all.
  • Distributed training makes it expensive or wrong. With the batch split across many GPUs, correct statistics require an extra cross-device synchronization (SyncBN) at every normalization layer — extra communication in exactly the place (parallel training) where communication is the bottleneck. Skipping the sync means each device normalizes by different statistics.
  • Information leaks across examples. Because each example’s output depends on its batchmates, BatchNorm can become an unintended side channel. In reinforcement learning and contrastive learning this is a documented source of silent cheating — the model exploits batch statistics to compare examples it was never supposed to compare.

By 2016 the fix was clear, and it is a one-sentence change.

Layer normalization: normalize the token, not the batch

Ba, Kiros, and Hinton (2016) kept BatchNorm’s arithmetic and changed only the direction of the average. BatchNorm normalizes one feature across many examples. Layer normalization (LayerNorm) normalizes one example across its many features. Each token’s activation vector is standardized using the mean and variance of its own components — no batch, no other examples, no running statistics, no train/eval split. Every token carries its own normalizer with it.

∑The math

For a single activation vector h∈Rdh \in \R^dh∈Rd (one token’s hidden state, with ddd features h1,…,hdh_1, \dots, h_dh1​,…,hd​):

μ=1d∑j=1dhj,σ2=1d∑j=1d(hj−μ)2,LayerNorm(h)j=γj hj−μσ2+ϵ+βj.\mu = \frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d} h_j, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(h_j - \mu)^2, \qquad \text{LayerNorm}(h)_j = \gamma_j\,\frac{h_j - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}} + \beta_j.μ=d1​j=1∑d​hj​,σ2=d1​j=1∑d​(hj​−μ)2,LayerNorm(h)j​=γj​σ2+ϵ​hj​−μ​+βj​.

The formulas are BatchNorm’s with the sum over batch index iii replaced by a sum over feature index jjj. The learnable γ,β∈Rd\gamma, \beta \in \R^dγ,β∈Rd are again per-feature (elementwise), so each of the ddd dimensions can have its own learned scale and offset — 2d2d2d parameters per LayerNorm.

✏️Worked example

A single 4-dimensional token vector h=[2, −1, 0, 3]h = [2,\ -1,\ 0,\ 3]h=[2, −1, 0, 3], so d=4d = 4d=4.

Mean over features: μ=(2−1+0+3)/4=1.0\mu = (2 - 1 + 0 + 3)/4 = 1.0μ=(2−1+0+3)/4=1.0.

Variance over features: deviations [1,−2,−1,2][1, -2, -1, 2][1,−2,−1,2], so σ2=(1+4+1+4)/4=2.5\sigma^2 = (1 + 4 + 1 + 4)/4 = 2.5σ2=(1+4+1+4)/4=2.5 and 2.5≈1.581\sqrt{2.5} \approx 1.5812.5​≈1.581.

Normalize: h^=[1,−2,−1,2]/1.581=[0.632, −1.265, −0.632, 1.265]\hat{h} = [1, -2, -1, 2]/1.581 = [0.632,\ -1.265,\ -0.632,\ 1.265]h^=[1,−2,−1,2]/1.581=[0.632, −1.265, −0.632, 1.265].

With γ=[1,1,1,1]\gamma = [1,1,1,1]γ=[1,1,1,1], β=[0,0,0,0]\beta = [0,0,0,0]β=[0,0,0,0] (their initial values), that is the output. Two things worth checking with a pencil: the components sum to 0 (the mean was removed), and the vector’s length is 0.6322+1.2652+0.6322+1.2652=4.0=2=d\sqrt{0.632^2 + 1.265^2 + 0.632^2 + 1.265^2} = \sqrt{4.0} = 2 = \sqrt{d}0.6322+1.2652+0.6322+1.2652​=4.0​=2=d​. That last fact is general and geometric: LayerNorm (before γ,β\gamma, \betaγ,β) projects every activation vector onto the sphere of radius d\sqrt{d}d​ intersected with the “mean-zero” hyperplane. Whatever length upstream layers produce, the output lands on that sphere; only the direction of the activation vector survives. This connects to a running theme from high-dimensional geometry: the information is in the direction, and normalization makes that literal.

Note what changed and what didn’t. LayerNorm is batch-independent: batch size 1 works, generation token-by-token works, no synchronization across devices is ever needed, and the same code path runs at training and inference — the entire running-statistics apparatus disappears. It is also position-independent: the token at position 3 and the token at position 3,000 are each normalized by their own statistics.

The cost is a different assumption. BatchNorm standardizes each feature separately, respecting that feature 7 and feature 12 might legitimately live on different scales; LayerNorm pools all features of a token into one mean and one variance, implicitly treating the ddd dimensions as exchangeable. For hidden states inside a network — where no dimension has privileged meaning, especially given what superposition will say about features not aligning with dimensions anyway — this assumption is harmless, and the per-dimension γj\gamma_jγj​ recovers per-feature scaling where it matters. LayerNorm became the normalizer of the original transformer and of GPT-1 through GPT-3.

RMSNorm: drop the mean, keep the scale

LayerNorm does two distinct things to a vector: it re-centers (subtracts the mean, requiring one pass to compute μ\muμ) and it re-scales (divides by the standard deviation, requiring a second statistic). Zhang and Sennrich (2019) asked which of the two earns its keep — and found, across machine translation and language modeling experiments, that re-centering was doing approximately nothing. Networks trained with scale-only normalization matched full LayerNorm’s quality. So root mean square normalization (RMSNorm) deletes the mean entirely and divides by the root-mean-square of the components:

∑The math

For h∈Rdh \in \R^dh∈Rd, define the root mean square

RMS(h)=1d∑j=1dhj2+ϵ,\text{RMS}(h) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d} h_j^2 + \epsilon},RMS(h)=d1​j=1∑d​hj2​+ϵ​,

and output

RMSNorm(h)j=γj hjRMS(h).\text{RMSNorm}(h)_j = \gamma_j\, \frac{h_j}{\text{RMS}(h)}.RMSNorm(h)j​=γj​RMS(h)hj​​.

Compared with LayerNorm: no μ\muμ (one reduction over the vector instead of two), and — in the standard formulation — no additive β\betaβ either, halving the parameter count to ddd. Note the relationship to vector length: since ∥h∥=∑jhj2\|h\| = \sqrt{\sum_j h_j^2}∥h∥=∑j​hj2​​, we have RMS(h)=∥h∥/d\text{RMS}(h) = \|h\|/\sqrt{d}RMS(h)=∥h∥/d​ (ignoring ϵ\epsilonϵ), so RMSNorm is exactly “rescale hhh to length d\sqrt{d}d​, then apply the elementwise gain γ\gammaγ”. It is pure direction-preservation: where LayerNorm projects onto a sphere after removing the mean component, RMSNorm projects the raw vector onto the sphere and touches nothing else.

✏️Worked example

Same token as before: h=[2, −1, 0, 3]h = [2,\ -1,\ 0,\ 3]h=[2, −1, 0, 3], d=4d = 4d=4.

Mean of squares: (4+1+0+9)/4=3.5(4 + 1 + 0 + 9)/4 = 3.5(4+1+0+9)/4=3.5, so RMS(h)=3.5≈1.871\text{RMS}(h) = \sqrt{3.5} \approx 1.871RMS(h)=3.5​≈1.871.

Normalize: h/1.871=[1.069, −0.535, 0, 1.604]h / 1.871 = [1.069,\ -0.535,\ 0,\ 1.604]h/1.871=[1.069, −0.535, 0, 1.604] (with γ\gammaγ at its initialization of all ones, this is the output).

Compare with the LayerNorm output for the same vector, [0.632,−1.265,−0.632,1.265][0.632, -1.265, -0.632, 1.265][0.632,−1.265,−0.632,1.265]: different numbers, because LayerNorm first removed the mean of 1.01.01.0 and RMSNorm did not — the RMSNorm output’s components sum to 2.1382.1382.138, not 000. What both outputs share is the scale: each has length exactly d=2\sqrt{d} = 2d​=2 (check: 1.0692+0.5352+0+1.6042=1.143+0.286+2.573≈4.01.069^2 + 0.535^2 + 0 + 1.604^2 = 1.143 + 0.286 + 2.573 \approx 4.01.0692+0.5352+0+1.6042=1.143+0.286+2.573≈4.0 ✓). The empirical claim of the RMSNorm paper is that this shared property — fixed scale — is the one that matters for stable training, and the property they differ on — centering — is close to irrelevant in practice.

Why is dropping the mean nearly free in quality terms? Honest answer: the field has ablations, not a theorem. The suggestive intuition is that what normalization must control is the magnitude explosion/vanishing documented in the first figure — a scale phenomenon. The mean of a hidden vector is one direction (the all-ones direction) out of thousands; re-centering deletes that single component, a minor geometric edit that the next weight matrix could absorb anyway. Meanwhile the cost difference is real: one fewer reduction over the hidden dimension, fewer parameters, a shorter dependency chain in the backward pass. Zhang and Sennrich reported wall-clock reductions ranging from a few percent to tens of percent depending on the architecture (their headline range was roughly 7–64%); in a modern fused-kernel transformer the saving is smaller but still nonzero, and at frontier-training scale (the training run) nonzero savings on an operation executed at every layer of every step are worth taking — especially when quality is unchanged.

⚙️In practice

As of 2026, RMSNorm is not an alternative — it is the default. Llama (all generations), Mistral, Qwen, DeepSeek-V2/V3, Gemma, and nearly every prominent open-weights transformer since 2023 uses pre-norm RMSNorm; the GPT-2/3 lineage’s LayerNorm survives mainly in older checkpoints and in BERT-era encoders. Typical settings: ϵ=10−5\epsilon = 10^{-5}ϵ=10−5 or 10−610^{-6}10−6; γ\gammaγ initialized to ones. One implementation detail that matters at scale: even when the model trains in bf16 (the 16-bit brain floating-point format — precision), the sum of squares inside RMSNorm is accumulated in fp32 (32-bit) — a low-precision reduction over 8,000+ values is exactly where rounding error concentrates, and normalization layers are load-bearing for stability. Framework kernels (e.g., fused RMSNorm in most training stacks) do this for you; hand-rolled implementations that skip it are a known source of mysterious divergence.

Pre-norm vs post-norm: where the normalizer goes

We now know what the normalizer computes. The next question — where it sits relative to the residual connection — sounds like a detail and turned out to shape five years of transformer training practice. Recall from residual connections that a transformer block computes x+Sublayer(x)x + \text{Sublayer}(x)x+Sublayer(x): a skip path (the “residual highway”) carries xxx forward unchanged while a sublayer (attention or a multi-layer perceptron, MLP) computes a correction. The normalizer can go in two places:

  • Post-norm (the original transformer, 2017): normalize after the addition — xout=Norm(x+Sublayer(x))x_{\text{out}} = \text{Norm}(x + \text{Sublayer}(x))xout​=Norm(x+Sublayer(x)). The normalizer sits on the highway; everything, including the skip path, passes through it.
  • Pre-norm (GPT-2 onward, and nearly everything since): normalize before the sublayer, on the branch only — xout=x+Sublayer(Norm(x))x_{\text{out}} = x + \text{Sublayer}(\text{Norm}(x))xout​=x+Sublayer(Norm(x)). The highway runs clean from the embeddings to the final layer; normalizers only guard the entrances to the sublayers.

Why does this matter so much? Follow the gradient. In a pre-norm network the output is, unrolling the recursion, the input plus a sum of branch contributions:

xL  =  x0  +  ∑ℓ=0L−1Sublayerℓ(Norm(xℓ)),x_L \;=\; x_0 \;+\; \sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1} \text{Sublayer}_\ell\big(\text{Norm}(x_\ell)\big),xL​=x0​+ℓ=0∑L−1​Sublayerℓ​(Norm(xℓ​)),

so ∂xL/∂x0\partial x_L / \partial x_0∂xL​/∂x0​ contains an identity term — a gradient superhighway straight from the loss to every layer, exactly the property that made residual networks trainable at depth. In a post-norm network there is no such identity term: the gradient reaching layer ℓ\ellℓ has been multiplied by the Jacobian of a normalization L−ℓL - \ellL−ℓ times, and each of those Jacobians rescales the gradient by a data-dependent factor. Xiong et al. (2020) worked out what this does at initialization: in post-norm transformers, gradient magnitudes near the output layers are large, so the network can only survive its first steps if the learning rate starts tiny — which is why the original transformer recipe required the now-famous learning-rate warmup (start near zero, ramp up over thousands of steps) and diverged without it. Pre-norm transformers, in their analysis and experiments, have well-behaved gradients at initialization across all depths and can train without warmup at learning rates that would kill a post-norm model.

💡Intuition

Post-norm says: “compute the update, add it in, then re-standardize the sum” — the network’s accumulated state is renormalized at every block, so nothing can grow, but also nothing survives untouched; the highway has a toll booth every mile, and by the 60th booth the gradient’s scale is whatever the booths made of it. Pre-norm says: “standardize what the sublayer sees, but leave the accumulated state alone” — the residual stream becomes a protected ledger that sublayers read from (through a normalizer) and write into (additively), and gradients travel the ledger for free. That “residual stream as ledger” picture becomes the central object of the interpretability chapters later in the book.

Pre-norm’s dominance was cemented by scale: as models went from 12 layers to 100+, post-norm’s depth-dependent gradient pathology went from “needs warmup” to “diverges outright,” while pre-norm stayed docile. GPT-2 shipped pre-norm in 2019, and Llama, Mistral, Qwen, DeepSeek, Gemma, and their contemporaries are all pre-norm architectures. Two footnotes keep the story honest, though. First, pre-norm has a known cost: because the stream is never renormalized, its magnitude grows with depth (each block adds a contribution on top of what is already there), so later sublayers see a stream whose scale dwarfs their own additive updates — several papers argue this makes very deep pre-norm layers behave too much like the identity, “wasting” depth, and post-norm models, when you can train them, sometimes end slightly stronger. Second, hybrids exist: Gemma 2 normalizes both the input and the output of each sublayer branch (“sandwich” placement — both norms on the branch, highway still clean), buying extra stability without giving up the identity path. Placement remains an active dial, but the center of gravity in 2026 is unambiguous: pre-norm, with one final normalization applied to the stream after the last block, right before the unembedding — without which the ever-growing stream would feed the output layer at an arbitrary scale.

⚠️Careful

A pre-norm model has 2L+12L + 12L+1 normalization layers for LLL blocks (two per block plus the final one), and the final one is load-bearing: reimplementations that forget the last RMSNorm before the language-model head produce a model that trains but generates subtly badly calibrated logits. When porting checkpoints between frameworks, mismatched ϵ\epsilonϵ values (10−510^{-5}10−5 vs 10−610^{-6}10−6) and a dropped final norm are two of the most common silent-quality bugs.

QK-norm: keeping attention’s logits sane

Normalizing the residual stream turned out not to be quite enough. Inside scaled dot-product attention, the model computes logits q⋅k/dhq \cdot k / \sqrt{d_h}q⋅k/dh​​ between query and key vectors. The dh\sqrt{d_h}dh​​ divisor calibrates the dot product assuming qqq and kkk have roughly unit-scale components — but nothing enforces that. The query and key projections are ordinary weight matrices, and during long training runs their outputs can grow. When they do, attention logits grow quadratically (both factors of the dot product scale up), the softmax saturates into a one-hot distribution, gradients through it collapse toward zero, and — as the team scaling a 22-billion-parameter Vision Transformer (ViT-22B) documented — training loss diverges. These attention-logit explosions became a signature instability of large training runs, showing up as sudden loss spikes mid-run (the training run chapter covers the operational side: who gets paged and what gets rolled back).

QK-norm is the by-now-familiar cure applied one level deeper: normalize the queries and keys themselves, per head, immediately before the dot product —

logits  =  Norm(q)⋅Norm(k)dh,\text{logits} \;=\; \frac{\text{Norm}(q) \cdot \text{Norm}(k)}{\sqrt{d_h}},logits=dh​​Norm(q)⋅Norm(k)​,

with Norm being RMSNorm or LayerNorm with its own learnable gain. Now the logit scale is pinned by construction: no matter what the projection matrices do, the dot product of two fixed-length vectors is bounded, softmax saturation can’t run away, and the learnable gains give the model back a controlled knob for how sharp its attention may get. The cost is small (two extra norms per attention layer over head-sized vectors), and adoption through 2024–25 was rapid: Gemma 3, Qwen3, and OLMo 2 all ship QK-norm, and it has become a standard item on the stability checklist for new pretraining runs — not universal (Llama 3 does without it), but no longer exotic.

Life without normalization?

Given how much machinery this chapter has added, a reasonable question is whether any of it is necessary or whether it is a crutch for bad initialization. The field has probed this seriously. Careful init-and-rescaling schemes (Fixup, SkipInit) train deep residual networks with no normalization at all; NFNets (2021) matched BatchNorm-network accuracy on ImageNet normalization-free, using scaled weight standardization and aggressive gradient clipping. More recently, Zhu et al. (2025) showed that transformers’ LayerNorm/RMSNorm layers can be replaced by Dynamic Tanh (DyT) — the elementwise function tanh⁡(αx)\tanh(\alpha x)tanh(αx) with a learnable α\alphaα, no statistics computed at all — with matching quality across vision and language models. The observation motivating DyT is telling: trained LayerNorms empirically behave like soft squashing functions (their input–output maps look tanh-shaped), so perhaps the statistics-gathering was never the essential part — bounding extreme values was.

✎Note

Read these results as clarifying, not as practice-changing. They demonstrate that normalization layers are not magic — their benefits (bounded activations, smoother optimization, tamed outliers) can be obtained by other means, at the price of more delicate recipes. But every frontier LLM you can name in 2026 still trains with RMSNorm: at $100M+ per pretraining run, nobody trades a mechanism that is cheap, well-understood, and boring for one that saves a rounding error of compute and adds risk. “Boring and load-bearing” is the highest compliment infrastructure can earn.

The modern decoder block, annotated

Time to assemble the chapter into the exact placement used by a 2026-vintage decoder-only LLM. One block of a Llama-family model computes, for a residual stream state xxx:

# attention half-block
a   = RMSNorm₁(x)                     # pre-norm: standardize what attention sees
q,k = RMSNorm_q(W_q a), RMSNorm_k(W_k a)   # QK-norm (if used), per head
h   = x + Attention(q, k, W_v a)      # write result onto the clean highway

# MLP half-block
m   = RMSNorm₂(h)                     # pre-norm again, separate learned gains
out = h + MLP(m)

Stack LLL of these blocks, then finish with the one normalization that lives outside any block:

final  = RMSNorm_f(x_L)               # tame the accumulated stream
logits = W_unembed · final

Count the roles: two RMSNorms per block guarding the sublayer entrances, optional QK-norms inside attention guarding the logits, one final RMSNorm guarding the unembedding — and a residual highway that runs from the embedding layer to the top without a single normalizer, weight, or nonlinearity on it. Every design choice in that listing is one this chapter derived: why RMS instead of LayerNorm (same stability, one reduction cheaper, no evidence centering helps), why pre instead of post (identity gradient path, no warmup pathology, stable at depth), why the extra norms inside attention (logit explosion at scale), why the final norm (pre-norm streams grow with depth).

⚙️In practice

Concrete 2026 defaults, for orientation (approximate, and conventions drift): RMSNorm with ϵ=10−5\epsilon = 10^{-5}ϵ=10−5–10−610^{-6}10−6 and fp32 statistic accumulation; gains initialized to 1; no bias terms anywhere in the norm. Llama 3, Mistral, and DeepSeek-V3 run exactly the pre-norm RMSNorm skeleton above (DeepSeek-V3 with mixture-of-experts (MoE) MLPs and its own extra per-expert scaling); Gemma models add the sandwich (post-branch) norms; Qwen3 and OLMo 2 add QK-norm. BatchNorm, meanwhile, remains alive and well in convolutional neural network (CNN) vision backbones and in production CNN-era systems — it lost the sequence-modeling war, not the vision one.

🕰History

The arc in one paragraph: 2015 — BatchNorm (Ioffe & Szegedy) makes deep vision nets train fast and becomes ResNet’s constant companion. 2016 — LayerNorm (Ba, Kiros & Hinton) removes the batch dependence so normalization can serve recurrent neural networks (RNNs), then transformers. 2017 — the original transformer ships post-norm LayerNorm and a mandatory warmup nobody fully understood yet. 2018 — Santurkar et al. dismantle the “internal covariate shift” explanation; Wu & He’s GroupNorm handles small batches in vision. 2019 — GPT-2 quietly moves the norm to pre-position; RMSNorm (Zhang & Sennrich) halves the work. 2020 — Xiong et al. explain pre-norm’s advantage formally. 2023 — ViT-22B popularizes QK-norm after logit-explosion divergences at scale; Llama’s pre-norm RMSNorm recipe becomes the open-weights standard. 2024–25 — QK-norm spreads (Gemma 3, Qwen3, OLMo 2); DyT shows norms can in principle be replaced by a pointwise tanh. The through-line: each step removed a dependence (on the batch, on the mean, on the placement, on the statistics themselves) while keeping the one thing that mattered — bounded, stable scale.

Where this leaves us

  • You can explain why normalization exists: initialization sets activation scales correctly once, training drifts them, and depth compounds the drift geometrically — so modern networks re-standardize activations inside the forward pass, a constraint the optimizer never has to fight.
  • You can run BatchNorm by hand: batch mean, batch variance, standardize, then learnable γ,β\gamma, \betaγ,β — and you can say precisely why the learnable scale/shift restore expressiveness without restoring the old tangled dynamics, and why running statistics (and the train/eval switch) exist.
  • You can articulate why BatchNorm fails for LLMs — noisy small-batch statistics, position-mixing in sequences, sync costs in distributed training, cross-example leakage — and how LayerNorm fixes all four by normalizing across one token’s features instead.
  • You can compute LayerNorm and RMSNorm on the same vector and explain their geometric meaning: both pin the activation to the sphere of radius d\sqrt{d}d​; LayerNorm additionally removes the mean component, which experiments say buys nothing — so the field kept the cheaper one.
  • You can draw pre-norm vs post-norm from memory, give the gradient-path argument for why post-norm demanded warmup and pre-norm scales to hundreds of layers, and place every norm in a modern decoder block — including QK-norm inside attention and the final norm before the unembedding.
  • You know the honest edges: why normalization helps is still partly open (the smoothness account beats internal covariate shift), pre-norm trades a growing stream for its stability, and norm-free training is possible but nobody at the frontier is buying.

The normalizer standardizes what each sublayer sees; the very next thing the signal meets is the nonlinearity that gives the network its expressive power — and that function has its own three-act history. Next: Activation Functions.
