Open vs Closed Models

What "open" really means for a model, what the licenses actually permit, how fast the capability gap closed, and how to decide what to build on.

Every serious decision about building with AI eventually runs into the same fork: do you call someone else’s model over an application programming interface (API), or do you download weights and run them yourself? The question sounds logistical. It is not. It determines what your costs look like at scale, whether your data ever leaves your infrastructure, whether you can fine-tune at all, whether researchers can study the model’s internals — and, at the level of nations and labs, who sets the standards for the most consequential technology of the decade.

The debate around it is also unusually noisy. “Open source” gets used for models that are nothing of the sort; capability comparisons get quoted from whichever month favors the speaker; and the safety argument is usually presented as a cartoon of whichever side the speaker dislikes. This chapter’s job is to be the calm version: define the terms precisely, read the actual licenses, measure the capability gap honestly, explain each lab’s strategy as the rational move it is, steel-man both sides of the safety debate, and end with practical guidance for choosing.

One framing to carry throughout: “open vs closed” is not a binary. It is a ladder, and almost everything interesting happens on the middle rungs.

What “open” actually means

A modern model is not one artifact. Releasing one involves at least four separable things:

  1. Weights — the trained parameters, the multi-hundred-gigabyte tensor files. With weights you can run the model, fine-tune it, quantize it, and inspect its activations.
  2. Code — the training and inference implementations.
  3. Data — the pretraining and post-training corpora, or at least a precise description of them.
  4. The recipe — hyperparameters, data mixture schedules, the post-training pipeline, evaluation harnesses: the knowledge needed to reproduce the model, not merely run it.

Different releases open different subsets, which gives a ladder rather than a switch:

The rungs, precisely:

Closed API. You send tokens, you receive tokens. GPT-5, Claude, and Gemini live here. You never see weights, activations, or (usually) even the exact model version’s lifespan — the provider can silently update or retire what you build on. In exchange you get the frontier: the strongest capabilities, maintained infrastructure, and someone else’s graphics processing units (GPUs).

Open-weight. The parameters are downloadable, so you can serve, quantize, fine-tune, and inspect the model — but the training data and most of the recipe stay private, and a license governs what you may do. This rung splits in two, and the split matters commercially: restricted open-weight releases (Llama’s community license, Mistral’s research license) attach real conditions, while permissive releases (MIT, Apache 2.0) attach almost none.

Open source proper. Weights and code and data and recipe, under licenses meeting the classic open-source freedoms: use, study, modify, redistribute, for anyone, for any purpose. Very few capable models live here — the Allen Institute for AI’s OLMo line and EleutherAI’s Pythia suite are the standard examples, built explicitly so that researchers can study training itself, checkpoint by checkpoint.

⚠️Careful

“Open source” is a contested term for models, and most uses of it are wrong. The Open Source Initiative (OSI) — the traditional steward of the term for software — published its Open Source AI Definition in October 2024. To qualify, a release must grant the four freedoms and include the code, the parameters, and sufficiently detailed data information to allow a skilled person to substantially recreate the model. Llama fails (license restrictions); DeepSeek and Qwen release weights under open licenses but not their data or full recipe, so they are best called open-weight, not open source. The definition itself is contested from the other side too: critics argue that by requiring only “data information” rather than the data, OSI set the bar too low. When you read “open source model” in the wild, translate it to “open-weight” until proven otherwise — this book uses the narrower terms deliberately.

Why does anyone withhold the data while releasing the weights? Three honest reasons: the pretraining mixture is a competitive edge that took enormous effort to curate; the corpus almost certainly contains copyrighted material that is legally safer to describe than to redistribute; and the post-training data encodes the lab’s alignment choices. The weights are the product; the data is the process.

The license fine print

Licenses are where “open” stops being a vibe and becomes a contract. The table below is the practical landscape; treat model placements as a mid-2026 snapshot, because relicensing happens (DeepSeek, for instance, moved its releases to plain MIT in early 2025).

Three practical readings of this table. First, MIT and Apache 2.0 mean what they always meant in software: build a product, charge money, keep your modifications private, no permission needed. This is why DeepSeek’s and Qwen’s choices were strategically loud — they handed the world frontier-adjacent models with fewer restrictions than Meta’s.

Second, the Llama community license is business-friendly for almost everyone but is not open source. The famous 700-million-monthly-active-users clause exists to stop exactly the handful of Meta-scale competitors (Google, Amazon, Apple, ByteDance) from free-riding; a startup will never hit it. The clauses that bite in practice are subtler: the acceptable-use policy, the attribution requirement, and the naming rule for derivatives (the name must begin with “Llama”) — which is why you see fine-tunes with names like “Llama-3.1-Nemotron” rather than fully rebranded models.

Third, the sharpest legal line in the ecosystem is about outputs, not weights. Closed providers’ terms of service generally prohibit using their outputs to train competing models, while permissive open-weight licenses allow distillation freely. We return to why that asymmetry matters in the distillation section.

⚙️In practice

When evaluating a model for commercial use in 2026, engineers check, in order: (1) the exact license file on the exact Hugging Face repo — not the blog post; (2) whether the license permits hosting the model for third parties (some research licenses forbid offering it as a service); (3) whether derivative naming or attribution requirements clash with the product; (4) for anything touching the EU, whether the EU AI Act’s general-purpose-AI obligations apply — the Act partially exempts models under free and open-source licenses, unless they cross the systemic-risk compute threshold (set at 102510^{25}1025 floating-point operations of training compute). Legal review of a model license is now a standard step in enterprise procurement, like reviewing a database license was in 2010.

The capability gap, measured honestly

In 2023, the question “open or closed?” answered itself for demanding applications: closed, because nothing open came close. The interesting story is how fast that stopped being true — and in exactly which ways it is still true.

The honest metric is lag: how many months pass before an open-weight model matches a given closed capability level. Benchmark scores themselves are gameable and saturate (see Benchmarks and How to Read Them), but lag is robust to that, since both sides are measured on the same yardstick.

The trajectory in words. GPT-4 shipped in March 2023 (see The Frontier Landscape for the full timeline). The best open-weight models of that year — Llama 2 in July, Mistral 7B in September — were useful but unmistakably a tier below. Open-weight models did not credibly reach GPT-4’s level until roughly mid-2024, with Llama 3.1 405B: a lag around 12–16 months, depending on the benchmark you pick.

Then the lag collapsed. OpenAI’s o1 introduced reasoning-trained models in September 2024; DeepSeek-R1 matched much of that capability in January 2025 — about four months — with MIT-licensed weights and a paper explaining the method. R1 is the inflection point of this whole chapter: it demonstrated that the reasoning-training recipe was reproducible outside the closed labs, at a cost that startled everyone (the underlying DeepSeek-V3 base model reported about 2.79M H800 GPU-hours of pretraining compute, roughly $5.6M at $2 per GPU-hour — a figure that excludes research, ablations, staff, and infrastructure, and was widely misquoted as the total cost of the project). Through 2025 and into 2026, dense waves of strong permissive releases — Qwen3, further DeepSeek checkpoints, OpenAI’s own gpt-oss — kept the measured lag at months, not years. (These lag figures are fast-moving; treat them as a mid-2026 snapshot.)

💡Intuition

Why does the gap keep closing? Because the ingredients of a frontier model diffuse at different speeds. Ideas — architectures, training recipes — travel at the speed of papers and job changes: months. Outputs leak by default, through distillation and through frontier-generated text landing on the public web. Only compute stays scarce, and compute buys the leader time rather than a permanent lead: it keeps moving the frontier outward, but the cost of reaching any fixed capability level falls relentlessly (see Scaling Laws). A closed lab’s moat is a moving frontier, not a wall.

Honesty requires the other half of the picture. Where closed still leads: the absolute frontier of reasoning — the hardest math, agentic software-engineering, and research-grade tasks — where the newest closed models hold a real edge for some months after each release; multimodal polish, especially natively integrated vision, audio, and image generation; and reliability as a product — uptime, safety tuning, long-tail robustness, and ecosystem tooling that a raw checkpoint does not include. Where open wins: cost at scale (next box), privacy and data control (the weights run inside your perimeter — decisive for hospitals, law firms, defense, and anyone with regulator problems), unrestricted fine-tuning on proprietary data, latency control and offline deployment, and — the reason Part VII of this book can exist at all — research access. You cannot attach a probe to an API. Every sparse autoencoder, every activation-patching experiment, every open replication in interpretability runs on models whose weights someone released.

✏️Worked example

The self-hosting cost curve, with arithmetic. Suppose your product generates 1 billion output tokens per month. (All numbers below are rough mid-2026 magnitudes — recompute with current prices before deciding anything.)

The rule of thumb this arithmetic produces: self-hosting wins at high, steady volume or under hard privacy constraints; APIs win for spiky, low, or exploratory workloads. The crossover moves every year as both GPU rental and API prices fall.

Why labs choose what they choose

None of the major release strategies is ideological accident; each is a rational move given the lab’s position. Understanding the incentives makes the landscape predictable.

Meta opened to commoditize its complements. Meta’s business is attention and advertising, not selling model access. If frontier-quality models become free commodities, the paid-API business of its competitors shrinks while Meta’s products get the capability anyway — the classic “commoditize your complement” strategy, plus real recruiting and ecosystem benefits: Llama became the substrate of academic and startup work, so the world’s tooling, optimizations, and talent pipeline organized around Meta’s architecture. Mark Zuckerberg made the strategic case explicitly in his July 2024 essay “Open Source AI Is the Path Forward.” (A wrinkle worth flagging: after Meta’s 2025 superintelligence-lab reorganization, its leadership signaled more caution about opening its most capable future models — how far that shift goes is unsettled as of this writing.)

DeepSeek and Qwen opened for standards, talent, and reach. For a Chinese lab, open weights convert export-control disadvantage into influence: a model anyone on Earth can download and self-host spreads faster than any API, especially into markets and institutions that cannot or will not buy American API access. Open releases with strong papers also buy scientific credibility and recruiting power, and — for Alibaba’s Qwen — seed a cloud ecosystem the way Llama seeds Meta’s. The permissive licensing (MIT, Apache) is part of the point: maximal adoption is the strategy.

OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind stay mostly closed for two reasons that reinforce each other. The business reason: frontier training runs cost billions, and selling access to the resulting model is how that capital is recouped; giving away the weights gives away the margin. The stated safety reason: releasing weights is irreversible, strips off every deployment-time safeguard, and — the labs argue — becomes dangerous as capabilities approach thresholds for serious misuse. Skeptics note how conveniently the safety argument aligns with the business one; the labs reply that both can be true at once. Both observations are correct.

gpt-oss is the interesting middle move. In August 2025, OpenAI — the company whose name had become the punchline about closed models — released gpt-oss-120b and gpt-oss-20b: Apache-2.0 mixture-of-experts reasoning models (roughly 117B and 21B total parameters, with about 5.1B and 3.6B active per token), its first open-weight language models since GPT-2 in 2019. The logic: concede the commodity tier, where DeepSeek and Qwen were setting standards anyway, and compete there for developer mindshare — while keeping the actual frontier closed. It is the clearest demonstration that “open vs closed” is a portfolio decision per capability tier, not a lab-level identity.

One careful paragraph on the geopolitics, because it now shapes the release calendar. Since 2025, the strongest permissive open-weight models have come predominantly from Chinese labs — DeepSeek, Qwen, and a second rank including Moonshot, Zhipu, and MiniMax — while US labs keep their best models closed, with gpt-oss as the partial exception. This inverts the intuitive framing: US export controls restrict the chips, so Chinese labs compete on diffusion — being the weights the world builds on. In Washington this produced a genuine policy tension, between fear of accelerating a rival and fear that the global default stack becomes Chinese; the US National Telecommunications and Information Administration’s (NTIA) 2024 report on open-weight models landed on monitoring rather than restriction, and that has broadly held. These dynamics are fast-moving; treat any specific claim here as a snapshot, not a law of nature.

🕰History

The open-weight era has a precise, slightly absurd origin story. OpenAI’s GPT-2 (2019) was initially released in stages out of misuse concern — the first mainstream “too dangerous to release” debate. EleutherAI, BigScience (BLOOM, 2022), and Meta’s OPT (2022) kept open replication alive through the GPT-3 years. Then in February 2023 Meta granted researchers gated access to LLaMA — and within a week the weights were on BitTorrent via 4chan. The leak demonstrated overwhelming demand and an ecosystem (llama.cpp, low-rank adaptation (LoRA) fine-tunes on laptops) that Meta chose to embrace rather than fight: Llama 2 shipped in July 2023 with a commercial license, and the modern open-weight ecosystem dates from that decision.

The safety debate, steel-manned

Whether powerful model weights should be released is the most consequential open question in AI policy, and this book will not pretend it has the answer. What it can do is present each side’s strongest form — not the strawman the other side prefers to quote.

✎Note

The case for restraint, at full strength. (1) Irreversibility. An API can be patched, monitored, rate-limited, or shut down; released weights are on ten thousand hard drives forever. Whatever the worst use of a model is, it is now permanent. (2) Safeguard removal. Deployment-time safety training is shallow relative to capability: published work — e.g. Qi et al., 2023 — shows that a small amount of fine-tuning, sometimes a few dozen examples, strips refusal behavior from an open model at trivial cost. Open-weight safety mitigations are therefore best understood as defaults, not guarantees. (3) Dual-use uplift. The concern is not today’s models writing phishing emails; it is tomorrow’s models providing meaningful uplift for biological or cyber weapons development. If capability trends continue, some future release crosses a line — and because of (1), the release decision must be made before the danger is demonstrated, under uncertainty, with no undo. (4) Evaluation gaps. We cannot currently prove a model lacks a dangerous capability; benchmarks miss what they do not test. Restraint buys time for evaluation science to catch up.

✎Note

The case for openness, at full strength. (1) Concentration of power. If frontier AI stays closed, a handful of corporations control the most important cognitive infrastructure in history — its values, its pricing, its availability, and the surveillance of everything queried through it. That is a certain harm, weighed against speculative ones. (2) Auditability. Closed models cannot be independently verified for safety, bias, or backdoors; every claim reduces to “trust the vendor.” Meaningful external safety research — including the entire field of Part VII — requires weights. (3) Defense via access. Defenders, red-teamers, and safety researchers get the same access attackers do; historically (cryptography is the standard analogy) open scrutiny produced more security than obscurity. (4) Marginal risk. The right question — argued carefully in Kapoor et al., 2024 — is not “can open models do bad things?” but “what harm is possible with the open model that was not already possible without it — given search engines, textbooks, and closed models with imperfect guardrails?” For documented harms to date, that measured marginal risk has been modest. (5) Diffusion of benefits. Open weights are how capability reaches hospitals, poor countries, and researchers without frontier budgets.

Notice that the two cases largely talk past each other: restraint arguments are about tail risks of future models; openness arguments are about present, systemic harms of concentration. Both can be right, which is why serious policy work — the NTIA process, the EU AI Act’s general-purpose-AI tiers, labs’ responsible scaling policies — converges on capability-conditional positions: openness by default, flipping at defined evaluation thresholds. Where those thresholds should sit is the live debate, and this book flags it as unresolved.

Distillation: learning from the frontier at a discount

One economic force deserves its own section, because it quietly couples the open and closed worlds: distillation — training a smaller or cheaper model to imitate a stronger one.

💡Intuition

A frontier model’s outputs are a compressed export of its training investment. If a strong teacher writes out 800,000 worked solutions, a student model that trains on them inherits much of the behavior without paying for the search — the billions of dollars of pretraining and reinforcement learning that discovered how to produce those solutions. The expensive thing was never the tokens; it was finding the distribution the tokens are drawn from. Distillation lets a follower buy that distribution at the price of inference.

∑The math

Classic distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) trains the student to match the teacher’s full output distribution, rather than only its top answer. For a context xxx, let the teacher’s next-token distribution be p(⋅ ∣ x)p(\cdot\,|\,x)p(⋅∣x) and the student’s be qθ(⋅ ∣ x)q_\theta(\cdot\,|\,x)qθ​(⋅∣x), where θ\thetaθ are the student’s parameters. The loss is the Kullback–Leibler divergence from teacher to student, averaged over contexts:

L(θ)  =  Ex ⁣[ DKL ⁣(p(⋅ ∣ x) ∥ qθ(⋅ ∣ x))]  =  Ex ⁣[ ∑t∈Vp(t ∣ x) log⁡p(t ∣ x)qθ(t ∣ x)],\mathcal{L}(\theta) \;=\; \E_{x}\!\left[\, \KL\!\left(p(\cdot\,|\,x)\,\|\,q_\theta(\cdot\,|\,x)\right) \right] \;=\; \E_{x}\!\left[\, \sum_{t \in V} p(t\,|\,x)\,\log \frac{p(t\,|\,x)}{q_\theta(t\,|\,x)} \right],L(θ)=Ex​[DKL​(p(⋅∣x)∥qθ​(⋅∣x))]=Ex​[t∈V∑​p(t∣x)logqθ​(t∣x)p(t∣x)​],

where VVV is the vocabulary. The soft targets p(t ∣ x)p(t\,|\,x)p(t∣x) carry far more information per example than a single correct token — the teacher’s ranking of alternatives is part of the signal. When only API access to the teacher exists, its full distribution is unavailable, so modern LLM “distillation” is usually sequence-level: sample whole outputs y∼p(⋅ ∣ x)y \sim p(\cdot\,|\,x)y∼p(⋅∣x) from the teacher, then run ordinary supervised fine-tuning — cross-entropy on the teacher’s tokens — which approximates the same objective with hard samples instead of soft targets.

Note the asymmetry doing its job: the biggest positive term comes from the token the teacher likes most and the student underweights. Gradient descent on this loss pushes qqq toward [0.7,0.2,0.1][0.7, 0.2, 0.1][0.7,0.2,0.1] — including the teacher’s relative preference between the two wrong answers, which a hard label (“token 1 is correct”) would never convey.

The precedent that made distillation a headline economic issue is in the R1 paper itself: DeepSeek generated 800K samples with R1 (600K reasoning, 200K general) and fine-tuned off-the-shelf Qwen and Llama checkpoints on them. The resulting “R1-Distill” models — spanning 1.5B to 70B parameters — inherited a large share of R1’s reasoning ability with no reinforcement learning at all, and several outperformed much larger non-reasoning models. Anyone with a few thousand dollars of compute could now fine-tune a capable reasoner. That is the distillation economy in one release.

The tension: closed providers’ terms of service (ToS) prohibit exactly this — using their outputs to build competing models — and in early 2025 OpenAI publicly suggested DeepSeek had trained on OpenAI-generated data (DeepSeek did not confirm this; the evidence offered was indirect, and this book takes no position on the specific claim). Structurally, though, ToS restrictions are hard to enforce across borders, hard to prove (output-fingerprint “model DNA” detection is an active research area), and awkward to litigate for companies whose own pretraining corpora are the subject of copyright suits. And every frontier output that lands on the public web becomes next year’s pretraining data for everyone. Frontier capability leaks downward by default; the durable moat is not the last generation’s behavior but the pipeline that produces the next one.

Choosing in practice

Strip away the ideology and the build-vs-buy decision comes down to four factors, roughly in the order teams weigh them in 2026:

Data sensitivity and compliance. If prompts contain health records, privileged legal material, classified data, or anything a regulator cares about, self-hosted open weights (or a single-tenant cloud deployment of a closed model — the expensive middle option) may be the only acceptable architecture. This factor is binary and trumps the others when it applies.

Capability requirements. Be specific about the hardest task in your workload, and test it (see Chapter 46 for how to do that without fooling yourself). If your product needs frontier-grade agentic reasoning, the honest answer in most months is a closed API. If your workload is classification, extraction, summarization, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) over your documents (Chapter 45), or a well-scoped domain task, a fine-tuned open model at 8–70B parameters frequently matches or beats a frontier generalist on your distribution — at a fraction of the cost.

The cost curve. Run the utilization arithmetic from the example box above with your real volumes. High and steady favors self-hosting; spiky and exploratory favors APIs; low volume always favors APIs.

Fine-tunability and control. If your differentiation is a model adapted to proprietary data — or you need guaranteed model stability (no silent version changes), offline operation, or sub-100ms latency at the edge — you need weights.

⚙️In practice

What teams deploy in 2026 is rarely one or the other: the standard pattern is a cascade. A cheap open model (often 4–8B, quantized, sometimes on-device) handles the high-volume easy tier; mid-size fine-tuned open models handle the domain tier; a closed frontier API handles the hard tail and anything agentic — with routing logic deciding per request. Open-weight releases are also permanent price anchors: closed-API price cuts of 2024–2026 repeatedly tracked the arrival of comparable open models. Even teams that never deploy open weights benefit from their existence at the negotiating table.

Where this leaves us

  • “Open source model” is usually a misnomer: the working taxonomy is closed API → open-weight with restrictive license → open-weight permissive → open source proper (weights + code + data + recipe, per the OSI definition), and nearly all famous “open” models are open-weight only.
  • Licenses are the substance: MIT/Apache (DeepSeek, Qwen, gpt-oss, Mistral’s Apache line) permit almost everything; Llama’s community license is business-friendly but conditional; research-only weights and API terms of service forbid the things that matter commercially.
  • The capability lag from closed to open collapsed from roughly 16 months (GPT-4 era) to a few months (R1 era); closed retains the frontier ceiling, multimodal polish, and product reliability, while open wins on cost, privacy, fine-tunability, and research access.
  • Each lab’s stance is strategy, not ideology: commoditize-your-complement (Meta), diffusion-and-standards (DeepSeek, Qwen), recoup-the-frontier (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) — with gpt-oss showing that the real unit of decision is the capability tier, not the lab.
  • The safety debate has two strong cases — irreversibility and safeguard removal versus concentration of power and auditability — and serious policy now conditions openness on evaluated capability thresholds rather than picking a side outright.
  • Distillation transfers frontier behavior downward cheaply (you can now compute the KL objective it approximates), which is why capability leaks from closed to open by default.
  • You can run the build-on-what decision as arithmetic plus four factors: data sensitivity, hardest-task capability, utilization-adjusted cost, and need for control.

The strongest argument for open weights in this book’s own view of the field is the one you are about to spend ten chapters inside: you cannot look into a model you cannot touch. Next: Why Interpretability.
