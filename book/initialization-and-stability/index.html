Initialization and Gradient Pathologies

Why deep networks were untrainable for decades — vanishing and exploding gradients — and the fixes: variance-preserving initialization, gradient clipping, and the structural repairs that finally made depth work.

In this chapter
  1. The multiplication at the heart of the problem
  2. Sigmoid saturation: how the 2000s made it worse
  3. Initialization as variance bookkeeping
  4. Gradient clipping: the seatbelt
  5. The structural fixes that actually solved depth
  6. Modern LLM init recipes
  7. Where this leaves us

There is an uncomfortable fact hiding in the history of deep learning: backpropagation was fully worked out by 1986, the universal approximation theorem said depth-plus-width could represent almost anything, and yet for roughly twenty years nobody could reliably train a network more than a few layers deep. Add layers and training would fail outright, with the loss sitting motionless or detonating into NaNs (not-a-number values, the floating-point error state). The algorithms were correct. The math of backpropagation was correct. Something else was wrong.

That something is the subject of this chapter, and it is mundane: repeated multiplication. A deep network is a chain of transformations, and the gradient signal that trains layer 1 must travel backward through every layer above it, getting multiplied by something at each step. Multiply fifty numbers together and the result is almost never “about 1” — it is almost always astronomically large or indistinguishable from zero. Networks were untrainable for decades because everyone was, without realizing it, asking a product of fifty matrices to behave politely, while initializing those matrices with no regard for what the product would do.

The fixes came in three waves. First, initialization done right: choose the starting weights so that signal variance is preserved layer to layer — a small amount of bookkeeping (Glorot 2010, He 2015) that suddenly made 20–30 layer networks trainable. Second, gradient clipping: a seatbelt that caps the size of any single update so a rare explosion cannot destroy the run. Third — and this is the one that conquered depth — structural changes to the network itself: residual connections and normalization layers, which get their own chapters. By the end of this one you will be able to compute exactly why a 50-layer sigmoid network from 2005 could not learn, and read a modern large language model’s (LLM’s) initialization recipe (down to GPT-2’s odd little 1/2N1/\sqrt{2N}1/2N​ factor) as the solution to a problem you can state precisely.

The multiplication at the heart of the problem

Recall from the backpropagation chapter how the gradient reaches an early layer. The loss depends on the output of layer 50, which depends on layer 49, and so on down. The chain rule turns that dependency chain into a product: the gradient at layer 1 is the gradient at the output, multiplied by the Jacobian of layer 50, then the Jacobian of layer 49, and so on — one Jacobian per layer crossed.

Each Jacobian answers the question “if this layer’s input wiggles a little, how much does its output wiggle?” It is a local amplification factor. And here is the whole problem in one sentence: the early layers’ gradient is the product of many amplification factors, and long products of numbers that are not exactly 1 diverge exponentially.

💡Intuition

Picture a rumor passed through fifty people, where each person either slightly exaggerates (multiplies by 1.1) or slightly downplays (multiplies by 0.9). After fifty retellings the rumor is either a hundred times the original or a faint whisper — there is no stable middle unless every teller repeats it exactly. The backward pass is this game of telephone played with gradients, and the “tellers” are the layers. Initialization is our one chance to make every teller start out honest.

Let’s put real numbers on it, because the numbers are the point.

✏️Worked example

Suppose each of 50 layers scales the backward signal by a modest, harmless looking factor — nothing dramatic, only 10% off from neutral.

Each layer shrinks by 0.9: the gradient reaching layer 1 is scaled by

0.950≈0.00515.0.9^{50} \approx 0.00515.0.950≈0.00515.

The first layer receives about half a percent of the gradient the last layer gets. With the same learning rate, it learns 200 times slower. At 100 layers, 0.9100≈2.7×10−50.9^{100} \approx 2.7 \times 10^{-5}0.9100≈2.7×10−5: layer 1 is, for all practical purposes, frozen.

Each layer grows by 1.1: the scale factor is

1.150≈117.1.1^{50} \approx 117.1.150≈117.

A hundredfold amplification — and at 100 layers, 1.1100≈13,7801.1^{100} \approx 13{,}7801.1100≈13,780. One step with that gradient throws the early weights into a regime where activations saturate or overflow, which typically corrupts every later step. The run does not degrade; it dies.

The gap between the two scenarios — 0.90.90.9 versus 1.11.11.1 per layer, a 22% local difference — becomes a factor of roughly 23,000 at depth 50. That is the knife edge deep networks live on: the per-layer gain must be almost exactly 1, because any consistent deviation compounds exponentially with depth.

The scalar story is the honest heart of it, but real layers multiply the gradient by matrices, not numbers. The conclusion survives the upgrade.

∑The math

Consider a network of LLL layers, where layer ℓ\ellℓ computes hℓ=f(Wℓ hℓ−1)h_\ell = f(W_\ell\, h_{\ell-1})hℓ​=f(Wℓ​hℓ−1​) for a weight matrix WℓW_\ellWℓ​ and an elementwise activation fff. Backpropagation gives the gradient with respect to an early activation as a product of per-layer Jacobians:

∂L∂h0=∂L∂hL  JL JL−1⋯J1,Jℓ=Dℓ Wℓ,\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h_0} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h_L} \; J_L\, J_{L-1} \cdots J_1, \qquad J_\ell = D_\ell\, W_\ell,∂h0​∂L​=∂hL​∂L​JL​JL−1​⋯J1​,Jℓ​=Dℓ​Wℓ​,

where DℓD_\ellDℓ​ is a diagonal matrix holding the activation’s slopes f′(⋅)f'(\cdot)f′(⋅) at each unit — this is exactly the structure we built in the chapter on Jacobians. Taking norms and using the fact that a matrix can stretch a vector by at most its largest singular value σmax⁡\sigma_{\max}σmax​ (from matrices as transformations):

∥∂L∂h0∥  ≤  ∥∂L∂hL∥∏ℓ=1Lσmax⁡(Wℓ) max⁡i∣f′∣.\left\| \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h_0} \right\| \;\le\; \left\| \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h_L} \right\| \prod_{\ell=1}^{L} \sigma_{\max}(W_\ell)\,\max_i |f'|.​∂h0​∂L​​≤​∂hL​∂L​​ℓ=1∏L​σmax​(Wℓ​)imax​∣f′∣.

If every layer’s effective gain σmax⁡(Wℓ)⋅max⁡∣f′∣\sigma_{\max}(W_\ell) \cdot \max|f'|σmax​(Wℓ​)⋅max∣f′∣ sits below some γ<1\gamma < 1γ<1, the early gradient is bounded by γL\gamma^LγL times the late gradient — exponential vanishing. A matching lower-bound argument with the smallest singular value shows the exploding case. This is the formal version of the telephone game, worked out for recurrent networks by Pascanu, Mikolov & Bengio (2013) — a recurrent net applying the same WWW at every timestep is the purest case, since the product becomes the same matrix multiplied by itself once per timestep, and the largest eigenvalue’s exponential wins outright.

✎Note

The bound above is a worst case, not an equality — matrix products can partially cancel, and directions matter (a gradient aligned with a small singular direction of one layer may hit a large one at the next). But in expectation, with randomly initialized matrices, the norm of the product really does behave like a product of per-layer gains. The scalar intuition is quantitatively trustworthy, which is why the fix below is phrased entirely in terms of per-layer variance.

Sigmoid saturation: how the 2000s made it worse

The networks of the pre-2012 era mostly used the sigmoid activation σ(x)=1/(1+e−x)\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})σ(x)=1/(1+e−x) — a smooth squash from all of R\RR into (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1), beloved for its probabilistic interpretation. It has a property that, combined with the multiplication story, is quietly catastrophic. Its derivative is

σ′(x)=σ(x) (1−σ(x)),\sigma'(x) = \sigma(x)\,\bigl(1 - \sigma(x)\bigr),σ′(x)=σ(x)(1−σ(x)),

which reaches its maximum where σ(x)=12\sigma(x) = \tfrac12σ(x)=21​, i.e. at x=0x = 0x=0, giving σ′(0)=12⋅12=0.25\sigma'(0) = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \mathbf{0.25}σ′(0)=21​⋅21​=0.25. Read that carefully: the best case slope, achieved only at exactly zero input, is 0.250.250.25. Everywhere else it is smaller, and for inputs beyond about ±4\pm 4±4 it is effectively zero — the curve has gone flat, and we say the unit has saturated.

Now feed this into the depth calculation. Each layer’s backward Jacobian carries a factor of DℓD_\ellDℓ​ — the diagonal of activation slopes — so every layer multiplies the gradient by at most 0.250.250.25 from the activation alone, before the weight matrix even gets a vote.

✏️Worked example

Ten sigmoid layers, each operating at its absolute best (every unit exactly at zero input, slope 0.250.250.25): the activation slopes alone scale the gradient by

0.2510≈9.5×10−70.25^{10} \approx 9.5 \times 10^{-7}0.2510≈9.5×10−7

— under one millionth. At twenty layers, 0.2520≈9×10−130.25^{20} \approx 9 \times 10^{-13}0.2520≈9×10−13. And this is the optimistic case; real units drift into saturation, where the factor is not 0.250.250.25 but 0.010.010.01 or 0.00010.00010.0001. The weight matrices would need per-layer gains of about 4 to compensate — but a gain of 4 through the forward pass drives pre-activations to huge values, which saturates the sigmoids, which kills the slopes even harder. The architecture is trapped: sigmoid depth cannot win.

🕰History

The vanishing gradient problem was diagnosed early: Sepp Hochreiter’s 1991 diploma thesis (in German, supervised by Jürgen Schmidhuber) identified exactly this exponential decay in recurrent networks, and the LSTM (long short-term memory) architecture of 1997 was engineered around it — its cell state is an additive path along which the “multiply at every step” rule is suspended. If you learned LSTMs in 2017: the forget-gate-near-1 trick and the residual connections of modern transformers are the same idea — create a path where gradients add instead of multiply. We tell that story in CNNs and RNNs and residual connections. The feedforward version of the diagnosis, and the initialization fix, came with Glorot & Bengio (2010); the switch to ReLU (rectified linear unit, max⁡(0,x)\max(0, x)max(0,x)) removed the 0.250.250.25 ceiling entirely, since ReLU’s slope is exactly 1 wherever it is active — a story continued in activation functions.

⚠️Careful

Saturation is not only an initialization-time problem. A network initialized perfectly can drift into saturation mid-training — one oversized update pushes a layer’s pre-activations to ±20\pm 20±20, the local slopes collapse, and that layer stops receiving gradient and cannot recover: the units are stuck reporting 0 or 1 with no slope to climb back. This “dead layer” failure is one reason the fixes later in this chapter (clipping, normalization) matter even after the starting point is right.

Initialization as variance bookkeeping

So the requirement is: every layer’s gain must be almost exactly 1, forward and backward. We cannot set the weights to make this true exactly — they are random, and they will change during training — but we can make it true on average, in the statistical sense. This turns initialization from folklore (“small random numbers”) into an actual calculation: track the variance of the signal as it passes through a layer, and choose the weight variance that leaves it unchanged.

You have the tool for this from the probability chapter: variance of a sum of independent things is the sum of their variances. Here is the whole derivation, walked slowly.

∑The math

One output unit of a linear layer computes a weighted sum over its nnn inputs (this nnn is called the fan-in — the number of connections coming in):

y=∑i=1nwi xi.y = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i\, x_i .y=i=1∑n​wi​xi​.

Assume the weights wiw_iwi​ are drawn independently with mean zero and variance Var⁡(w)\Var(w)Var(w), the inputs xix_ixi​ have mean zero and variance Var⁡(x)\Var(x)Var(x), and weights are independent of inputs. Take one term wixiw_i x_iwi​xi​. Its mean is E[wixi]=E[wi] E[xi]=0\E[w_i x_i] = \E[w_i]\,\E[x_i] = 0E[wi​xi​]=E[wi​]E[xi​]=0 (independence lets the expectation factor). Its variance is therefore its mean square:

Var⁡(wixi)=E ⁣[wi2xi2]=E ⁣[wi2]E ⁣[xi2]=Var⁡(w) Var⁡(x),\Var(w_i x_i) = \E\!\left[w_i^2 x_i^2\right] = \E\!\left[w_i^2\right]\E\!\left[x_i^2\right] = \Var(w)\,\Var(x),Var(wi​xi​)=E[wi2​xi2​]=E[wi2​]E[xi2​]=Var(w)Var(x),

again using independence to factor, and using mean-zero to identify E[w2]\E[w^2]E[w2] with Var⁡(w)\Var(w)Var(w). The nnn terms are mutually independent, so their variances add:

  Var⁡(y)=n Var⁡(w) Var⁡(x).  \boxed{\;\Var(y) = n \,\Var(w)\,\Var(x).\;}Var(y)=nVar(w)Var(x).​

Every forward pass through the layer multiplies the signal variance by nVar⁡(w)n\Var(w)nVar(w). Demanding Var⁡(y)=Var⁡(x)\Var(y) = \Var(x)Var(y)=Var(x) — the gain-of-1 condition — gives

Var⁡(w)=1n=1fan-in.\Var(w) = \frac{1}{n} = \frac{1}{\text{fan-in}}.Var(w)=n1​=fan-in1​.

The backward pass runs the same argument through W⊤W\TW⊤, where the relevant count is the fan-out mmm (connections going out of a unit), giving the condition Var⁡(w)=1/fan-out\Var(w) = 1/\text{fan-out}Var(w)=1/fan-out. When n≠mn \ne mn=m we cannot satisfy both, so Glorot & Bengio (2010) split the difference with the harmonic-style compromise now called Xavier (or Glorot) initialization:

Var⁡(w)=2fan-in+fan-out.\Var(w) = \frac{2}{\text{fan-in} + \text{fan-out}}.Var(w)=fan-in+fan-out2​.

Draw from a Gaussian with that variance, or from a uniform distribution on ±6/(fan-in+fan-out)\pm\sqrt{6/(\text{fan-in}+\text{fan-out})}±6/(fan-in+fan-out)​, which has the same variance (recall a uniform on ±a\pm a±a has variance a2/3a^2/3a2/3).

✏️Worked example

Concrete numbers, small enough to check by hand. A layer with fan-in n=100n = 100n=100, inputs with Var⁡(x)=1\Var(x) = 1Var(x)=1.

Careless init, std 0.5 (so Var⁡(w)=0.25\Var(w) = 0.25Var(w)=0.25): Var⁡(y)=100×0.25×1=25\Var(y) = 100 \times 0.25 \times 1 = 25Var(y)=100×0.25×1=25, so the outputs have standard deviation 25=5\sqrt{25} = 525​=5. One layer quintupled the signal. Stack 50 such layers and the variance gain per layer compounds exactly like the toy example — with a per-layer std gain of 1.6, say, you get 1.650≈1.6×10101.6^{50} \approx 1.6 \times 10^{10}1.650≈1.6×1010. And with a squashing activation it is worse in a different way: a pre-activation of typical size 5 lands deep in the sigmoid’s rose-shaded saturation zone.

Careless init, std 0.01 (so Var⁡(w)=10−4\Var(w) = 10^{-4}Var(w)=10−4): Var⁡(y)=100×10−4=0.01\Var(y) = 100 \times 10^{-4} = 0.01Var(y)=100×10−4=0.01, std 0.10.10.1: each layer shrinks the signal tenfold. Ten layers in, the signal is 10−1010^{-10}10−10 of its input — numerically indistinguishable from a network computing the constant zero.

Xavier, with fan-out 100 too: Var⁡(w)=2/(100+100)=0.01\Var(w) = 2/(100+100) = 0.01Var(w)=2/(100+100)=0.01, std 0.10.10.1… which looks identical to the “careless small” case — but here fan-in is 100, so Var⁡(y)=100×0.01×1=1\Var(y) = 100 \times 0.01 \times 1 = 1Var(y)=100×0.01×1=1. Gain exactly 1. The point is that “small” is meaningless in isolation; only the product nVar⁡(w)n\Var(w)nVar(w) matters, so the right std depends on the layer’s width. That width-dependence is the entire content of good initialization.

The ReLU correction: He initialization

Xavier’s derivation treated the layer as linear. A ReLU between layers breaks one of its assumptions in a precisely fixable way: ReLU sets every negative input to zero, and that halves the variance.

∑The math

Let zzz be a pre-activation that is symmetrically distributed around zero (true at init, where zzz is a zero-mean weighted sum). Write a=ReLU(z)=max⁡(0,z)a = \mathrm{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z)a=ReLU(z)=max(0,z). Then

E ⁣[a2]=E ⁣[z2⋅1[z>0]]=12 E ⁣[z2],\E\!\left[a^2\right] = \E\!\left[z^2 \cdot \mathbf{1}[z > 0]\right] = \tfrac12\, \E\!\left[z^2\right],E[a2]=E[z2⋅1[z>0]]=21​E[z2],

because by symmetry the positive half of zzz‘s distribution carries exactly half of E[z2]\E[z^2]E[z2] — the negative half, which ReLU zeroes out, carried the other half. So each ReLU layer multiplies the propagating second moment by 12\tfrac1221​, on top of the weight layer’s nVar⁡(w)n\Var(w)nVar(w). The gain-of-1 condition becomes 12 nVar⁡(w)=1\tfrac12\, n \Var(w) = 121​nVar(w)=1, i.e.

Var⁡(w)=2fan-in,\Var(w) = \frac{2}{\text{fan-in}},Var(w)=fan-in2​,

which is He initialization (He et al., 2015) — Xavier’s rule with a factor of 2 to pay for the half that ReLU throws away. (A subtlety for the careful reader: aaa is not mean-zero, so we track second moments E[a2]\E[a^2]E[a2] rather than variances through the stack; the recursion works out the same, which is exactly the route He et al. take in their Section 2.2.)

For a transformer-scale layer with fan-in 256: Xavier with equal fan-out gives std 2/512=0.0625\sqrt{2/512} = 0.06252/512​=0.0625; He gives std 2/256≈0.0884\sqrt{2/256} \approx 0.08842/256​≈0.0884 — a 41% (2\sqrt22​) larger scale. At depth 50, using Xavier where He was needed means the weight layer’s gain nVar⁡(w)=1n\Var(w) = 1nVar(w)=1 gets multiplied by ReLU’s 12\tfrac1221​: every layer halves the signal variance, and 0.550≈10−150.5^{50} \approx 10^{-15}0.550≈10−15. The factor of 2 is not a nicety.

⚙️In practice

In 2026 the descendants of these rules are in every framework’s defaults: PyTorch’s nn.Linear initializes with a Kaiming-style (He) uniform rule based on fan-in, and kaiming_normal_ / xavier_normal_ are the explicit options. For ReLU-family activations (including GELU, the Gaussian error linear unit, and SiLU/Swish, the sigmoid linear unit — the ones used in modern transformers) He-style fan-in scaling is the standard choice; Xavier survives mainly for tanh networks and some attention projections. But — a preview of the last section — frontier LLMs mostly use a fixed small std (GPT-2’s 0.02 became a convention) combined with normalization layers that make the network far more forgiving of the exact constant, plus a depth-dependent correction on residual branches. The variance-bookkeeping logic is still how every one of those recipes is derived and debugged.

The symmetry trap: why all-zeros (and all-anything) fails

Given all this fuss about the right scale, you might ask: why not the maximally humble choice, all weights zero? Gain problems solved — nothing explodes, nothing vanishes (well, everything vanishes, but at least predictably). The failure here is different in kind, and it teaches something important about why randomness itself is a load-bearing ingredient.

Suppose every weight in a layer is initialized to the same constant ccc (zero or otherwise). Then every hidden unit in that layer computes the same function of the input: same weights, same bias, same activation — identical outputs. Now run backpropagation. Each unit’s gradient depends on its output, its local slope, and the downstream weights it feeds — all of which are identical across the units. So every unit receives an identical gradient, takes an identical update, and remains identical to its siblings. By induction, this holds at every step: the units were born clones and gradient descent, being a deterministic function of the situation, can never tell them apart. A layer of 4,096 neurons behaves — forever — as one neuron photocopied 4,096 times. The network’s effective capacity collapses to a single unit per layer.

💡Intuition

Gradient descent can amplify differences between neurons — each unit drifts toward whichever feature it was accidentally slightly better at detecting — but it cannot create a difference from nothing. Random initialization is the symmetry-breaking seed: it hands every neuron a different random “opinion,” and training grows those arbitrary differences into a division of labor. No randomness, no specialization. (Biases, by contrast, are almost always initialized to exactly zero — the random weights have already broken the symmetry, so the biases have no tie to break.)

Gradient clipping: the seatbelt

Correct initialization sets the average gain to 1, but training is a stochastic process: a rare batch, a bad interaction between layers, a moment of near-saturation somewhere — and occasionally the backward product spikes anyway. A single enormous gradient can undo days of training in one step, by teleporting the weights into a bad region (often followed by a cascade: bad weights → weirder activations → worse gradients). The standard seatbelt is global-norm gradient clipping, popularized for recurrent nets by Pascanu et al. (2013).

∑The math

Collect every parameter’s gradient into one giant vector ggg (concatenating across all layers) and measure its overall length ∥g∥2\|g\|_2∥g∥2​ — the square root of the sum of squares of every individual gradient entry in the whole model. Choose a threshold τ\tauτ. Then:

g  ←  g⋅min⁡ ⁣(1,  τ∥g∥2).g \;\leftarrow\; g \cdot \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{\tau}{\|g\|_2}\right).g←g⋅min(1,∥g∥2​τ​).

If the global norm is under τ\tauτ, nothing happens — the min is 1 and the gradient passes through untouched. If it exceeds τ\tauτ, the entire vector is rescaled to have norm exactly τ\tauτ. The direction is preserved: every parameter’s gradient shrinks by the same factor, so the update still points the same way in parameter space, only shorter. (This is why global-norm clipping is preferred over clipping each entry independently to ±τ\pm\tau±τ, which changes the direction — entries at the cap get flattened relative to small ones.)

What do spikes look like in practice? In a healthy large-model run the global gradient norm hovers in a fairly steady band — say drifting between 0.2 and 0.6 for a well-tuned LLM — and then, out of nowhere, a step reports a norm of 15, or 400. On the loss curve this is the infamous loss spike: a vertical jump in an otherwise smooth descent, sometimes recovering over the next few hundred steps, sometimes not. Clipping converts “sometimes not” into “almost always recovers”: the spike still happens, but the step taken during it is bounded, so the model is not flung far from where it was.

⚙️In practice

The near-universal default is τ=1.0\tau = 1.0τ=1.0 on the global norm — used by GPT-3, the Llama family, and most open training recipes; some runs use 0.5. Every serious training codebase logs the pre-clip gradient norm every step, and it is one of the two or three most-watched health metrics of a frontier run: a rising baseline norm predicts instability before the loss shows anything. Frequent clipping is a symptom, not a treatment — if more than a small percentage of steps are being clipped, the learning rate is too high, the warmup too short, or something is wrong (bad data shard, precision issue). The full menagerie of mid-run spike remedies — skipping bad batches, rewinding to checkpoints, tweaking Adam’s ϵ\epsilonϵ (see optimizers) — lives in the training run.

⚠️Careful

Clipping is anesthetic, not surgery. It caps the damage of an exploding gradient but does nothing about vanishing gradients (you cannot clip a signal upward from zero), and it can mask real pathologies: a run that clips on every step will “train” — the loss goes down — while effectively ignoring the true gradient magnitudes, often converging worse. If your norms are persistently above threshold, fix the cause; do not raise a toast to the seatbelt.

The structural fixes that actually solved depth

Here is an honest accounting: careful initialization got the field from ~5 trainable layers to ~20–30. It did not get us to hundred-layer networks, because variance bookkeeping at step 0 says nothing about step 100,000 — the weights move, the tidy statistical assumptions dissolve, and depth’s exponential still lurks. The barrier fell only when the architecture was changed so that the failure mode is structurally impossible. Three changes, each with its own chapter:

Residual connections (He et al., 2015) replace hℓ+1=f(hℓ)h_{\ell+1} = f(h_\ell)hℓ+1​=f(hℓ​) with hℓ+1=hℓ+f(hℓ)h_{\ell+1} = h_\ell + f(h_\ell)hℓ+1​=hℓ​+f(hℓ​). The Jacobian of that layer is I+JfI + J_fI+Jf​ — identity plus something — so the backward product becomes a product of terms anchored at 1, not centered wherever the weights happen to put them. There is now an unbroken additive path from the loss to layer 1 along which the gradient is never multiplied by anything. This is the LSTM’s cell-state trick, generalized to any network, and it is the single most important structural idea in modern deep learning: residual connections devotes a chapter to it.

Normalization layers (BatchNorm, LayerNorm, RMSNorm) re-standardize activations at every layer, on every forward pass — actively resetting the signal scale that initialization could only set once, at step 0. With normalization in the network, the forward pass cannot drift into saturation territory no matter what the weights have become. How and why (including the surprising subtleties of where to put the norm) is the normalization chapter.

Learning-rate warmup starts training with a tiny learning rate and ramps up over the first few hundred to few thousand steps. Early training is the most fragile phase — the Adam optimizer’s second-moment estimates are still unreliable, and the weights are at their statistically delicate init — so warmup keeps steps small until the network has settled into a stable region. It is a stability tool of the same family as everything in this chapter, and it is covered with the rest of the practical liturgy in the training run.

With residuals and normalization in place, initialization stopped being the difference between “trains” and “does not train.” But at LLM scale it quietly returned as the difference between “trains well” and “trains with spikes” — which brings us to what frontier labs do.

Modern LLM init recipes

A 2026-era transformer is a stack of residual blocks — attention and MLP (multi-layer perceptron) sublayers, each wrapped in a residual connection and a normalization layer. Its initialization recipe descends directly from GPT-2 (Radford et al., 2019) and has three moving parts.

A small fixed std for most weights. GPT-2 initialized weights from a Gaussian with standard deviation 0.02, and the constant stuck as a convention across the GPT lineage and many open models. Note this is a fixed number, not a fan-in formula — the normalization layers absorb much of the sensitivity that Xavier/He were built to handle. Some modern recipes do keep width-aware scaling, and some go smaller: DeepSeek-V3’s report gives an init std of 0.006 (reported figure; conventions vary lab to lab and these details are among the least-documented parts of frontier recipes).

Downscaled residual-branch outputs. Here is the one depth-aware correction that survived into the modern stack. Every residual block adds its output into the running residual stream: h←h+f(h)h \leftarrow h + f(h)h←h+f(h). If each block’s branch output has variance vvv, then after NNN blocks the stream’s variance is roughly Var⁡(h0)+(number of additions)×v\Var(h_0) + (\text{number of additions}) \times vVar(h0​)+(number of additions)×v — variances of (approximately independent) additions accumulate. A transformer block contains two residual additions (attention and MLP), so a depth-NNN model performs 2N2N2N additions, and the stream’s variance grows linearly with depth. GPT-2’s fix: at initialization, scale the weights of each residual branch’s final projection by 1/2N1/\sqrt{2N}1/2N​ — for GPT-2’s 48-layer configuration, 1/96≈0.1021/\sqrt{96} \approx 0.1021/96​≈0.102, i.e. those layers start about 10×10\times10× smaller than the rest. Then each addition contributes variance v/2Nv/2Nv/2N, and the total contribution over 2N2N2N additions is ≈v\approx v≈v: constant in depth, by design. The same trick, in various guises (scaled init, “depth-scaled” init, zero-init of the last projection), appears throughout the open-model world precisely because it targets the accumulation, not the per-layer gain.

✏️Worked example

Feel the difference with numbers. Take N=48N = 48N=48 blocks, each branch output with variance v=1v = 1v=1, stream starting at variance 1.

Without scaling: after 96 additions, stream variance ≈1+96=97\approx 1 + 96 = 97≈1+96=97, std ≈9.8\approx 9.8≈9.8 — the deepest layers see inputs ten times larger than the first layers do, and whatever the normalization layers can rescue, the residual stream itself (and the logits computed from it) has drifted an order of magnitude.

With 1/961/\sqrt{96}1/96​ scaling: each branch’s output variance becomes 1/961/961/96, the 96 additions contribute 96×196=196 \times \tfrac{1}{96} = 196×961​=1, and the stream ends at variance ≈2\approx 2≈2, std ≈1.4\approx 1.4≈1.4. Depth-independent, as promised.

Zero-init where symmetry permits. Biases start at zero everywhere, and several recipes zero-initialize the final projection of each residual branch outright — making every block the identity function at step 0, so the network begins as a shallow model and “grows into” its depth. (Why doesn’t this hit the symmetry trap? Because the other matrices in the block are random: two neurons’ incoming weights already differ, so their gradients through the zeroed projection differ too. Zero is only fatal when it makes units mutually identical, not merely small.)

✎Note

The advanced frontier: μP. There is a modern theory that re-derives all of this — init variances and per-layer learning rates — as functions of network width, called the maximal update parametrization (μP) (Yang, Hu et al., 2022). Its killer feature is hyperparameter transfer: under μP, the optimal learning rate becomes approximately independent of width, so you can tune hyperparameters on a small cheap model and reuse them on the full-size one — this was used for Cerebras-GPT and has been adopted or adapted in several open efforts since, with frontier labs generally quiet about whether their internal recipes use it. Treat this as a signpost rather than a lesson: the one-sentence takeaway is that “how should init and learning rate scale as the model grows?” has a principled answer, and it is not “keep them fixed.” The scaling mindset returns in scaling laws.

⚙️In practice

A representative modern recipe, assembled from open training codebases (GPT-2/GPT-3 lineage, Llama-style models, OLMo, and similar):

  • Most weight matrices: Gaussian, std 0.020.020.02 (or a fan-in-scaled variant); embeddings often the same, sometimes larger.
  • Final projection of each residual branch: same Gaussian further scaled by 1/2N1/\sqrt{2N}1/2N​ for NNN blocks — or zero-initialized.
  • All biases (where the architecture has them at all — many modern LLMs drop biases entirely): zero.
  • RMSNorm gains: one.
  • Global-norm gradient clipping at 1.01.01.0; learning-rate warmup over the first ~0.1–1% of steps.

None of these numbers is sacred and labs differ in the details — but every line item exists to hold one of this chapter’s failure modes at bay, and when a training run misbehaves, this list is where the debugging starts.

Where this leaves us

  • Deep networks fail by repeated multiplication: the early-layer gradient is a product of per-layer Jacobians, and you can now compute the visceral numbers — 0.950≈0.0050.9^{50} \approx 0.0050.950≈0.005, 1.150≈1171.1^{50} \approx 1171.150≈117 — that show why per-layer gain must sit almost exactly at 1.
  • Sigmoid saturation compounds the problem: its derivative is capped at 0.250.250.25 (so ten layers cost a factor of a million even in the best case) and is near zero for saturated units.
  • Initialization is variance bookkeeping: from Var⁡(y)=nVar⁡(w)Var⁡(x)\Var(y) = n\Var(w)\Var(x)Var(y)=nVar(w)Var(x) you can derive Xavier (Var⁡(w)=2/(fan-in+fan-out)\Var(w) = 2/(\text{fan-in}+\text{fan-out})Var(w)=2/(fan-in+fan-out)) and He (2/fan-in2/\text{fan-in}2/fan-in, the 2 paying for the half of the variance ReLU discards).
  • All-equal initialization fails for a different reason — symmetry: identical neurons receive identical gradients and remain clones forever; randomness is what lets neurons specialize.
  • Global-norm gradient clipping (threshold ~1.0) bounds the damage of gradient spikes while preserving update direction — a seatbelt, not a cure.
  • Depth was ultimately conquered structurally: residual connections anchor every layer’s Jacobian at the identity, normalization re-standardizes activations continuously, and warmup protects the fragile early steps.
  • You can read a modern LLM init recipe — fixed small std, GPT-2’s 1/2N1/\sqrt{2N}1/2N​ residual scaling, zeroed biases, clipping at 1.0 — and say exactly which pathology each line prevents.

Stability determines whether a network can train at all; the next question is whether what it learns will hold up on data it has never seen. Next: Regularization and Generalization.
