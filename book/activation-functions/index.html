Activation Functions

From sigmoid to SwiGLU: why networks need nonlinearity, why ReLU conquered deep learning, why GELU replaced it in transformers, and how modern gated FFN blocks actually work — with the parameter arithmetic done by hand.

Somewhere inside every forward pass of every frontier model, billions of times per second, a number arrives at a function so small you could tattoo it on a grain of rice — something like max⁡(0,x)\max(0, x)max(0,x), or x⋅σ(x)x \cdot \sigma(x)x⋅σ(x) — and gets bent. That bending is the entire reason the network is more than a matrix. We saw in Neurons, Layers, and MLPs that stacking linear layers without nonlinearities collapses into a single linear map, no matter how many layers you stack: all that machinery would amount to one matrix multiply. The activation function is the cheap, pointwise act of defiance that prevents the collapse and lets depth mean something.

For such a small component, the field has churned through a long list of candidates: sigmoid, tanh, ReLU, Leaky ReLU, ELU, GELU, Swish, Mish, and a zoo of others. Each transition happened for a reason — a specific failure mode of the previous choice — and those reasons are worth knowing, because they are miniature lessons in how gradients live and die. The sigmoid’s saturation is the cleanest possible illustration of the vanishing-gradient problem from Chapter 12. ReLU’s “dying neuron” failure is the cleanest illustration of what a zero gradient costs you. GELU and SiLU show what “fixing ReLU’s corner” buys.

And then the story takes a turn the activation-zoo framing misses entirely. In modern transformers, the interesting question stopped being “which curve?” and became “how is the curve wired into the block?” The feed-forward network (FFN) inside every transformer layer — roughly two-thirds of the parameters of a typical dense model — was redesigned in 2020 around gating: one linear path decides what content to carry, a second parallel path decides how much of it gets through, and the activation function moved into the gate. That design, SwiGLU, is now as close to universal as anything in deep learning: Llama, PaLM, Mistral, Qwen, and DeepSeek all use it. This chapter covers the whole arc: what nonlinearities do, the tour of the curves (with their derivatives, which matter just as much), and then the modern gated FFN, with every dimension and parameter count worked out by hand.

Why a network needs nonlinearity at all

A quick proof of the claim that opened the chapter, because it takes three lines. Suppose two “layers” are purely linear: h=W1xh = W_1 xh=W1​x and y=W2hy = W_2 hy=W2​h. Then

y=W2(W1x)=(W2W1) x,y = W_2 (W_1 x) = (W_2 W_1)\, x,y=W2​(W1​x)=(W2​W1​)x,

and W2W1W_2 W_1W2​W1​ is itself a single matrix. By induction, any stack of linear layers — a hundred of them, a million — equals one linear layer. Everything we learned about matrices in Chapter 2 applies: the composite can rotate, scale, and shear space, but it must keep straight lines straight, keep the origin fixed, and keep parallel lines parallel. It can never carve out a curved decision boundary, never say “these two far-apart regions belong together,” never compute XOR.

Insert a nonlinear function ϕ\phiϕ between the layers — y=W2 ϕ(W1x)y = W_2\, \phi(W_1 x)y=W2​ϕ(W1​x) — and the collapse fails, because ϕ\phiϕ applied elementwise is not a matrix multiply. Geometrically, each layer now folds or bends space before handing it to the next, and folds compound: with enough layers you can crumple the input space so that any region maps near any other. That is the picture behind the universal approximation results mentioned in Chapter 8, and it is worth restating what the activation’s job is: it is the network’s main source of curvature. Every other operation in a transformer — embeddings, attention’s weighted averaging, projections, residual additions — is linear or built from linear pieces plus softmax (normalization contributes a mild rescaling nonlinearity, Chapter 14). The FFN’s activation is where most of the raw nonlinear capacity lives.

Two design constraints shape everything that follows. First, the function is applied elementwise to millions of numbers per token, so it must be cheap — a couple of arithmetic operations, no loops, vectorizable. Second, we train by backpropagation (Chapter 10), so what matters is not only the function but its derivative: during the backward pass, every gradient flowing through a neuron gets multiplied by ϕ′(z)\phi'(z)ϕ′(z) at the point zzz where that neuron operated. An activation function is really a pair of functions, and half the history of the field is people staring at the derivative plot rather than the function plot. Every figure in this chapter therefore shows both.

The classics: sigmoid and tanh

The 1980s–90s answer was the sigmoid (also called the logistic function), which you met as the binary-classification output in Loss Functions:

σ(z)=11+e−z.\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}.σ(z)=1+e−z1​.

It squashes any real number into (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1): large negative inputs go to 0, large positive inputs go to 1, and around zero it is roughly linear with slope 14\tfrac1441​. Its historical appeal was twofold: it looks like a smooth version of a biological neuron’s firing rate (off, then increasingly on), and it is differentiable everywhere, which the backpropagation era required. Its cousin tanh is the same S-shape rescaled to (−1,1)(-1, 1)(−1,1): tanh⁡(z)=2σ(2z)−1\tanh(z) = 2\sigma(2z) - 1tanh(z)=2σ(2z)−1.

∑The math

The sigmoid has an unusually tidy derivative. Starting from σ(z)=(1+e−z)−1\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}σ(z)=(1+e−z)−1 and applying the chain rule from Chapter 5:

σ′(z)=e−z(1+e−z)2=11+e−z⋅e−z1+e−z=σ(z) (1−σ(z)).\sigma'(z) = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2} = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} \cdot \frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}} = \sigma(z)\,\bigl(1 - \sigma(z)\bigr).σ′(z)=(1+e−z)2e−z​=1+e−z1​⋅1+e−ze−z​=σ(z)(1−σ(z)).

Since σ(z)∈(0,1)\sigma(z) \in (0,1)σ(z)∈(0,1), the product σ(1−σ)\sigma(1-\sigma)σ(1−σ) is maximized when σ(z)=12\sigma(z) = \tfrac12σ(z)=21​, i.e. at z=0z=0z=0, where it equals 14\tfrac1441​. So σ′(z)≤0.25\sigma'(z) \le 0.25σ′(z)≤0.25 everywhere, and it decays exponentially in both tails. For tanh, the same style of computation gives tanh⁡′(z)=1−tanh⁡2(z)\tanh'(z) = 1 - \tanh^2(z)tanh′(z)=1−tanh2(z), which peaks at 111 at the origin — four times better than sigmoid, but with the same exponentially-vanishing tails.

✏️Worked example

Saturation with actual numbers. At z=5z = 5z=5: σ(5)=0.9933\sigma(5) = 0.9933σ(5)=0.9933, so σ′(5)=0.9933×0.0067≈0.0066\sigma'(5) = 0.9933 \times 0.0067 \approx 0.0066σ′(5)=0.9933×0.0067≈0.0066. The neuron is “confident,” and its gradient is 38 times smaller than the best case of 0.250.250.25. Now stack this. In a 10-layer sigmoid network, backprop multiplies one σ′\sigma'σ′ factor per layer, each at most 0.250.250.25. Even in the best case the product is at most 0.2510≈9.5×10−70.25^{10} \approx 9.5 \times 10^{-7}0.2510≈9.5×10−7: the gradient reaching layer 1 is about a million times smaller than the gradient at layer 10, before the weight matrices have their say. With realistic (partly saturated) neurons it is far worse. This is the vanishing-gradient arithmetic of Chapter 12 in its purest form.

Tanh survives longer in the historical record for a second reason: it is zero-centered. A sigmoid neuron’s output is always positive, so every neuron in the next layer receives an all-positive input vector. Recall from Chapter 10 that the gradient of a weight is (upstream gradient) × (input to that weight); if all inputs are positive, then for a given neuron all its weight gradients share the sign of that neuron’s single upstream gradient — the entire weight vector must move in an all-positive or all-negative direction each step, and reaching a target that requires mixed signs takes an inefficient zig-zag. Tanh’s symmetric range removes this constraint, one of several reasons LeCun-era practical wisdom preferred it. If you learned deep learning in the 2017 era, this is also why long short-term memory (LSTM) networks are full of tanh (content) and sigmoid (gates): inside a recurrent cell, bounded outputs were a feature, not a bug — an unbounded activation recurrently applied can explode. Hold that thought about sigmoid-as-gate; it returns triumphantly at the end of this chapter.

⚠️Careful

Sigmoid and tanh are not “bad functions” — they are bad hidden-layer activations for deep stacks. Sigmoid remains exactly the right output layer for binary classification and exactly the right gate inside gated architectures, and tanh persists in odd corners (it appears inside the GELU approximation below). The lesson of this section is narrower than “never use sigmoid”: it is that any activation whose derivative is bounded well below 1 and vanishes in both tails will strangle gradients when composed many times.

ReLU: the function that deep learning was built on

The rectified linear unit (ReLU) could not be simpler:

ReLU(z)=max⁡(0,z)={zz>00z≤0.\mathrm{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z) = \begin{cases} z & z > 0 \\ 0 & z \le 0. \end{cases}ReLU(z)=max(0,z)={z0​z>0z≤0.​

Pass positives through untouched; delete negatives. Its derivative is a step: 111 for z>0z > 0z>0, 000 for z<0z < 0z<0 (at exactly zero the derivative is undefined; software picks 0 or 1, and it never matters in practice because hitting exactly 0.00.00.0 in floating point is measure-zero rare). After Nair and Hinton (2010) and Glorot, Bordes and Bengio (2011) showed it trained deep networks that sigmoids could not, and AlexNet (2012) demonstrated it at scale — its paper reports roughly 6× faster convergence than tanh, on a small CIFAR-10 test network — ReLU became the default for a decade. Three properties explain the dominance:

  1. It does not saturate on the positive side. For any z>0z > 0z>0, the derivative is exactly 111 — not 0.250.250.25, not e−ze^{-z}e−z-ish, but 111. A gradient can flow backward through fifty active ReLUs completely unattenuated. This single property is most of why post-2012 networks could be ten times deeper than pre-2012 networks.
  2. It is nearly free. One comparison per element. No exponentials. (On modern accelerators this matters less than it did — FFN layers are dominated by the matrix multiplies — but at 2012 compute budgets it was a real argument, and it still matters for inference on CPUs and edge devices.)
  3. It produces genuine sparsity. With zero-mean pre-activations, about half of all neurons output exactly zero on any given input. The representation for each input is effectively a different sub-network — a different subset of neurons — which works as a mild regularizer and gives activations an interpretable “on/off” character. (When we reach features and superposition, the fact that ReLU can produce exact zeros will turn out to shape how models pack concepts into neurons.)

Dying ReLU, mechanically

ReLU’s weakness is the mirror image of its strength: for z<0z < 0z<0 the gradient is not small, it is exactly zero. Usually that is fine — a neuron that is off for this input is on for others, and gradients from those other inputs keep its weights alive. The pathology arrives when a neuron’s pre-activation becomes negative for every input in the data distribution. Then its output is always 0, so the local derivative is always 0, so by the chain rule every gradient to its weights and bias is always 0 — and here is the trap: the only thing that could restore the neuron is a weight update, and weight updates are exactly what the zero gradient forbids. The neuron is not merely quiet; it is unreachable. It will stay dead for the remainder of training, contributing nothing while still costing parameters and compute. This is the dying ReLU problem.

✏️Worked example

Watch one neuron die. It has a single input xxx drawn from roughly [−1,1][-1, 1][−1,1], weight w=1.2w = 1.2w=1.2, bias b=0.5b = 0.5b=0.5, so its pre-activation z=wx+bz = wx + bz=wx+b ranges over [−0.7, 1.7][-0.7,\ 1.7][−0.7, 1.7] — it fires on most inputs and learns normally. Now one unlucky mini-batch produces a large gradient (perhaps the learning rate is too high, or an outlier example slips in), and the update knocks the parameters to w=0.8w = 0.8w=0.8, b=−4.0b = -4.0b=−4.0. The new pre-activation range is [−4.8, −3.2][-4.8,\ -3.2][−4.8, −3.2]: negative for every possible input. Output: always 0. Gradient through max⁡(0,z)\max(0,z)max(0,z): always 0. Updates to www and bbb: always 0×(anything)=00 \times (\text{anything}) = 00×(anything)=0. No future data can ever move bbb back up, because moving bbb requires a gradient and the gradient requires the neuron to fire on at least one input. One bad step, permanent damage. This is why dying ReLU incidents cluster early in training and after learning-rate spikes — the same loss-spike events discussed in Chapter 12.

The straightforward fixes patch the flat region. Leaky ReLU gives the negative side a small constant slope α\alphaα (typically 0.01–0.1): ϕ(z)=z\phi(z) = zϕ(z)=z for z>0z > 0z>0, αz\alpha zαz otherwise — now a “dead” neuron still receives α\alphaα-scaled gradients and can recover; the price is losing exact sparsity. ELU, the exponential linear unit (Clevert et al., 2015), replaces the negative side with the smooth curve α(ez−1)\alpha(e^{z} - 1)α(ez−1), which saturates at −α-\alpha−α: it keeps gradients alive near zero, pushes mean activations toward zero (helping the same zig-zag problem tanh solved), and is smooth — at the price of an exponential. Both work; neither ever displaced plain ReLU as the convolutional-era default, because dying neurons are an annoyance rather than a catastrophe when networks are wide, learning rates are sane, and batch normalization (Chapter 14) keeps pre-activation distributions centered. The variant that did eventually displace ReLU came from a different line of thinking altogether.

Smooth gates: GELU and SiLU

Look back at ReLU and describe it differently: it multiplies its input by a hard binary gate, ReLU(z)=z⋅1[z>0]\mathrm{ReLU}(z) = z \cdot \mathbf{1}[z > 0]ReLU(z)=z⋅1[z>0], where 1[⋅]\mathbf{1}[\cdot]1[⋅] is 1 when the condition holds and 0 otherwise. The 2016 question of Hendrycks and Gimpel was: what if the gate were soft — what if, instead of keeping the input exactly when it is positive, we keep it with a probability that grows with how positive it is?

∑The math

Let Φ(z)\Phi(z)Φ(z) be the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the standard normal distribution from Chapter 6: Φ(z)=P(Z≤z)\Phi(z) = \P(Z \le z)Φ(z)=P(Z≤z) for Z∼N(0,1)Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)Z∼N(0,1) — the S-shaped curve that rises from 0 to 1, passing through Φ(0)=12\Phi(0) = \tfrac12Φ(0)=21​. Imagine multiplying each pre-activation zzz by a random Bernoulli mask mmm (as dropout does, Chapter 13), but with an input-dependent keep-probability p=Φ(z)p = \Phi(z)p=Φ(z): strongly positive inputs are almost always kept, strongly negative ones almost always zeroed. The expected output of that stochastic neuron is

E[ z⋅m ]=z⋅P(m=1)=z Φ(z),\E[\,z \cdot m\,] = z \cdot \P(m = 1) = z\,\Phi(z),E[z⋅m]=z⋅P(m=1)=zΦ(z),

and taking this expectation as a deterministic function defines the Gaussian error linear unit (GELU):

GELU(z)=z Φ(z).\mathrm{GELU}(z) = z\,\Phi(z).GELU(z)=zΦ(z).

Its derivative, by the product rule, is GELU′(z)=Φ(z)+z φ(z)\mathrm{GELU}'(z) = \Phi(z) + z\,\varphi(z)GELU′(z)=Φ(z)+zφ(z), where φ(z)=12πe−z2/2\varphi(z) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}φ(z)=2π​1​e−z2/2 is the normal density. Two features distinguish it from everything in the ReLU family: the derivative briefly exceeds 1 (peaking near 1.131.131.13 around z≈1.4z \approx 1.4z≈1.4), and it is briefly negative (bottoming near −0.13-0.13−0.13 around z≈−1.4z \approx -1.4z≈−1.4) — the function itself is non-monotonic, dipping to a minimum of about −0.17-0.17−0.17 near z≈−0.75z \approx -0.75z≈−0.75 before decaying to zero.

✏️Worked example

GELU by hand, using normal-CDF values: Φ(1)=0.841\Phi(1) = 0.841Φ(1)=0.841, so GELU(1)=1×0.841=0.841\mathrm{GELU}(1) = 1 \times 0.841 = 0.841GELU(1)=1×0.841=0.841 — a mildly positive input is “84% kept.” Φ(2)=0.977\Phi(2) = 0.977Φ(2)=0.977, so GELU(2)=1.954\mathrm{GELU}(2) = 1.954GELU(2)=1.954 — nearly identity. On the negative side, GELU(−1)=−1×0.159=−0.159\mathrm{GELU}(-1) = -1 \times 0.159 = -0.159GELU(−1)=−1×0.159=−0.159 and GELU(−2)=−2×0.0228=−0.046\mathrm{GELU}(-2) = -2 \times 0.0228 = -0.046GELU(−2)=−2×0.0228=−0.046: negative inputs are damped, not deleted, and more negative means more thoroughly damped — unlike Leaky ReLU’s fixed slope. A neuron at z=−1z = -1z=−1 still passes back a (small, negative) gradient and can recover; nothing dies.

Φ\PhiΦ has no closed form, so implementations historically used approximations, and one of them became famous by accident: the tanh approximation GELU(z)≈0.5 z(1+tanh⁡[2/π (z+0.044715 z3)])\mathrm{GELU}(z) \approx 0.5\,z\bigl(1 + \tanh[\sqrt{2/\pi}\,(z + 0.044715\,z^3)]\bigr)GELU(z)≈0.5z(1+tanh[2/π​(z+0.044715z3)]), which is the exact formula hard-coded in the GPT-2 source (there is also a cruder sigmoid approximation, z σ(1.702z)z\,\sigma(1.702z)zσ(1.702z)). The approximation is accurate to a few times 10−410^{-4}10−4 at worst, but “faithfully reproducing GPT-2” requires the approximation, not true GELU — a minor archaeological detail that has tripped up more than one reimplementation. Modern frameworks compute exact GELU via the built-in erf and it costs almost nothing.

SiLU/Swish is the same idea with the logistic curve as the gate instead of the normal CDF:

SiLU(z)=z σ(z).\mathrm{SiLU}(z) = z\,\sigma(z).SiLU(z)=zσ(z).

The name has a tangled history worth thirty seconds: the function was first written down and named the sigmoid linear unit (SiLU) in the GELU paper itself (2016), independently studied by Elfwing et al. for reinforcement learning, and then rediscovered in 2017 by Ramachandran et al. via an automated search over thousands of candidate activation formulas — the search’s winner, which they named Swish (z σ(βz)z\,\sigma(\beta z)zσ(βz), with Swish-1 the β=1\beta=1β=1 case). Both names survive: papers say SiLU, blog posts say Swish, and they mean the same curve. Numerically it is a hair cheaper than GELU (one sigmoid versus one erf) and nearly indistinguishable in shape: same identity-like positive side, same smooth dip on the negative side (minimum ≈−0.28\approx -0.28≈−0.28 at z≈−1.28z \approx -1.28z≈−1.28).

Why did GELU beat ReLU in transformers? The honest answer is: empirically and modestly. BERT, GPT-2, and GPT-3 all used GELU, consistent small perplexity gains were reproduced widely, and folk explanations — smoothness gives better-behaved curvature for the optimizer (Chapter 5); the negative dip preserves a gradient path everywhere; the soft gate is a gentler regularizer — are plausible but not proven as the mechanism. What is certain is the practical consequence: from 2018 to roughly 2022, “activation = GELU” was part of the transformer recipe, and dying-ReLU stopped being a thing language-model trainers thought about.

Where activations live: the transformer FFN block

Everything so far treated the activation as a curve floating in space. Time to install it in its actual home. In a transformer (Chapter 25 assembles the whole machine), each layer has two sub-blocks: attention, which moves information between token positions, and the feed-forward network (FFN) — also called the multi-layer perceptron (MLP) block — which processes each position independently — the same little two-layer network applied to every token’s vector. The classic FFN is:

FFN(x)=ϕ(xW1+b1) W2+b2,\mathrm{FFN}(x) = \phi(x W_1 + b_1)\, W_2 + b_2,FFN(x)=ϕ(xW1​+b1​)W2​+b2​,

where x∈Rdx \in \R^{d}x∈Rd is one token’s representation (a row vector here), W1∈Rd×dffW_1 \in \R^{d \times d_{\mathrm{ff}}}W1​∈Rd×dff​ up-projects it to a wider hidden dimension dffd_{\mathrm{ff}}dff​, the activation ϕ\phiϕ is applied elementwise, and W2∈Rdff×dW_2 \in \R^{d_{\mathrm{ff}} \times d}W2​∈Rdff​×d down-projects back to ddd so the result can be added to the residual stream (Chapter 16). The 2017 transformer set dff=4dd_{\mathrm{ff}} = 4ddff​=4d — expand fourfold, bend, contract — and that ratio became a near-universal convention.

💡Intuition

Why expand at all? Think of the FFN as a key–value memory: each of the dffd_{\mathrm{ff}}dff​ hidden neurons has an input pattern (its row-direction in W1W_1W1​) that it detects, and an output pattern (its row in W2W_2W2​) that it writes into the residual stream when detected, scaled by how strongly the activation fired. More hidden neurons means more detectable patterns — 4d4d4d of them, e.g. 16,384 pattern-detectors per layer at d=4096d = 4096d=4096 — and the activation is the decision point determining which fire on this token. Wide-then-narrow, with a nonlinearity at the wide point, buys lots of decisions while keeping the interface to the rest of the network at dimension ddd. This “neurons as detect-and-write memory” view returns as a central object of study in interpretability.

(Biases would add 16,384+4096=20,48016{,}384 + 4096 = 20{,}48016,384+4096=20,480 — about 0.015% — which is one reason most modern large language models (LLMs) drop biases entirely.) The FFN is where roughly two-thirds of a dense transformer’s parameters sit, which is exactly why mixture-of-experts models replicate this block, and why its design deserves the scrutiny it is about to get.

Gating the FFN: the GLU family and SwiGLU

In 2016, Dauphin et al. introduced the gated linear unit (GLU) for convolutional language models: instead of computing one hidden vector and bending it, compute two linear projections of the input — use one, sigmoid-squashed, to gate the other:

GLU(x)=σ(xW)⊙(xV),\mathrm{GLU}(x) = \sigma(x W) \odot (x V),GLU(x)=σ(xW)⊙(xV),

where ⊙\odot⊙ is elementwise multiplication and W,VW, VW,V are separate weight matrices. This is the LSTM gate idea (multiply content by a learned 0-to-1 signal) transplanted into a feed-forward setting. In 2020, Noam Shazeer asked a very Shazeer question: what happens if we rebuild the transformer FFN this way, and what if the gate’s squashing function were something other than sigmoid? Swapping in ReLU gives “ReGLU,” GELU gives “GEGLU,” and Swish/SiLU gives SwiGLU:

FFNSwiGLU(x)=( SiLU(xW1) ⊙ (xV) ) W2.\mathrm{FFN}_{\mathrm{SwiGLU}}(x) = \bigl(\,\mathrm{SiLU}(x W_1)\ \odot\ (x V)\,\bigr)\, W_2 .FFNSwiGLU​(x)=(SiLU(xW1​) ⊙ (xV))W2​.

Trace the dimensions carefully, because this is the block running inside nearly every open-weights frontier model you can download today. Let x∈R1×dx \in \R^{1 \times d}x∈R1×d be one token:

  1. Gate path: xW1x W_1xW1​ with W1∈Rd×dffW_1 \in \R^{d \times d_{\mathrm{ff}}}W1​∈Rd×dff​ gives u∈R1×dffu \in \R^{1 \times d_{\mathrm{ff}}}u∈R1×dff​, then apply SiLU elementwise: a=SiLU(u)a = \mathrm{SiLU}(u)a=SiLU(u).
  2. Content path: xVx VxV with V∈Rd×dffV \in \R^{d \times d_{\mathrm{ff}}}V∈Rd×dff​ gives b∈R1×dffb \in \R^{1 \times d_{\mathrm{ff}}}b∈R1×dff​ — no activation on this path; it stays linear.
  3. Gating: h=a⊙b∈R1×dffh = a \odot b \in \R^{1 \times d_{\mathrm{ff}}}h=a⊙b∈R1×dff​, each content coordinate scaled by its own gate value.
  4. Down-projection: y=h W2y = h\, W_2y=hW2​ with W2∈Rdff×dW_2 \in \R^{d_{\mathrm{ff}} \times d}W2​∈Rdff​×d, giving y∈R1×dy \in \R^{1 \times d}y∈R1×d for the residual stream.

(You will see the three matrices in code as gate_proj, up_proj, down_proj — Llama’s names for W1W_1W1​, VVV, W2W_2W2​.)

💡Intuition

The ⊙\odot⊙ is the whole point, so hold it up to the light. In a standard FFN, each hidden coordinate’s value and its “should this pass?” decision are entangled in one number: ϕ(ui)\phi(u_i)ϕ(ui​) is both the signal and the switch. In a gated FFN they are separated: bib_ibi​ (content path) carries what — an unrestricted linear readout of the token, free to be large, negative, whatever — while SiLU(ui)\mathrm{SiLU}(u_i)SiLU(ui​) (gate path) decides how much of it gets through, computed from a different learned projection of the same token. The block can now express things like “copy this feature strongly, but only when that other feature is present” — a multiplicative interaction between two learned views of the input, which a single bent projection cannot represent as directly. It is the LSTM lesson learned once more: letting the network modulate its own signal path multiplicatively is worth parameters. (It is also, at a different scale, the same shape of idea as attention — data-dependent multiplicative routing — and as the router in a mixture-of-experts layer.)

The two-thirds arithmetic

There is a bookkeeping wrinkle that every model card inherits. The gated FFN has three matrices of shape d×dffd \times d_{\mathrm{ff}}d×dff​ (or its transpose) instead of two. Keep dff=4dd_{\mathrm{ff}} = 4ddff​=4d and you get 3×4096×16,384≈201.33 \times 4096 \times 16{,}384 \approx 201.33×4096×16,384≈201.3M parameters per layer — a 50% increase, which would make any comparison against the standard FFN unfair and blow up the parameter budget. Shazeer’s fix: shrink the hidden width by a factor of 23\tfrac2332​ so the totals match. Set parameters equal:

3 d dffgated=2 d (4d)⟹dffgated=23⋅4d=8d3.3\, d\, d_{\mathrm{ff}}^{\mathrm{gated}} = 2\, d\, (4d) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d_{\mathrm{ff}}^{\mathrm{gated}} = \tfrac{2}{3}\cdot 4d = \tfrac{8d}{3}.3ddffgated​=2d(4d)⟹dffgated​=32​⋅4d=38d​.

So SwiGLU trades away a third of its hidden neurons (11,008 pattern detectors instead of 16,384) to buy each surviving neuron a private multiplicative gate — and at matched parameters and compute, that trade wins on language-modeling quality. Shazeer’s paper showed it across the GLU family on T5-style pretraining and fine-tuning benchmarks; PaLM confirmed it at 540B scale; Llama baked it into the open-weights default; and Mistral, Qwen, and DeepSeek (whose V3 experts are small SwiGLU FFNs) carried it forward. Google’s Gemma models use the GELU-gated sibling, GeGLU — same architecture, different squash. As for why gating wins, the honest state of knowledge was stated by Shazeer himself in the paper’s conclusion, and the sentence has become a minor classic of research humility:

“We offer no explanation as to why these architectures seem to work; we attribute their success, as all else, to divine benevolence.”

Six years later there are partial stories — multiplicative interactions raise effective expressivity per parameter; the linear content path gives gradients an unsquashed highway; second-order terms xW1⊙xVx W_1 \odot x VxW1​⊙xV resemble a cheap quadratic feature map — but no consensus mechanism, and the empirical margin, while consistent, is modest (fractions of a point of perplexity, not miracles). It is worth sitting with this example: one of the most universally adopted architectural choices in modern AI is justified by “we tried it at matched compute and it was reliably a bit better.” A great deal of frontier architecture is exactly this kind of knowledge.

🕰History

The arc in one paragraph: step functions in the 1958 perceptron; sigmoid and tanh through the backprop era (1986 onward) because differentiability was everything; ReLU’s takeover 2010–2012 (Nair & Hinton, Glorot et al., AlexNet) once depth exposed saturation as the binding constraint; Leaky ReLU (2013) and ELU (2015) patching the dead zone; GELU (2016) smoothing the gate probabilistically, adopted by BERT and the GPT line; Swish (2017) rediscovering SiLU by automated search; Dauphin’s GLU (2016) reviving multiplicative gates; and Shazeer (2020) fusing the last two threads into SwiGLU, which Llama (2023) fixed as the open default. Since then the curve wars have been largely quiet — the frontier moved on to where computation happens (mixture-of-experts, attention variants) rather than which bend to use.

Choosing an activation in 2026

⚙️In practice

For large language models, this decision is now settled science, in the sense that nobody spends innovation budget on it. The default is a SwiGLU FFN (no biases, hidden width ≈83d\approx \tfrac{8}{3}d≈38​d rounded to a hardware-friendly multiple), as in Llama 3, Mistral, Qwen, and DeepSeek-V3 — in DeepSeek’s case, as the inner block of each of the 256 routed experts per mixture-of-experts (MoE) layer. GEGLU (Gemma) is an equivalent-quality sibling. Plain GELU with dff=4dd_{\mathrm{ff}} = 4ddff​=4d persists in the GPT-2/3 lineage, BERT-family encoders, and vision transformers, where it remains entirely respectable. Plain ReLU still rules many convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and latency-critical edge models (sometimes precisely because exact zeros can be exploited for sparsity-aware inference). One live counter-current: squared ReLU, max⁡(0,x)2\max(0,x)^2max(0,x)2, proposed in Google’s Primer search (2021), has shown up in efficiency-focused training-speedrun communities as matching or beating GELU in non-gated FFNs — a reminder that “settled” means “not worth relitigating at the frontier,” not “proven optimal.”

⚠️Careful

One place activation choice still bites in practice: quantization. Post-SwiGLU transformers exhibit activation outliers — a few hidden coordinates whose magnitudes run orders of magnitude larger than the rest (the “massive activations” phenomenon; the elementwise product of two linear paths can spike quadratically where a bent single path could not). Weights quantize to 4 bits gracefully; these outlier activations are what make cheap 8-bit activation quantization hard, forcing the mixed-precision decompositions and smoothing tricks you will meet in Inference: Sampling, Quantization, Serving (e.g. LLM.int8()). When you read that chapter, remember the culprit lives here, in the FFN’s gate.

A last conceptual note before the recap. It is tempting to file this chapter under “minor plumbing,” but the FFN-with-activation is, by parameter count, most of what a dense transformer is — roughly two-thirds of the weights — and interpretability work increasingly treats its hidden neurons (and the finer-grained features hiding inside them, via sparse autoencoders) as the model’s basic units of stored knowledge. The little bend is where the memories live.

Where this leaves us

  • You can prove why nonlinearity is necessary: linear layers compose into one linear layer, so the activation is the network’s only source of curvature — and it must be judged by its derivative as much as its value.
  • You can explain sigmoid’s failure quantitatively (σ′≤0.25\sigma' \le 0.25σ′≤0.25, vanishing tails, 0.2510≈10−60.25^{10} \approx 10^{-6}0.2510≈10−6) and why tanh’s zero-centered range helps.
  • You can explain ReLU’s dominance (unit gradient on the positive side, near-zero cost, sparsity) and narrate a neuron’s death mechanically: one large update makes the pre-activation negative for all inputs, and the resulting zero gradient forecloses recovery — with Leaky ReLU and ELU as the patches.
  • You can define GELU as z Φ(z)z\,\Phi(z)zΦ(z), derive it as the expectation of an input-dependent stochastic gate, and compute it by hand; SiLU is the same idea with σ\sigmaσ as the gate.
  • You can draw the modern SwiGLU FFN from memory — gate path through SiLU, linear content path, ⊙\odot⊙, down-projection — trace every shape, and reproduce the 8d3\tfrac{8d}{3}38d​ width arithmetic that keeps parameters matched (11,008 at d=4096d = 4096d=4096).
  • You know what the field does and does not understand: gating reliably wins at matched compute, and the best available explanation is still Shazeer’s “divine benevolence.”

The FFN’s output, like attention’s, is added to the token’s vector rather than replacing it — and that additive design, the residual connection, is its own deep story about why hundred-layer networks train at all: Next: Residual Connections and the Residual Stream.
