The KV Cache and Efficient Attention

Why LLM inference is a memory problem, not a compute problem: the KV cache and its brutal arithmetic, MQA/GQA/MLA, sliding windows, attention sinks, and FlashAttention.

The last few chapters built attention as mathematics: queries, keys, values, scaled dot products, many heads in parallel, all stacked into the transformer. The math is finished. This chapter is about what happens when you run it — and the running turns out to be dominated by a problem the equations never mention.

Here is the uncomfortable fact. When a model generates text, it produces one token at a time, and each new token must attend over everything that came before. Done naively, that means re-running attention over the entire growing sequence for every single token — the thousandth token of a reply costs a thousand times more than it should. The fix, the KV (key–value) cache, is one of those ideas that takes a sentence to state and then quietly takes over the entire economics of the field: store the keys and values you already computed, and never compute them again. But the cache has a price of its own. It grows with every token, every layer, and every concurrent user, and for a modern model at a long context it can be as large as the model itself. In 2026, the cost of serving a large language model (LLM) — the price per token on every API pricing page — is substantially the story of who shrinks and shuffles this cache best.

So this is a systems chapter, the first real one in the book. It has two centerpieces. The first is the cache and the family of architectural tricks invented to shrink it: multi-query attention (MQA), grouped-query attention (GQA), and DeepSeek’s multi-head latent attention (MLA), which is the payoff of the low-rank ideas from Chapter 2. The second is FlashAttention, which attacks a different bottleneck — the memory traffic inside the graphics processing unit (GPU) during attention itself — and does it without approximating anything. Along the way we will meet the two-phase life of every LLM request (prefill and decode), sliding windows, the strange phenomenon of attention sinks, and the honest case for and against linear attention. By the end, when you read that some model “uses GQA with 8 KV heads” or “achieves 75% GPU utilization with FA3”, you will know exactly what is being claimed and why it matters.

The recomputation problem

Start with what generation requires. A decoder-only transformer generates autoregressively: feed in the prompt, get a probability distribution over the next token, sample one, append it, repeat. At step ttt, the model needs attention outputs for position ttt — and, because of the causal mask, position ttt attends to positions 111 through ttt and nothing else.

The naive implementation re-runs the whole forward pass on the whole sequence at every step. Generating token 1,001 means embedding 1,001 tokens, computing queries, keys, and values for all of them in every layer, forming the full attention pattern, and then throwing away everything except the output at the final position. Almost all of that work is wasted, and it is wasted in a particularly galling way: it is identical to work done at the previous step.

Why identical? Look at what the causal mask guarantees. The key and value vectors at position jjj are computed from the hidden state at position jjj, and the hidden state at position jjj only ever attends backward — it depends on positions 1…j1 \dots j1…j and nothing later. Appending token t+1t+1t+1 to the sequence changes nothing about positions 1…t1 \dots t1…t. Their hidden states, and therefore their keys and values, are bit-for-bit the same as last step. A decoder transformer never revises its past.

💡Intuition

Think of the model’s processed context as a filing cabinet. Each generated token files two index cards per layer per head: a key (“here is how to find me”) and a value (“here is what I contribute if you do”). Once filed, a card never changes — causality means no future token can reach back and edit it. So generation should be: the new token walks in with its query, riffles through every card in the cabinet, takes its weighted combination, and files its own two cards on the way out. The cabinet is the KV cache.

That is the entire mechanism. Cache the keys and values; compute only the new token’s query, key, and value each step. The new query attends against all cached keys, the resulting weights combine all cached values, and the new key–value pair is appended to the cache for the benefit of future tokens. Per step, attention now costs one query against ttt keys — a matrix–vector product — instead of ttt queries against ttt keys. Generating nnn tokens drops from O(n3)O(n^3)O(n3) total attention work (summing t2t^2t2 over steps) to O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2), and, more importantly in practice, each step drops from O(t2)O(t^2)O(t2) to O(t)O(t)O(t).

Note what is not cached: queries. A query is used exactly once, at the moment its token is generated, to look backward. No future token ever needs it again. Keys and values are used at every subsequent step. That asymmetry — queries are ephemeral, keys and values are permanent — is the load-bearing observation of this entire chapter, and every architecture in it is a different answer to the question “how small can we make the permanent part?”

The cache’s bill: a memory formula worth memorizing

The cache converts wasted compute into stored memory, and the exchange rate is steep. Every layer keeps its own cache (the keys and values live in each layer’s own representation space), every key–value head within a layer keeps its own, and every sequence being served keeps its own. Nothing is shared.

∑The math

For a model with LLL layers, nkvn_{kv}nkv​ key–value heads per layer, head dimension dhd_hdh​, serving a batch of BBB sequences of current length SSS tokens, with bbb bytes per number (2 for the 16-bit floating-point formats FP16/BF16), the cache occupies

KV bytes  =  2⏟K and V×L×nkv×dh×S×B×b.\text{KV bytes} \;=\; \underbrace{2}_{\text{K and V}} \times L \times n_{kv} \times d_h \times S \times B \times b .KV bytes=K and V2​​×L×nkv​×dh​×S×B×b.

Every factor is linear. Doubling context length, batch size, or KV-head count doubles the cache. The per-token, per-layer cost 2 nkv dh b2\, n_{kv}\, d_h\, b2nkv​dh​b is the number architects fight over; everything else is workload.

✏️Worked example

Take Llama 3.1 8B, a real config you can check against its model card: L=32L = 32L=32 layers, nkv=8n_{kv} = 8nkv​=8 KV heads, dh=128d_h = 128dh​=128, BF16 (b=2b = 2b=2), context up to S=131,072S = 131{,}072S=131,072 tokens (the “128k” on the box).

Per token: 2×32×8×128×2=131,0722 \times 32 \times 8 \times 128 \times 2 = 131{,}0722×32×8×128×2=131,072 bytes — 128 KB per token. A full 128k-token context for one user:

128 KB×131,072≈16 GB.128\ \text{KB} \times 131{,}072 \approx 16\ \text{GB}.128 KB×131,072≈16 GB.

The model’s weights in BF16 are about 16 GB. One user at full context doubles the model’s memory footprint. Serve a batch of 8 such users and the cache alone is 128 GB — more than an entire 80 GB H100, before loading a single weight. And this is with grouped-query attention already shrinking the cache 4×; with classic multi-head attention (nkv=32n_{kv} = 32nkv​=32), the same context would cost 512 KB per token and 64 GB per user.

Sit with that example, because it is the punchline of the first half of the chapter. The KV cache is why “context length” is a headline spec, why serving long-context requests costs more, and why memory capacity — not floating-point throughput — is often what decides how many users fit on a GPU. It is also why the next three sections exist: MQA, GQA, and MLA are all attacks on the factor nkv×dhn_{kv} \times d_hnkv​×dh​ in the formula.

⚠️Careful

A common confusion: the KV cache does not grow with the number of query heads. A model can have 128 query heads and 8 KV heads; only the 8 appear in the memory formula. When you read a config file, the parameter that controls cache size is usually called num_key_value_heads, and it is frequently much smaller than num_attention_heads. Conflating the two makes your memory estimates wrong by up to 16×.

Prefill and decode: two phases, two bottlenecks

Every LLM request lives two very different lives, and understanding the difference explains most of inference engineering.

Prefill is what happens to your prompt. All prompt tokens are known upfront, so the model processes them in one parallel pass — exactly like a training forward pass. The matrices are big and chunky: multiplying a 2,048×4,0962{,}048 \times 4{,}0962,048×4,096 activation block by a 4,096×4,0964{,}096 \times 4{,}0964,096×4,096 weight matrix, thousands of times. GPUs love this. Prefill is compute-bound: the limiting resource is floating-point throughput, and a well-written prefill keeps the GPU’s matrix units nearly saturated. This is why the “time to first token” of a long prompt is roughly proportional to prompt length but still measured in seconds, not minutes.

Decode is what happens after: one token at a time, each step a full pass through the model for a single position (per sequence). Every matrix multiplication in the network degenerates into a matrix–vector product: one activation vector times each weight matrix, plus one query vector against the cached keys. And matrix–vector products have a dirty secret — they barely use the GPU’s compute at all.

∑The math

Arithmetic intensity is the ratio of floating-point operations (FLOPs) a computation performs to the bytes of memory it must move. A matrix–vector product WxW\mathbf{x}Wx with W∈Rm×nW \in \R^{m \times n}W∈Rm×n in BF16 reads 2mn2mn2mn bytes of weights and performs 2mn2mn2mn FLOPs (one multiply and one add per weight): intensity ≈1\approx 1≈1 FLOP/byte, roughly independent of size. Each weight is read from memory, used once, and discarded.

Compare the hardware’s balance point. An H100 SXM delivers roughly 990 TFLOP/s of dense BF16 compute and 3.35 TB/s of memory bandwidth (both approximate; marketing numbers). To keep the compute units fed, a kernel must perform about

990×1012 FLOP/s3.35×1012 B/s≈295 FLOPs per byte moved.\frac{990 \times 10^{12}\ \text{FLOP/s}}{3.35 \times 10^{12}\ \text{B/s}} \approx 295\ \text{FLOPs per byte moved}.3.35×1012 B/s990×1012 FLOP/s​≈295 FLOPs per byte moved.

Decode’s intensity of ∼1\sim 1∼1 is nearly 300× below that ridge. During single-stream decoding the GPU’s arithmetic units sit more than 99% idle, waiting on memory. This “am I limited by compute or by bandwidth?” analysis is called a roofline model, and the plain-English version is all you need: big matrix–matrix products are compute-bound; matrix–vector products are memory-bandwidth-bound.

✏️Worked example

The same Llama 3.1 8B during decode, batch size 1. Each token requires reading all ~16 GB of BF16 weights (plus the KV cache) and performs about 2×8×109=162 \times 8\times10^9 = 162×8×109=16 GFLOPs. On 3.35 TB/s of bandwidth, reading the weights alone takes 16/3350≈4.816 / 3350 \approx 4.816/3350≈4.8 ms — a hard ceiling of roughly 210 tokens/second, no matter how fast the compute is, and reality is lower once the growing KV cache adds to the traffic. At a 128k context, the 16 GB of cache doubles the bytes per step and roughly halves the ceiling. Meanwhile the compute used, 16 GFLOPs in ~5 ms, is about 3 TFLOP/s — a third of one percent of the chip’s peak.

This is why serving systems obsess over batching: with BBB sequences decoded together, the weights are read once per step and shared across all BBB matrix–vector products, multiplying arithmetic intensity by BBB. But note the sting in the tail — the KV cache reads are not shared. Each sequence has its own cache, so cache traffic scales with BBB even as weight traffic is amortized. At long contexts, the cache becomes the dominant per-step memory traffic and the thing that caps batch size. Once again: shrink the cache, and everything downstream gets better — more users per GPU, faster steps, cheaper tokens. Which brings us to the shrinking.

Sharing keys: MQA and GQA

The first idea is almost embarrassingly direct. Multi-head attention (MHA) gives each of the hhh heads its own queries, keys, and values, on the theory that different heads should look for different things. Multi-query attention (MQA) — proposed by Noam Shazeer in a terse 2019 paper, “Fast Transformer Decoding: One Write-Head is All You Need” — keeps all hhh query heads but makes them share a single key head and a single value head. Every head still looks at the sequence through its own learned query lens; they all riffle through the same one set of filing cards.

The cache shrinks by a factor of hhh — for a 32-head model, 32× — and decode gets dramatically faster because each step reads 1/h1/h1/h as many cached bytes. The cost is quality: one shared key space must serve every head’s retrieval needs, and in practice MQA models train slightly worse and degrade more on tasks that lean hard on attention. For years this looked like an awkward either/or.

Grouped-query attention (GQA) (Ainslie et al., 2023) supplied the obvious-in-retrospect middle: partition the hhh query heads into ggg groups, and give each group one shared KV head. With g=hg = hg=h you recover MHA; with g=1g = 1g=1 you recover MQA; with g=8g = 8g=8 and h=64h = 64h=64 you cut the cache 8× while leaving each KV head serving only 8 query heads. The paper’s second trick was economic: you can convert a trained MHA checkpoint to GQA by mean-pooling each group’s key and value projections, then “uptraining” briefly — about 5% of the original pretraining compute — to recover quality. Ablations showed GQA at modest ggg matching MHA quality within noise while decoding nearly as fast as MQA. The debate ended quickly; GQA became the default architecture of the open-weights era.

MLA: compressing the cache instead of sharing it

GQA shrinks the cache by making heads share. DeepSeek’s multi-head latent attention (MLA), introduced in DeepSeek-V2 (2024), shrinks it by compression — and manages to keep every head fully distinct while caching only about twice what all-sharing MQA would (the V2 paper puts it at “GQA with 2.25 groups”). It is the most mathematically interesting architecture in this chapter, and it is a direct payoff of an idea from Chapter 2: a large matrix that is approximately low-rank can be stored as two thin factors.

The observation: the concatenated keys and values at a position are computed from one hidden vector ht\mathbf{h}_tht​ by linear projections. Together they form a 2nhdh2 n_h d_h2nh​dh​-dimensional object (32,768 numbers per layer in DeepSeek-V3’s shape) — but they are all linear functions of the same input, so the information they contain can’t exceed what a much smaller vector carries. Why store the expanded form? Store the compressed one, and expand on demand.

∑The math

MLA replaces the per-head key/value projections with a two-stage factorization. First, a down-projection compresses the hidden state into a small latent:

ct  =  WDKV ht,ct∈Rdc,dc≪nhdh.\mathbf{c}_t \;=\; W^{DKV}\,\mathbf{h}_t, \qquad \mathbf{c}_t \in \R^{d_c},\quad d_c \ll n_h d_h .ct​=WDKVht​,ct​∈Rdc​,dc​≪nh​dh​.

(DeepSeek-V2/V3: dc=512d_c = 512dc​=512 while nhdh=128×128=16,384n_h d_h = 128 \times 128 = 16{,}384nh​dh​=128×128=16,384.) Only ct\mathbf{c}_tct​ is cached. At attention time, per-head keys and values are reconstructed by up-projections:

kt(i)=W(i)UK ct,vt(i)=W(i)UV ct.\mathbf{k}_t^{(i)} = W^{UK}_{(i)}\,\mathbf{c}_t, \qquad \mathbf{v}_t^{(i)} = W^{UV}_{(i)}\,\mathbf{c}_t .kt(i)​=W(i)UK​ct​,vt(i)​=W(i)UV​ct​.

Each head’s key projection is now the product W(i)UKWDKVW^{UK}_{(i)} W^{DKV}W(i)UK​WDKV — a low-rank factorization (rank at most dcd_cdc​) of what MHA would learn as a full matrix. Heads remain fully distinct; they share only the compressed substrate.

The apparent catch — don’t we now pay the up-projection for every cached token, at every step? — dissolves via the absorption trick. The attention score for query q\mathbf{q}q against a reconstructed key is

q⊤kt(i)=q⊤(W(i)UKct)=(W(i)UK ⊤ q)⊤ct.\mathbf{q}\T \mathbf{k}_t^{(i)} = \mathbf{q}\T \bigl(W^{UK}_{(i)} \mathbf{c}_t\bigr) = \bigl(W^{UK\,\T}_{(i)}\, \mathbf{q}\bigr)\T \mathbf{c}_t .q⊤kt(i)​=q⊤(W(i)UK​ct​)=(W(i)UK⊤​q)⊤ct​.

Associativity lets us fold W(i)UKW^{UK}_{(i)}W(i)UK​ into the query side: transform the single new query once per step, then dot it directly against the cached latents. Full keys are never materialized, for any token, ever. The value up-projection absorbs into the output projection the same way. Attention effectively runs in the 512-dimensional latent space.

💡Intuition

MLA is a zip file for the filing cabinet. Instead of filing 128 heads’ worth of index cards per token, the model files one compressed summary card, and each head carries its own decoder ring (the up-projection) for reading summaries. The linear-algebra gift is that the decoder ring can be applied to the question instead of to every card — so the compressed cabinet is searched directly, and decompression never actually happens.

The RoPE conflict, and the decoupled fix

There is one genuine obstacle, and the DeepSeek paper’s solution is worth understanding because it illuminates both ideas involved. Rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) inject position by rotating each key by a position-dependent rotation matrix RtR_tRt​ (and each query by RsR_sRs​), so that scores depend on relative offset: qs⊤Rt−skt\mathbf{q}_s\T R_{t-s} \mathbf{k}_tqs⊤​Rt−s​kt​. But now trace the absorption trick with RoPE in place. The cached-side object becomes RtW(i)UKctR_t W^{UK}_{(i)} \mathbf{c}_tRt​W(i)UK​ct​, and the score is

qs⊤ Rs⊤Rt W(i)UK ct  =  qs⊤ Rt−s W(i)UK ct.\mathbf{q}_s\T\, R_{s}\T R_t\, W^{UK}_{(i)}\, \mathbf{c}_t \;=\; \mathbf{q}_s\T\, R_{t-s}\, W^{UK}_{(i)}\, \mathbf{c}_t .qs⊤​Rs⊤​Rt​W(i)UK​ct​=qs⊤​Rt−s​W(i)UK​ct​.

The rotation Rt−sR_{t-s}Rt−s​ is wedged between the query and the up-projection, and it is different for every query–key pair. Rotation matrices do not commute with arbitrary matrices, so W(i)UKW^{UK}_{(i)}W(i)UK​ can no longer be folded into the query once per step — you would have to apply a different absorbed matrix for every cached position, which is exactly the per-token work the trick existed to avoid. Compression and rotation are individually cheap and jointly awkward: one wants keys to be a fixed linear function of the cache; the other insists keys change with position.

The fix is to stop asking one vector to do both jobs. Decoupled RoPE splits each key (and query) into two concatenated parts: a large content part — compressed, position-free, absorbable, exactly as derived above — and a small positional part (dimension dR=64d_R = 64dR​=64 in V2/V3) that carries RoPE and is cached in rotated form. The positional key part is a single head shared across all query heads, MQA-style, so it adds only 64 numbers per token per layer to the cache. Scores are the sum of a content dot product (in latent space) and a positional dot product (in RoPE space). Position information flows; compression survives.

✏️Worked example

DeepSeek-V3’s cache per token per layer: dc+dR=512+64=576d_c + d_R = 512 + 64 = 576dc​+dR​=512+64=576 numbers, total — shared across all 128 heads. Full MHA at the same shape would cache 2×128×128=32,7682 \times 128 \times 128 = 32{,}7682×128×128=32,768 numbers: 57× more. Even against a typical GQA config (8 KV heads of 128 dims = 2,048 numbers), MLA stores 3.6× less — while its ablations (and V2/V3’s benchmark results) indicate quality at or above full MHA, because unlike MQA/GQA it never collapses head diversity, it only bets that K/V are jointly low-rank. The V2 paper reports a 93.3% KV-cache reduction and 5.76× higher generation throughput versus their previous 67B dense model.

⚙️In practice

MLA is a large part of why DeepSeek could price API tokens an order of magnitude below Western frontier labs in 2024–2025 and still run positive margins (their stated numbers; exact economics are fast-moving and disputed in detail, not in direction). A smaller cache means longer contexts fit, and more concurrent sequences fit, which multiplies batch size, which — recall the roofline section — multiplies arithmetic intensity and therefore throughput per GPU. DeepSeek-V2 (236B total parameters, 21B active) proved the design; DeepSeek-V3 (671B total, 37B active, 61 layers) and the R1 reasoning models carried it unchanged into the frontier tier. Since then MLA-style latent attention has spread well beyond DeepSeek — a rare case of a serving optimization changing what architectures labs train from scratch. Chapter 29 picks up the other half of DeepSeek’s economics, mixture-of-experts.

Windows, sinks, and sparsity: attending to less

MQA, GQA, and MLA shrink what each token stores. A complementary family shrinks how many tokens are attended to at all.

Sliding-window attention (SWA) is the bluntest version: each token attends only to the previous WWW tokens instead of the whole past. Mistral 7B (2023) shipped this at W=4,096W = 4{,}096W=4,096 with a rolling buffer cache — once the cache holds WWW entries, new tokens overwrite the oldest slots, capping cache memory at a constant regardless of sequence length.

The interesting question is what this does to the model’s effective memory, and the answer is subtler than “it forgets everything beyond 4k tokens.” Attention layers stack. A token at layer 2 attends to layer-1 outputs within its window — but each of those positions already summarizes its own window from layer 1. Information hops backward one window per layer, so after kkk layers the receptive field reaches roughly k×Wk \times Wk×W tokens: with 32 layers and a 4k window, in principle about 131k tokens. If you met convolutional networks in 2017, this is exactly how stacked small convolutions build large receptive fields — attention rediscovering CNN geometry.

The honest caveat: “in principle” is doing work in that sentence. Information that survives 30 relay hops through hidden states is much lossier than information attended to directly; sliding-window models are measurably worse at verbatim recall of distant text. This is why pure SWA didn’t conquer the world, and why the design that did spread is interleaving: most layers use a cheap local window while every kkk-th layer attends globally. Google’s Gemma 2 alternated local (4k window) and global layers 1:1; Gemma 3 moved to 5 local : 1 global with a 1,024-token window, cutting cache memory several-fold; OpenAI’s open-weights gpt-oss models (2025) alternate sliding-window and full layers too (configs approximate but public). The global layers preserve true long-range recall; the local layers make most of the cache constant-size.

✎Note

Attention sinks. In 2023, Xiao et al. found something odd while trying to make models stream indefinitely with a sliding cache: evict the oldest tokens — the first few in the sequence — and generation quality collapses, even though those tokens are semantically irrelevant (often just a beginning-of-sequence (BOS) token). The explanation: softmax must distribute attention mass somewhere at every step, and trained models learn to park unneeded mass on the earliest positions, which are visible to every token during training. The first tokens become “attention sinks” — structural, not semantic. Their StreamingLLM recipe — permanently keep the first ~4 tokens plus a sliding window of recent ones — keeps perplexity stable over millions of streamed tokens. The sink phenomenon has since shown up everywhere from quantization outliers to interpretability work, and some 2025-era models (gpt-oss among them) build a learned per-head sink term directly into the attention softmax.

Before transformers went decoder-only-and-huge, an earlier wave of sparse attention patterns tackled the O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) cost for encoders: Longformer combined local windows with a handful of designated global tokens (like [CLS]) that everyone attends to; BigBird added random long-range links and proved the combination keeps the theoretical expressiveness of full attention. These exact recipes faded — FlashAttention made dense attention cheap enough to postpone the reckoning — but their local + global + few-links blueprint visibly underlies today’s interleaved architectures and the trainable sparse-attention revival (e.g., DeepSeek’s 2025 native sparse attention work). Chapter 43 returns to sparsity at million-token scale.

Linear attention: the honest sales pitch

One more family deserves a fair hearing, mostly because Chapter 30 is its descendant. The O(n)O(n)O(n)-memory dream: what if the softmax is the only thing forcing us to keep the whole past around?

Attention output is ∑jsim(q,kj) vj\sum_j \text{sim}(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{k}_j)\, \mathbf{v}_j∑j​sim(q,kj​)vj​ normalized, with sim(q,k)=exp⁡(q⋅k/d)\text{sim}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}) = \exp(\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{k} / \sqrt{d})sim(q,k)=exp(q⋅k/d​). The exponential of a dot product does not factor, so the sum must be recomputed against every stored kj\mathbf{k}_jkj​. But if we replace the similarity with a factorable one — sim(q,k)=ϕ(q)⋅ϕ(k)\text{sim}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}) = \phi(\mathbf{q}) \cdot \phi(\mathbf{k})sim(q,k)=ϕ(q)⋅ϕ(k) for some feature map ϕ\phiϕ (the “kernel trick”) — the sum rearranges:

∑j(ϕ(q)⊤ϕ(kj)) vj  =  ϕ(q)⊤∑jϕ(kj) vj⊤⏟St, a d×d matrix.\sum_j \bigl(\phi(\mathbf{q})\T \phi(\mathbf{k}_j)\bigr)\,\mathbf{v}_j \;=\; \phi(\mathbf{q})\T \underbrace{\sum_j \phi(\mathbf{k}_j)\,\mathbf{v}_j\T}_{S_t,\ \text{a } d\times d \text{ matrix}} .j∑​(ϕ(q)⊤ϕ(kj​))vj​=ϕ(q)⊤St​, a d×d matrixj∑​ϕ(kj​)vj⊤​​​.

The state StS_tSt​ updates recurrently — St=St−1+ϕ(kt)vt⊤S_t = S_{t-1} + \phi(\mathbf{k}_t)\mathbf{v}_t\TSt​=St−1​+ϕ(kt​)vt⊤​ — so generation needs constant memory and constant time per token, independent of context length. No cache growth at all. This is the “transformers are RNNs” observation of Katharopoulos et al. (2020), with Performer and many successors supplying fancier feature maps.

The catch is fundamental, not an engineering detail: a fixed-size StS_tSt​ must lossily compress an unbounded past. Softmax attention can retrieve any one of 100,000 cached tokens exactly — its “memory” grows with the text. Linear attention squeezes everything through a d×dd \times dd×d bottleneck, and on recall-heavy tasks (find the needle, quote the document, resolve a distant variable name) it measurably trails. The 2024–2026 resolution is hybrids — mostly-linear layers with a few full-attention layers for recall — which is exactly where state-space models pick up the story.

FlashAttention: the same math, moved less

Everything so far shrank the cache or the attended set. The chapter’s second centerpiece attacks a different wall entirely: the memory traffic inside one attention computation, and it does so while computing exactly the same answer as the textbook algorithm. No approximation, no quality trade. FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022) is arguably the single most consequential systems paper of the transformer era, and its core ideas — awareness of the memory hierarchy, and an online softmax — are worth owning.

A GPU has two memories that matter here. High-bandwidth memory (HBM) is the big one on the spec sheet: 40–80 GB on an A100, moving data at 1.5–2.0 TB/s. SRAM (static random-access memory) is the small, fast scratchpad on the chip itself next to the compute units: about 20 MB on an A100, at roughly 19 TB/s — an order of magnitude faster, three orders of magnitude smaller. Every kernel’s real job is to do as much math as possible per trip to HBM.

Now watch the textbook attention algorithm fail at that job. For sequence length NNN: compute S=QK⊤S = QK\TS=QK⊤ (an N×NN \times NN×N matrix) and write it to HBM — it is far too big for SRAM. Read it back to compute the row-wise softmax; write the resulting PPP; read PPP again to multiply by VVV. At N=8,192N = 8{,}192N=8,192 in FP16, SSS alone is 8,1922×2≈1348{,}192^2 \times 2 \approx 1348,1922×2≈134 MB per head per sequence — with 32 heads, over 4 GB written and re-read, several times, for one layer’s attention. The arithmetic is fast; the round-trips to HBM are what you wait for. Attention at long NNN is memory-bound not because of the KV cache, but because of this O(N2)O(N^2)O(N2) intermediate that nobody wants — it is scaffolding, discarded the moment the output is formed.

FlashAttention’s move: never build the scaffolding. Split QQQ into blocks of rows and K,VK, VK,V into blocks of columns, sized so a few blocks fit in SRAM together. For one block of queries, loop over the key/value blocks: load a tile, compute that tile’s scores in SRAM, immediately fold its contribution into a running output, and discard the tile. The full N×NN \times NN×N matrix exists only one small tile at a time, on-chip. HBM sees QQQ, KKK, VVV read once-ish and OOO written once.

The online softmax

One obstacle makes this nontrivial, and its solution is a small gem. Softmax is a row-wise operation over all NNN scores — and for numerical stability it is always computed as esi−me^{s_i - m}esi​−m where mmm is the row maximum, which you seemingly can’t know until you’ve seen the whole row. Tiling shows you the row in pieces. The online softmax (an idea from Milakov and Gimelshein, 2018, which FlashAttention put to work) processes pieces while maintaining three running quantities that can be retroactively corrected whenever a new piece raises the maximum.

∑The math

Maintain, per query row: the running max mmm, the running normalizer ℓ=∑esi−m\ell = \sum e^{s_i - m}ℓ=∑esi​−m, and the running unnormalized output o=∑esi−m vi\mathbf{o} = \sum e^{s_i - m}\, \mathbf{v}_io=∑esi​−mvi​. On processing a new block with local max m~\tilde mm~, local sums ℓ~,o~\tilde\ell, \tilde{\mathbf{o}}ℓ~,o~ (computed relative to m~\tilde mm~):

m′=max⁡(m,m~),ℓ′=em−m′ ℓ+em~−m′ ℓ~,o′=em−m′ o+em~−m′ o~.m' = \max(m, \tilde m), \qquad \ell' = e^{m - m'}\,\ell + e^{\tilde m - m'}\,\tilde\ell, \qquad \mathbf{o}' = e^{m - m'}\,\mathbf{o} + e^{\tilde m - m'}\,\tilde{\mathbf{o}} .m′=max(m,m~),ℓ′=em−m′ℓ+em~−m′ℓ~,o′=em−m′o+em~−m′o~.

The factors em−m′e^{m - m'}em−m′ rescale everything accumulated so far, as if we had known the true max all along — this works because every term is an exponential, and shifting the reference point multiplies all of them by the same constant. After the last block, the answer is o/ℓ\mathbf{o} / \ello/ℓ, identical to the all-at-once softmax up to floating-point rounding.

Two more pieces complete the algorithm. For the backward pass, the naive approach would need the N×NN \times NN×N probability matrix again — so FlashAttention simply doesn’t store it and recomputes tiles from Q,K,VQ, K, VQ,K,V during backprop, keeping only the output and one NNN-vector of softmax statistics (the log-sum-exp) per head. Spending extra FLOPs to avoid memory traffic sounds like a loss and is a win, for exactly the roofline reason: the FLOPs were idle anyway, the bandwidth was the bottleneck. (This is the same trade as activation checkpointing, which returns in Chapter 34.) And under a causal mask, tiles entirely above the diagonal are skipped outright — roughly halving the work.

The result: 2–4× faster attention end-to-end, memory usage linear in NNN instead of quadratic, and — because nothing is approximated — a pure drop-in. Within about a year it was the default in every serious training and inference stack, and “we can afford 8k/32k/128k context” timelines owe more to this kernel than to any architectural change. FlashAttention-2 (FA2, 2023) reorganized the work — parallelizing across the sequence dimension, cutting non-matmul operations, better partitioning across GPU thread blocks — for roughly another 2×, reaching 50–73% of A100 peak. FlashAttention-3 (FA3, 2024) rebuilt it for Hopper GPUs, exploiting hardware asynchrony (overlapping tile loads with compute via the H100’s tensor memory accelerator) and FP8 precision, pushing BF16 attention to roughly 75% of H100 peak — approximate figures, and a moving target, but the trajectory is the point: attention kernels now chase the same utilization numbers as plain matrix multiplication.

⚠️Careful

FlashAttention and the KV cache solve different problems, and it is easy to blur them. FlashAttention removes the O(N2)O(N^2)O(N2) intermediate memory and traffic inside one attention computation — it shines in training and prefill, where many queries are processed at once. It does not shrink the KV cache by one byte: during decode you still must read every cached key and value per step. Conversely, GQA/MLA shrink the cache but do nothing about the quadratic score matrix in prefill. Real systems need both, which is why every serving engine ships both.

What runs in production

⚙️In practice

A 2026 serving stack (vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM) layers most of this chapter, plus scheduling tricks that Chapter 39 covers properly:

  • FlashAttention-class kernels everywhere — FA2/FA3 or vendor equivalents for prefill; specialized decode kernels (e.g., FlashDecoding, FlashInfer) that parallelize a single query’s attention across the long KV dimension.
  • PagedAttention (Kwon et al., 2023 — the vLLM paper): the KV cache is allocated in small fixed-size blocks with an indirection table, like virtual memory pages, eliminating the fragmentation of reserving max-context-length slabs per user and enabling copy-on-write sharing of common prefixes.
  • Prefix caching: if two requests share a prompt prefix (a system prompt, a long document being re-queried), its KV blocks are computed once and shared. SGLang’s RadixAttention organizes the cache as a prefix tree to maximize such reuse. This is exactly what API “prompt caching” discounts (Anthropic’s, OpenAI’s, DeepSeek’s) are selling: cached input tokens are cheap because their KV entries already sit on the server.
  • KV quantization: storing cached K/V in FP8 or INT4 instead of BF16 halves or quarters the memory again, with small quality cost — measurement details in Chapter 39.
  • Chunked prefill and disaggregation: long prompts are prefilled in chunks interleaved with other users’ decode steps (so one 100k-token prompt doesn’t stall everyone), or prefill and decode run on separate GPU pools sized to their different bottlenecks — compute-heavy hardware for prefill, bandwidth-heavy for decode.

Where this leaves us

  • You can state why the KV cache exists (causality freezes the past — keys and values are computed once and reused; queries are used once and discarded) and compute its size: 2⋅L⋅nkv⋅dh⋅S⋅B⋅b2 \cdot L \cdot n_{kv} \cdot d_h \cdot S \cdot B \cdot b2⋅L⋅nkv​⋅dh​⋅S⋅B⋅b — 16 GB for an 8B model at 128k context, the number that drives serving economics.
  • You can explain the two-phase life of a request: prefill is compute-bound (big matmuls), decode is memory-bandwidth-bound (matrix–vector products at ~1 FLOP/byte against a hardware ridge near ~300), which is why batching helps and why the cache caps the batch.
  • You can rank the cache-shrinking family: MQA (share one KV head, ÷hhh, quality cost), GQA (share per group — the 8-KV-head industry default), and MLA (cache a low-rank latent, absorb up-projections into the query, patch the RoPE conflict with a decoupled positional channel — MHA-level quality at far below GQA-level memory).
  • You can draw sliding-window attention’s staircase receptive field (≈k⋅W\approx k \cdot W≈k⋅W after kkk layers), explain attention sinks and why StreamingLLM keeps the first four tokens, and say honestly what linear attention buys (constant state) and costs (lossy recall).
  • You can walk through FlashAttention’s logic — tile into SRAM, run the online softmax with running (m,ℓ,o)(m, \ell, \mathbf{o})(m,ℓ,o) and retroactive rescaling, recompute in the backward pass — and defend the claim that it is exact, not approximate.
  • You know what a modern serving stack adds on top: paged and prefix-shared caches, quantized KV, chunked prefill — continued in Chapter 39.
