Derivatives and Gradients

A genuine calculus refresher — derivatives as sensitivity, the gradient as a direction, and why "training a model" means following a gradient downhill.

  5. Contour maps: seeing a surface from above
  6. What “training” actually means

A frontier language model is a single mathematical function with somewhere between billions and roughly a trillion adjustable numbers — its parameters, the knobs the training process gets to turn. Training asks a brutally practical question about every single one of those knobs, billions of times over: if I turn this knob a tiny bit, does the model get better or worse, and by how much?

That question has a one-word answer: the derivative. Answer it for every knob at once and you get a vector called the gradient — a complete sensitivity report, one number per parameter. Nearly all of modern deep learning is a loop that computes this report and nudges every knob a small step in the direction the report recommends. When people say a model “learned” something, this loop is what they mean.

This chapter rebuilds the calculus you need from close to zero. If you took calculus years ago, treat this as the refresher you were owed: we will not teleport past the honest parts. We start with what a derivative is (two pictures: a slope, and a sensitivity), compute one from the raw definition so you see there is no magic, build the small table of rules that covers nearly every function inside a neural network, and then make the leap that matters: functions with many inputs, where the derivative becomes the gradient, and a little theorem — proved with the dot product from Chapter 1 — tells us the gradient always points in the direction of steepest increase. By the end you will hand-compute one real step of gradient descent, which is the exact operation that, repeated on the order of a trillion times, produces a GPT-class model.

The derivative: slope, and sensitivity

Start with a function of one variable, f(x)f(x)f(x) — one input number in, one output number out. Think of xxx as a knob and f(x)f(x)f(x) as a dial you are watching. The derivative answers: if I turn the knob slightly, how fast does the dial move?

To make “slightly” precise we need one informal idea from calculus: a limit. We will not do the formal epsilon-delta machinery, because the working intuition is enough for everything in this book: a limit is the value an expression settles toward as some quantity gets closer and closer to a target. The expression h2+3hh\frac{h^2 + 3h}{h}hh2+3h​ is undefined at h=0h = 0h=0 (zero divided by zero), but for any nonzero hhh it equals h+3h + 3h+3, and as hhh shrinks — 0.10.10.1, 0.010.010.01, 0.0010.0010.001 — the value settles toward 333. We say its limit as h→0h \to 0h→0 is 333. That is the whole idea: not what happens at the forbidden point, but what value the trend commits to.

💡Intuition

Two mental pictures of the derivative, both worth keeping.

Slope: draw the graph of fff, zoom in at a point until the curve looks like a straight line (every smooth curve eventually does), and read off that line’s slope. Positive slope: the function is rising there. Slope 444: it rises 4 units of output per unit of input.

Sensitivity: nudge the input by a tiny amount hhh, and the output moves by approximately f′(x)⋅hf'(x) \cdot hf′(x)⋅h. The derivative is the exchange rate between input changes and output changes at that particular point. This second picture is the one deep learning runs on: “if I increase this weight by 0.0010.0010.001, the loss changes by about f′(w)×0.001f'(w) \times 0.001f′(w)×0.001.”

∑The math

The derivative of fff at xxx is defined as

f′(x)  =  lim⁡h→0f(x+h)−f(x)h,f'(x) \;=\; \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h},f′(x)=h→0lim​hf(x+h)−f(x)​,

where hhh is the size of the nudge we give the input, f(x+h)−f(x)f(x+h) - f(x)f(x+h)−f(x) is how much the output moved in response, and dividing by hhh converts that into a rate — output change per unit of input change. The limit takes the nudge to zero so we get the instantaneous rate, not an average over some finite step.

Let us compute one honestly, from this definition, for f(x)=x2f(x) = x^2f(x)=x2:

f(x+h)−f(x)h=(x+h)2−x2h=x2+2xh+h2−x2h=2xh+h2h=2x+h.\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = \frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h} = \frac{x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2}{h} = \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} = 2x + h.hf(x+h)−f(x)​=h(x+h)2−x2​=hx2+2xh+h2−x2​=h2xh+h2​=2x+h.

Every step is ordinary algebra: expand the square, cancel the x2x^2x2 terms, divide through by hhh (allowed, since hhh is small but not zero). We are left with 2x+h2x + h2x+h, and as h→0h \to 0h→0 this settles toward 2x2x2x. So

f(x)=x2⟹f′(x)=2x.f(x) = x^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f'(x) = 2x.f(x)=x2⟹f′(x)=2x.

The derivative of x2x^2x2 is not 2x2x2x by decree; it is 2x2x2x because the difference quotient works out to 2x+h2x + h2x+h and the hhh washes out. Every derivative rule you have ever memorized is a shortcut for a computation of exactly this kind.

The trend is committing to 666 — and notice the pattern 2x+h2x + h2x+h visibly sitting in the numbers: 6.1=6+0.16.1 = 6 + 0.16.1=6+0.1, and so on. The sensitivity reading: near x=3x=3x=3, the output of x2x^2x2 moves about 6×6\times6× as fast as the input.

The “zoom in until it’s a line” picture deserves its own name, because it is the single most load-bearing idea in this chapter: local linearity. A smooth function, viewed closely enough, is indistinguishable from the straight line through that point with slope f′(x)f'(x)f′(x). Written as a formula, for small hhh:

f(x+h)  ≈  f(x)+f′(x) h.f(x + h) \;\approx\; f(x) + f'(x)\,h.f(x+h)≈f(x)+f′(x)h.

This is the linear approximation, and it is the derivative’s entire job description: replace a complicated function, locally, with the one line that matches it. Gradient descent, backpropagation, and every optimizer in Chapter 11 are schemes for exploiting local linearity over and over.

⚠️Careful

The linear approximation is a local promise. It holds for small nudges and degrades as the step grows — the secant in the figure has slope ≈4.8\approx 4.8≈4.8, not 444, because over a big step the curve bends away from its tangent line. Gradient descent inherits this fine print: the gradient only guarantees “downhill” for a sufficiently small step, which is why the learning rate — the step size — will haunt every optimization chapter in this book. Take a step too large and “downhill” can land you uphill. You will see this happen, with actual numbers, at the end of this chapter.

The rules you need, and the two functions that run deep learning

Nobody computes derivatives from the limit definition in daily life; you compute a handful of building-block derivatives once, plus rules for combining them, and everything else follows. Here is the short table this book needs. Each entry can be verified from the limit definition exactly the way we verified x2x^2x2.

Power rule. For any constant nnn, ddxxn=n x n−1\frac{d}{dx} x^n = n\,x^{\,n-1}dxd​xn=nxn−1. Our x2→2xx^2 \to 2xx2→2x result is the case n=2n=2n=2; x3x^3x3 gives 3x23x^23x2; x=x1x = x^1x=x1 gives 111; a constant c=c x0c = c\,x^0c=cx0 gives 000 (constants don’t respond to nudges).

Sums and constant multiples. Derivatives pass through addition and scaling: (f+g)′=f′+g′(f + g)' = f' + g'(f+g)′=f′+g′ and (cf)′=cf′(c f)' = c f'(cf)′=cf′. So polynomials fall immediately: ddx(4w2−28w+49)=8w−28\frac{d}{dx}\left(4w^2 - 28w + 49\right) = 8w - 28dxd​(4w2−28w+49)=8w−28. Keep that particular example in mind — it reappears at the end of the chapter doing real work.

Product rule. (fg)′=f′g+fg′(fg)' = f'g + fg'(fg)′=f′g+fg′ — nudge effects from each factor add, each weighted by the other factor’s current value. Quotient rule (used once in this chapter, then rarely): (fg)′=f′g−fg′g2\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}(gf​)′=g2f′g−fg′​.

That covers the algebra. Now the two transcendental functions that between them power the output layer and the loss function of every large language model: the exponential and the logarithm.

∑The math

Why eee is special. Consider an exponential axa^xax for some base a>0a > 0a>0 and apply the limit definition:

ax+h−axh=axah−axh=ax⋅ah−1h.\frac{a^{x+h} - a^x}{h} = \frac{a^x a^h - a^x}{h} = a^x \cdot \frac{a^h - 1}{h}.hax+h−ax​=haxah−ax​=ax⋅hah−1​.

  • base a=2a = 2a=2:   20.001−10.001≈0.6934\;\frac{2^{0.001} - 1}{0.001} \approx 0.69340.00120.001−1​≈0.6934

ddx ex=ex,\frac{d}{dx}\, e^x = e^x,dxd​ex=ex,

The logarithm. ln⁡x\ln xlnx is the inverse of exe^xex (it answers ”eee to what power gives xxx?”), and mirroring the exponential’s self-similarity it has the memorably clean derivative

ddxln⁡x=1x.\frac{d}{dx} \ln x = \frac{1}{x}.dxd​lnx=x1​.

One way to see it: if y=ln⁡xy = \ln xy=lnx then ey=xe^y = xey=x; a nudge dydydy in yyy moves eye^yey by ey dye^y\,dyeydy (its derivative is itself), and for that to equal the nudge dxdxdx we need dy=dx/ey=dx/xdy = dx / e^y = dx / xdy=dx/ey=dx/x.

Why do these two functions matter so much here, three parts before we build a transformer? Because exe^xex is the engine of softmax — the operation that converts a model’s raw scores into probabilities, which you met briefly in Chapter 0 and will meet properly in the probability chapter — and softmax uses base eee precisely because exe^xex is its own derivative, which makes every gradient formula downstream dramatically cleaner. And ln⁡\lnln is the heart of the cross-entropy loss (Chapter 9) that every language model minimizes: “maximize the log-probability of the correct next token.” The derivative 1x\frac{1}{x}x1​ is why log-loss pushes hardest on predictions the model got confidently wrong — when the probability xxx assigned to the truth is tiny, 1x\frac{1}{x}x1​ is enormous.

One more derivative is worth deriving in full, partly for its own historical importance and partly because the derivation is a perfect exercise: the sigmoid function, the S-shaped squashing function that dominated neural networks for decades.

∑The math

The sigmoid is

σ(x)=11+e−x,\sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}},σ(x)=1+e−x1​,

which maps any real number into (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1) — large positive xxx gives outputs near 1, large negative near 0. To differentiate it we need ddxe−x=−e−x\frac{d}{dx} e^{-x} = -e^{-x}dxd​e−x=−e−x: a nudge to xxx enters through the negation first, flipping the sign. (This “derivative of the inside times derivative of the outside” move is the chain rule, the star of the next chapter — consider this a preview.) σ′(x)=0⋅g−1⋅(−e−x)(1+e−x)2=e−x(1+e−x)2.\sigma'(x) = \frac{0 \cdot g - 1 \cdot (-e^{-x})}{(1 + e^{-x})^2} = \frac{e^{-x}}{(1 + e^{-x})^2}.σ′(x)=(1+e−x)20⋅g−1⋅(−e−x)​=(1+e−x)2e−x​.

σ′(x)=11+e−x⋅e−x1+e−x=σ(x)⋅(1−σ(x)),\sigma'(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} \cdot \frac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}} = \sigma(x) \cdot \bigl(1 - \sigma(x)\bigr),σ′(x)=1+e−x1​⋅1+e−xe−x​=σ(x)⋅(1−σ(x)),

where the second factor is 1−σ1 - \sigma1−σ because 1−11+e−x=e−x1+e−x1 - \frac{1}{1+e^{-x}} = \frac{e^{-x}}{1+e^{-x}}1−1+e−x1​=1+e−xe−x​. At x=2x = 2x=2:   e−2≈0.1353\;e^{-2} \approx 0.1353e−2≈0.1353, so σ(2)=11.1353≈0.881\sigma(2) = \frac{1}{1.1353} \approx 0.881σ(2)=1.13531​≈0.881. Then

⚠️Careful

Look at the rose curve’s ceiling: the sigmoid’s derivative never exceeds 0.250.250.25, and in the tails it approaches zero. When the input is large in either direction, nudging it barely changes the output — the unit is saturated, and the sensitivity chain through it is nearly severed. Stack many sigmoid layers and these small factors multiply: ten saturating layers can shrink a gradient by a factor of 0.2510≈10−60.25^{10} \approx 10^{-6}0.2510≈10−6. This is the vanishing gradient problem, the villain of Chapter 12, and the reason modern networks largely replaced sigmoid with functions like the rectified linear unit (ReLU) and the Gaussian error linear unit (GELU) (Chapter 15) whose derivatives don’t collapse.

⚙️In practice

This little table is not a warm-up — it is close to the complete inventory. Inside a 2026 frontier model, the functions being differentiated are: linear maps (derivatives are constants), the power rule’s easy cases (x2x^2x2 in losses and normalization, x\sqrt{x}x​ in RMSNorm), exe^xex (softmax, in attention and the output layer), ln⁡\lnln (cross-entropy), and a couple of smooth activation functions with known closed-form derivatives. Frameworks like PyTorch ship exactly such a table — each primitive operation carries its derivative rule — and automatic differentiation (Chapter 10) composes them mechanically through the chain rule. When you hear that a model “computed gradients over a trillion parameters,” the calculus involved is the calculus on this page, applied at industrial scale.

Many knobs at once: partial derivatives

Everything so far had one input. A neural network’s loss has billions. The step from one to many is mercifully small, and it starts with a modest idea: if a function has several inputs, study one knob at a time while freezing the others.

Take f(x,y)=x2yf(x, y) = x^2 yf(x,y)=x2y, a function of two inputs. The partial derivative with respect to xxx, written ∂f∂x\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}∂x∂f​ (the curly ∂\partial∂ signals “other variables held fixed”), asks: with yyy frozen at its current value, how sensitive is fff to a nudge in xxx? While frozen, yyy is a constant, so we differentiate x2yx^2 yx2y as “constant times x2x^2x2”:

∂f∂x=2xy,∂f∂y=x2,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2xy, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x^2,∂x∂f​=2xy,∂y∂f​=x2,

where the second line freezes xxx instead, leaving “constant x2x^2x2 times yyy,” whose derivative in yyy is that constant. No new calculus was needed — only the discipline of holding everything else still.

✏️Worked example

Evaluate at the point (x,y)=(2,3)(x, y) = (2, 3)(x,y)=(2,3), where f(2,3)=4⋅3=12f(2,3) = 4 \cdot 3 = 12f(2,3)=4⋅3=12:

∂f∂x∣(2,3)=2⋅2⋅3=12,∂f∂y∣(2,3)=22=4.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Big|_{(2,3)} = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 = 12, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\Big|_{(2,3)} = 2^2 = 4.∂x∂f​​(2,3)​=2⋅2⋅3=12,∂y∂f​​(2,3)​=22=4.

Verify by nudging each knob by 0.010.010.01:

  • Nudge xxx: f(2.01, 3)=2.012×3=12.1203f(2.01,\, 3) = 2.01^2 \times 3 = 12.1203f(2.01,3)=2.012×3=12.1203. Change: 0.1203≈12×0.010.1203 \approx 12 \times 0.010.1203≈12×0.01. ✓
  • Nudge yyy: f(2, 3.01)=4×3.01=12.04f(2,\, 3.01) = 4 \times 3.01 = 12.04f(2,3.01)=4×3.01=12.04. Change: 0.04=4×0.010.04 = 4 \times 0.010.04=4×0.01. ✓

At this point, fff is three times more sensitive to xxx than to yyy. If you had one unit of “nudge budget” and wanted to increase fff, you now know which knob gives more leverage.

Collect the partial derivatives into a vector and you have the object this chapter is named for. For a function f(x1,x2,…,xn)f(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)f(x1​,x2​,…,xn​) of nnn inputs, the gradient is

∇f  =  (∂f∂x1,  ∂f∂x2,  …,  ∂f∂xn),\nabla f \;=\; \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1},\; \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2},\; \dots,\; \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} \right),∇f=(∂x1​∂f​,∂x2​∂f​,…,∂xn​∂f​),

pronounced “grad fff” or “nabla fff.” Two things about it are worth saying slowly. First, the gradient is a vector living in the input space — the space of knob settings — with one component per knob; for our example, ∇f(2,3)=(12,4)\nabla f(2,3) = (12, 4)∇f(2,3)=(12,4), an arrow in the xyxyxy-plane. Everything you learned about vectors in Chapter 1 — lengths, dot products, angles — now applies to it. Second, the gradient changes from point to point: it is a field of arrows, a local sensitivity report reissued at every location.

The linear approximation upgrades in the natural way. For a small nudge vector h=(h1,…,hn)\mathbf{h} = (h_1, \dots, h_n)h=(h1​,…,hn​),

f(x+h)  ≈  f(x)+∇f⋅h  =  f(x)+∂f∂x1h1+⋯+∂f∂xnhn,f(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{h}) \;\approx\; f(\mathbf{x}) + \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{h} \;=\; f(\mathbf{x}) + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} h_1 + \cdots + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} h_n,f(x+h)≈f(x)+∇f⋅h=f(x)+∂x1​∂f​h1​+⋯+∂xn​∂f​hn​,

where ∇f⋅h\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{h}∇f⋅h is the dot product. In words: each knob’s nudge contributes its own sensitivity-times-nudge, and for small nudges the contributions add. That additivity is not obvious — it is the multivariable form of local linearity, and it holds for the same reason as before: zoom in far enough on a smooth surface and it looks like a flat plane.

💡Intuition

Think of the gradient as a shopping list for improvement. You are tuning nnn knobs; the gradient hands you nnn numbers, one per knob: “turning this knob up changes the output at this rate.” Positive entries say increase me to increase fff; negative entries say decrease me to increase fff; entries near zero say don’t waste budget on me. A language model’s training step computes exactly this list — one entry per parameter, billions of entries — and then acts on every line of it simultaneously.

The most important theorem in deep learning

The gradient’s components tell you the payoff of nudging each knob alone. But nothing forces us to move along one axis at a time — we can step in any direction of the input space, moving many knobs in concert. Which direction is best? The answer is the theorem this whole book leans on.

First, a definition. The directional derivative of fff at a point x\mathbf{x}x, in the direction of a unit vector u\mathbf{u}u (length 1, so it encodes pure direction with no magnitude), is the rate of change of fff as you glide through x\mathbf{x}x along u\mathbf{u}u:

Duf  =  lim⁡h→0f(x+h u)−f(x)h.D_{\mathbf{u}} f \;=\; \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(\mathbf{x} + h\,\mathbf{u}) - f(\mathbf{x})}{h}.Du​f=h→0lim​hf(x+hu)−f(x)​.

It is the ordinary one-variable derivative of the function you’d experience walking the surface along that bearing.

∑The math

Claim: Duf=∇f⋅uD_{\mathbf{u}} f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}Du​f=∇f⋅u.

The linear approximation makes this almost immediate. Substituting the nudge h=h u\mathbf{h} = h\,\mathbf{u}h=hu into f(x+h)≈f(x)+∇f⋅hf(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{h}) \approx f(\mathbf{x}) + \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{h}f(x+h)≈f(x)+∇f⋅h:

f(x+hu)−f(x)  ≈  ∇f⋅(h u)  =  h (∇f⋅u),f(\mathbf{x} + h\mathbf{u}) - f(\mathbf{x}) \;\approx\; \nabla f \cdot (h\,\mathbf{u}) \;=\; h\, (\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}),f(x+hu)−f(x)≈∇f⋅(hu)=h(∇f⋅u),

so dividing by hhh gives ∇f⋅u\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}∇f⋅u, exactly, in the limit.

Now the punchline. Chapter 1 gave us the geometric form of the dot product: ∇f⋅u=∥∇f∥ ∥u∥cos⁡θ\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u} = \|\nabla f\|\,\|\mathbf{u}\| \cos\theta∇f⋅u=∥∇f∥∥u∥cosθ, where θ\thetaθ is the angle between the two vectors and ∥⋅∥\|\cdot\|∥⋅∥ denotes length. Since u\mathbf{u}u is a unit vector, ∥u∥=1\|\mathbf{u}\| = 1∥u∥=1 and

Duf  =  ∥∇f∥cos⁡θ.D_{\mathbf{u}} f \;=\; \|\nabla f\| \cos\theta.Du​f=∥∇f∥cosθ.

As u\mathbf{u}u sweeps around the compass, only cos⁡θ\cos\thetacosθ changes, and cosine is maximized — equal to 111 — precisely when θ=0\theta = 0θ=0, i.e. when u\mathbf{u}u points along ∇f\nabla f∇f itself. Therefore:

  1. The gradient points in the direction of steepest increase of fff.
  2. The steepest rate of increase is ∥∇f∥\|\nabla f\|∥∇f∥, the gradient’s length.
  3. Cosine is minimized at θ=180°\theta = 180°θ=180°: the direction of steepest decrease is −∇f-\nabla f−∇f, with rate −∥∇f∥-\|\nabla f\|−∥∇f∥.
  4. At θ=90°\theta = 90°θ=90°, the rate is zero: perpendicular to the gradient, fff momentarily doesn’t change at all.

Nothing about this argument cared that we drew it in two dimensions; it holds verbatim for a billion. To find the best direction among infinitely many in a billion-dimensional space, you do not search — you compute one vector of partial derivatives, and the geometry of the dot product hands you the optimum.

✏️Worked example

Back to f(x,y)=x2yf(x,y) = x^2 yf(x,y)=x2y at (2,3)(2,3)(2,3), with ∇f=(12,4)\nabla f = (12, 4)∇f=(12,4) and ∥∇f∥=122+42=160≈12.65\|\nabla f\| = \sqrt{12^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{160} \approx 12.65∥∇f∥=122+42​=160​≈12.65. Rates of change along various unit directions u\mathbf{u}u:

💡Intuition

Stand on a hillside in fog. You can’t see the peak or the valley — you can only feel the tilt of the ground under your feet. The gradient is that felt tilt: it points straight up the slope, its length says how steep, and its negation points straight down. Gradient descent is the humble strategy of a hiker in fog: repeatedly step downhill and trust that locally-down eventually adds up to globally-lower. The fog is not decoration in this metaphor — a model with 101110^{11}1011 parameters can never survey its loss landscape; the local tilt is all it will ever know.

Contour maps: seeing a surface from above

For functions of two variables there is a visualization that survives, as a mental model, into a billion dimensions: the contour map. Pick a function L(w1,w2)L(w_1, w_2)L(w1​,w2​) — think of it as a loss over two parameters — and draw, in the input plane, the set of points where LLL equals some constant value: L=1L = 1L=1, L=2L = 2L=2, and so on. Each such curve is a level set (or contour), exactly like the elevation lines on a topographic hiking map. Closely spaced contours mean steep terrain; widely spaced mean gentle; a closed loop shrinking to a point surrounds a peak or a pit.

The gradient has a crisp relationship to this picture. Walk along a contour: by definition, LLL does not change, so your rate of change is zero — DuL=∇L⋅u=0D_{\mathbf{u}} L = \nabla L \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0Du​L=∇L⋅u=0 for u\mathbf{u}u tangent to the contour. A zero dot product means perpendicular (Chapter 1 again). Conclusion: the gradient is always perpendicular to the level set through its point, crossing the contour lines at right angles like water running straight down a hillside.

Notice something about the figure’s elongated contours: they describe a valley that is much steeper across than along. The gradient, dutifully perpendicular to the contours, points mostly across the valley — not toward the minimum. Steepest descent on such terrain zigzags: each step overshoots across the narrow direction while crawling along the shallow one. This is not a flaw in the theorem — the gradient really is locally optimal — but “locally optimal direction” and “efficient route to the bottom” are different things. That gap, called ill-conditioning, motivates half the optimizer engineering in Chapter 11, and its proper mathematical language (curvature, second derivatives, the Hessian) arrives in the next chapter.

What “training” actually means

Time to cash out. A neural network’s loss function L(θ)L(\theta)L(θ) takes the full parameter vector θ=(θ1,…,θn)\theta = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_n)θ=(θ1​,…,θn​) — every weight in the model, flattened into one gargantuan vector — and returns a single number scoring how badly the model performs on data (for a language model: how poorly it predicts the next token, made precise in Chapter 9). Training is the search for parameter settings with low loss. Everything else in Parts II and V — architectures, data, optimizers, clusters of graphics processing units (GPUs) — is in service of this one search problem.

Calculus reframes the search. At a minimum, the surface is locally flat: no direction leads downhill, so every directional derivative is zero, so (taking the axis directions) every partial derivative is zero:

∇L(θ∗)=0.\nabla L(\theta^*) = \mathbf{0}.∇L(θ∗)=0.

Points where the gradient vanishes are called critical points (or stationary points). In your first calculus course this launched a tidy procedure: find the critical points, test each one, done. For a model with n≈1011n \approx 10^{11}n≈1011 parameters that procedure is a fantasy — ∇L=0\nabla L = \mathbf{0}∇L=0 is a system of a hundred billion coupled nonlinear equations with no hope of a closed-form solution. All we can do is start somewhere and walk downhill: which is what gradient descent is, and why the gradient theorem matters so much.

But the critical-point concept still earns its keep, because it tells us what the terrain looks like — and the terrain in high dimensions defies low-dimensional intuition. A critical point need not be a minimum. In one dimension it can be a minimum (curve bends up), a maximum (bends down), or a flat inflection. In many dimensions there is a new character, and it dominates: the saddle point, where the surface curves up along some directions and down along others — flat to first order, but not a resting place.

Here is the honest, modern account of what the loss terrain of a large network looks like — an account that flipped the field’s folklore in the mid-2010s.

The old fear was bad local minima: pits in the landscape that are far above the best achievable loss, where a downhill walker gets permanently stuck. In low dimensions this fear is entirely reasonable — sketch any wiggly curve and you’ll draw several. But high dimensions change the odds. Here is the heuristic (and it is a heuristic, not a theorem for real networks): at a critical point, each of the nnn independent directions curves either up or down. To be a local minimum, every one of the nnn directions must curve up. If curvature directions behaved like independent coin flips, the chance of all-heads would be 2−n2^{-n}2−n — and nnn is in the billions. Generic critical points at middling loss values are overwhelmingly saddles: up in some directions, down in others, escapable. Dauphin et al. (2014) made this argument carefully (via random matrix theory) and backed it empirically, arguing the real obstacle to optimization is the proliferation of saddle points, not local minima. Around the same time, Choromanska et al. (2014), borrowing tools from spin-glass physics, argued that in large networks most local minima concentrate in a narrow band of loss slightly above the global minimum — local minima exist, but the ones you can find are nearly as good as the best. And Goodfellow, Vinyals & Saxe (2014) ran a disarmingly direct experiment: draw a straight line in parameter space from a random initialization to a trained solution and plot the loss along it — for real networks it descends almost monotonically, no mountain ranges in between. Later, Li et al. (2018) produced the field’s iconic landscape visualizations and showed that architectural choices — notably the residual connections you’ll meet in Chapter 16 — visibly smooth the terrain.

✎Note

Intellectual honesty requires flagging the fine print. The spin-glass result assumes unrealistic independence conditions; the coin-flip heuristic is not a proof; and “no bad local minima in practice” is an empirical regularity, not a guarantee. What the field actually knows in 2026 is roughly: gradient-based training of large networks reliably finds low-loss regions, saddle points and flat plateaus slow it down more than pits trap it, and stochastic gradient noise (from computing gradients on random data batches — see Chapter 10) helps rattle the walker off saddles. Why the minima found this way also generalize to unseen data is still an open question, deferred to Chapter 13. The landscape story is one of deep learning’s areas where practice runs ahead of theory — the practice works; the full explanation is still being written.

⚙️In practice

Nobody in a 2026 training run “finds critical points” or checks second-order conditions. What actually happens: a run like the ones behind Llama-class, DeepSeek-class, or GPT-class models starts from carefully scaled random parameters (Chapter 12) and takes on the order of 10510^5105–10610^6106 optimizer steps — each one a gradient computed on a batch of data, fed through an optimizer such as AdamW or Muon (Chapter 11) that rescales the raw gradient direction. Training stops when the compute budget is spent or the loss curve flattens — not because ∇L=0\nabla L = \mathbf{0}∇L=0 was reached. The gradient at the end is small, never zero. “The model converged” is engineering shorthand for “the loss stopped improving enough to justify more GPU-hours,” a budget decision, not a mathematical certificate.

One descent step, by hand

Everything above compresses into a two-line recipe, the gradient descent update:

θnew  =  θold−η ∇L(θold),\theta_{\text{new}} \;=\; \theta_{\text{old}} - \eta \,\nabla L(\theta_{\text{old}}),θnew​=θold​−η∇L(θold​),

where η\etaη (eta) is the learning rate — the step size, a small positive number chosen by the practitioner. The minus sign is the entire theorem of this chapter in one character: −∇L-\nabla L−∇L is the direction of steepest decrease, so we move that way, scaled by η\etaη to stay inside the zone where local linearity holds. Let’s execute one step with real numbers, on the smallest model that deserves the name.

✏️Worked example

The model: y^=wx+b\hat{y} = wx + by^​=wx+b — predict y^\hat{y}y^​ from input xxx using two parameters, a weight www and a bias bbb. The data: one example, x=2x = 2x=2 with true answer y=7y = 7y=7. The loss: squared error, L(w,b)=(wx+b−y)2L(w, b) = (wx + b - y)^2L(w,b)=(wx+b−y)2, which for our data point is L=(2w+b−7)2L = (2w + b - 7)^2L=(2w+b−7)2. Starting parameters: w=1w = 1w=1, b=0b = 0b=0.

Forward pass. Prediction: y^=1⋅2+0=2\hat{y} = 1 \cdot 2 + 0 = 2y^​=1⋅2+0=2. Error: 2−7=−52 - 7 = -52−7=−5. Loss: (−5)2=25(-5)^2 = 25(−5)2=25.

Gradient. Expand the loss into a polynomial so our power rule can act directly (the chain rule will make this expansion unnecessary next chapter):

L=(2w+b−7)2=4w2+b2+4wb−28w−14b+49.L = (2w + b - 7)^2 = 4w^2 + b^2 + 4wb - 28w - 14b + 49.L=(2w+b−7)2=4w2+b2+4wb−28w−14b+49.

Partial derivatives — differentiate treating the other variable as a constant:

∂L∂w=8w+4b−28,∂L∂b=2b+4w−14.\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = 8w + 4b - 28, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = 2b + 4w - 14.∂w∂L​=8w+4b−28,∂b∂L​=2b+4w−14.

At (w,b)=(1,0)(w, b) = (1, 0)(w,b)=(1,0):   ∂L∂w=8−28=−20\;\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = 8 - 28 = -20∂w∂L​=8−28=−20 and ∂L∂b=4−14=−10\frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = 4 - 14 = -10∂b∂L​=4−14=−10. So ∇L=(−20,−10)\nabla L = (-20, -10)∇L=(−20,−10): the loss decreases if we increase either parameter (negative sensitivities), and it’s twice as responsive to www as to bbb — which makes sense, since www is amplified by the input x=2x = 2x=2 on its way to the output.

Update, with learning rate η=0.05\eta = 0.05η=0.05:

w←1−0.05×(−20)=2,b←0−0.05×(−10)=0.5.w \leftarrow 1 - 0.05 \times (-20) = 2, \qquad b \leftarrow 0 - 0.05 \times (-10) = 0.5.w←1−0.05×(−20)=2,b←0−0.05×(−10)=0.5.

Did it work? New prediction: 2⋅2+0.5=4.52 \cdot 2 + 0.5 = 4.52⋅2+0.5=4.5. New error: −2.5-2.5−2.5. New loss: 6.256.256.25. One step took the loss from 252525 to 6.256.256.25 — a 75% drop, from calculus and arithmetic alone. Steps two, three, and beyond repeat the same motions from the new position, and the sequence converges toward parameters that fit the data.

Now watch the fine print bite. Rerun the step with an aggressive η=0.5\eta = 0.5η=0.5:   w←1+10=11\;w \leftarrow 1 + 10 = 11w←1+10=11,   b←0+5=5\;b \leftarrow 0 + 5 = 5b←0+5=5. Prediction: 272727. Loss: (27−7)2=400(27 - 7)^2 = 400(27−7)2=400 — sixteen times worse than where we started. The gradient’s downhill promise is local; a huge step drove straight through the valley floor and far up the opposite wall. Every real training run lives between these two failure modes — too timid and training takes forever, too bold and the loss diverges.

💡Intuition

Now scale the picture up, changing nothing conceptually. Replace 2 parameters with ∼1011\sim 10^{11}∼1011 (frontier-model scale — treat the exact count as fast-moving and approximate). Replace our single (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) pair with batches of text drawn from trillions of tokens. Replace squared error with cross-entropy on next-token prediction. Replace our hand-computed partials with backpropagation, which computes all 101110^{11}1011 of them for roughly the cost of running the model twice. Repeat for a million steps on tens of thousands of GPUs. That — plus an enormous amount of engineering to keep it stable, which occupies the rest of this book — is what “training GPT” means. The step you computed by hand is the atom of the whole process.

🕰History

The derivative was forged in the 1660s–1680s by Newton and Leibniz (independently, with a famously bitter priority dispute); Leibniz’s notation dydx\frac{dy}{dx}dxdy​ is the one that stuck. The gradient and the observation that −∇f-\nabla f−∇f is the steepest descent direction were classical by the mid-1800s, and Cauchy proposed iterating small steps along −∇f-\nabla f−∇f as a practical method in 1847. So the mathematical core of “training” predates electricity, let alone GPUs. What changed everything was scale and automation: an efficient algorithm for computing gradients through deep compositions (backpropagation, popularized for neural networks in 1986) and hardware fast enough to repeat it at the scale of 102510^{25}1025 floating-point operations per training run (a fast-moving, approximate figure). For a well-animated companion to this chapter’s ideas, see 3Blue1Brown’s Essence of Calculus series.

Where this leaves us

  • You can state what a derivative is two ways — the slope of the local linear approximation, and the sensitivity f(x+h)≈f(x)+f′(x)hf(x+h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)hf(x+h)≈f(x)+f′(x)h — and you computed ddxx2=2x\frac{d}{dx}x^2 = 2xdxd​x2=2x from the limit definition, with no step skipped.
  • You hold the working table of rules — power, sum, product, quotient — plus the three special functions that run deep learning: exe^xex (its own derivative, and why that singles out eee), ln⁡x\ln xlnx (derivative 1/x1/x1/x), and σ(x)\sigma(x)σ(x) (derivative σ(1−σ)\sigma(1-\sigma)σ(1−σ), derived, with its 0.250.250.25 ceiling foreshadowing vanishing gradients).
  • Partial derivatives are one-knob-at-a-time derivatives; the gradient ∇f\nabla f∇f stacks them into a vector in parameter space, and f(x+h)≈f(x)+∇f⋅hf(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{h}) \approx f(\mathbf{x}) + \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{h}f(x+h)≈f(x)+∇f⋅h.
  • You proved the central theorem: Duf=∇f⋅u=∥∇f∥cos⁡θD_{\mathbf{u}} f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u} = \|\nabla f\|\cos\thetaDu​f=∇f⋅u=∥∇f∥cosθ, so the gradient points in the steepest-ascent direction, −∇f-\nabla f−∇f in the steepest-descent direction — in any number of dimensions, courtesy of the dot product.
  • You can read a contour map: gradients cross level sets at right angles, and elongated contours mean steepest-descent zigzags — the seed of the optimizer chapter.
  • Training means seeking low L(θ)L(\theta)L(θ); critical points satisfy ∇L=0\nabla L = \mathbf{0}∇L=0, but in high dimensions they are mostly saddles, bad local minima appear rare in practice, and real runs stop at “small gradient, budget spent,” never at exact zero.
  • You executed one full gradient descent step by hand — forward pass, gradient, update, verification — and watched an oversized learning rate turn descent into disaster.

One tool is conspicuously missing: our loss was expanded by brute-force algebra because we couldn’t yet differentiate through a composition — and a neural network is nothing but a deep composition of functions.
