What a Language Model Is

A language model is a probability distribution over sequences of tokens — this chapter builds that definition from scratch, derives the autoregressive factorization and the training objective, and walks the road from n-grams to base models.

  1. A probability distribution over sequences
  2. The autoregressive factorization
  3. The generation loop
  4. Training: cross-entropy is maximum likelihood
  5. Before neural networks: counting
  6. Neural language models: representations replace counts
  7. What a base model is — and is not
  8. Where this leaves us

Every chapter before this one built machinery: vectors, gradients, losses, networks that can be trained. This chapter finally points that machinery at its target. What, precisely, is the object that GPT-5 and Claude and DeepSeek are? Not “an AI”, not “a chatbot” — what is the mathematical object, the thing with a definition you could write on one line?

Here is the line: a language model is a probability distribution over sequences of tokens. That is the entire definition. Everything in the rest of this book — attention, transformers, trillion-token training runs, reinforcement learning from human feedback — is machinery for making that distribution good and for drawing samples from it quickly. But the definition itself is one line of probability theory, and if you internalize it properly, a surprising amount of modern AI stops being mysterious. Why do models “hallucinate”? Why does the same prompt give different answers? Why does a raw pretrained model complete your question with more questions instead of answering it? Why is “predict the next word” enough to produce something that can write code? All of these have answers that fall directly out of the definition, and this chapter collects them.

We will build the definition slowly, derive the two equations that power everything (the autoregressive factorization and the cross-entropy training objective), work through the pre-neural history so you can feel why neural networks were needed, and end with an honest description of what a freshly pretrained “base model” actually is — which is stranger, and more illuminating, than the chat products built on top of it.

One housekeeping note before we start: throughout this chapter we say token for the units a model reads and writes. For now you can mentally substitute “word” almost everywhere; the real story — tokens are subword chunks produced by an algorithm called byte-pair encoding — is the entire subject of the next chapter.

A probability distribution over sequences

Start with a vocabulary VVV: a finite set of tokens, say ∣V∣=50,000|V| = 50{,}000∣V∣=50,000 of them. A sequence is any finite list of tokens from VVV. A language model is a function PPP that assigns a probability to every such sequence:

P(x1,x2,…,xT)∈[0,1],P(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_T) \in [0, 1],P(x1​,x2​,…,xT​)∈[0,1],

where x1,…,xTx_1, \dots, x_Tx1​,…,xT​ are the tokens of the sequence and TTT is its length, with the probabilities over all possible sequences summing to 1. That is the whole object. A language model does not “contain facts” or “store sentences” in any direct sense; it is a gigantic assignment of numbers to word-strings.

What should those numbers look like? Text that people produce should get more probability than text they don’t:

P(the cat sat)>P(cat the sat)>P(sat sat sat sat).P(\text{the cat sat}) > P(\text{cat the sat}) > P(\text{sat sat sat sat}).P(the cat sat)>P(cat the sat)>P(sat sat sat sat).

A good model of English assigns relatively high probability to grammatical, sensible, contextually coherent text and vanishingly small probability to word salad. Notice the modest phrasing: relatively high. Even a great sentence has minuscule absolute probability, because there are so many sentences. With ∣V∣=50,000|V| = 50{,}000∣V∣=50,000, the number of distinct sequences of length 10 is 50,00010≈9.8×104650{,}000^{10} \approx 9.8 \times 10^{46}50,00010≈9.8×1046 — comfortably more than the number of atoms in every human body on Earth combined. The probability mass of 1.0 has to be spread across all of them (and across every other length too). A specific, perfectly ordinary 10-word sentence might receive probability around 10−2010^{-20}10−20 from an excellent model, and that’s a compliment — the model concentrated its mass astronomically compared to a uniform guess of 10−4710^{-47}10−47.

💡Intuition

Think of a language model as a native speaker’s ear, made quantitative. A fluent English speaker, shown “the cat sat on the ___”, feels that mat, sofa, and windowsill are live options, the is unlikely, and purple is nearly impossible. They couldn’t tell you numbers, but the graded sense of “what could come next” is real, and it reflects everything they know — grammar, word meanings, physics (cats sit on things with surfaces), culture (the cat sat on the mat cliché). A language model is that same graded sense with actual numbers attached. Making the numbers good forces the model to acquire the knowledge, because grammar, meaning, and facts are exactly what make some continuations likelier than others.

Why probability, and not rules

You might reasonably ask: why is the fundamental object a probability distribution? Why not a grammar — a set of rules that says which sentences are valid? Linguists spent decades on exactly that program, and it fails for a deep reason: the uncertainty in language is irreducible. After “My favorite color is”, there is no fact of the matter about the next word. Blue is likelier than chartreuse, but both are legitimate, and any system that must pick a single “correct” continuation has already misunderstood the problem. Contrast “The capital of France is”, where Paris deserves nearly all of the probability mass — but even there, “actually a subject of trivia disputes” is a possible continuation in some document, somewhere. Language offers a spectrum from near-certainty to genuine openness, and a probability distribution is the only kind of object that can represent that spectrum honestly.

Probability also hands us generation for free. If you have a distribution over sequences, you can sample from it — draw a sequence at random, with likely sequences drawn often and unlikely ones rarely. A sample from a good model of English is a plausible English text. This identity — generation = sampling — is worth staring at, because it inverts how most people imagine AI text generation. The model is not retrieving an answer, consulting a database, or executing a plan. It is rolling weighted dice, where the weights encode everything it learned. It also immediately explains two everyday observations: the same prompt yields different outputs on different runs (independent samples differ), and models sometimes assert falsehoods fluently (a false-but-plausible sentence can carry real probability mass — the distribution models what text looks like, and confident false statements look exactly like confident true ones). We’ll return to hallucination in the evaluation chapter; here it’s enough to see that it is not a malfunction of the machinery but a property of the objective.

The autoregressive factorization

There is an immediate engineering problem with “a distribution over all sequences”: the object is unmanageably large. We can’t tabulate 104710^{47}1047 numbers, and no neural network can output them directly. We need to break the joint distribution into pieces a network can compute.

The tool is one you already own. In the probability chapter we met the chain rule of probability: for any two events, P(A,B)=P(A) P(B∣A)\P(A, B) = \P(A)\,\P(B \mid A)P(A,B)=P(A)P(B∣A) — the probability of both is the probability of the first times the probability of the second given the first. Nothing about that rule is specific to two events; apply it repeatedly and any joint distribution unrolls into a chain of conditionals.

∑The math

Take a sequence of three tokens (x1,x2,x3)(x_1, x_2, x_3)(x1​,x2​,x3​). Apply the chain rule to split off the last token:

P(x1,x2,x3)=P(x1,x2)  P(x3∣x1,x2).P(x_1, x_2, x_3) = P(x_1, x_2)\; P(x_3 \mid x_1, x_2).P(x1​,x2​,x3​)=P(x1​,x2​)P(x3​∣x1​,x2​).

Apply it again to the remaining pair:

P(x1,x2)=P(x1)  P(x2∣x1).P(x_1, x_2) = P(x_1)\; P(x_2 \mid x_1).P(x1​,x2​)=P(x1​)P(x2​∣x1​).

Substituting back:

P(x1,x2,x3)=P(x1)  P(x2∣x1)  P(x3∣x1,x2).P(x_1, x_2, x_3) = P(x_1)\; P(x_2 \mid x_1)\; P(x_3 \mid x_1, x_2).P(x1​,x2​,x3​)=P(x1​)P(x2​∣x1​)P(x3​∣x1​,x2​).

The same argument, applied T−1T-1T−1 times, gives the general statement for any sequence length:

P(x1,…,xT)  =  ∏t=1TP(xt∣x1,…,xt−1),P(x_1, \dots, x_T) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^{T} P(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1}),P(x1​,…,xT​)=t=1∏T​P(xt​∣x1​,…,xt−1​),

where the t=1t = 1t=1 factor is read as P(x1)P(x_1)P(x1​) conditioned on nothing (an empty prefix). Each factor P(xt∣x1,…,xt−1)P(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1})P(xt​∣x1​,…,xt−1​) is the distribution of the next token given everything so far — a distribution over only ∣V∣|V|∣V∣ outcomes, which is exactly the size of thing a neural network with a softmax output layer can produce.

Models that use this decomposition — predicting each token from the tokens before it — are called autoregressive (“regressing on themselves”: each output becomes part of the next input). Two things about this factorization deserve celebration, and the second is routinely misunderstood.

First, it converts an impossible problem into a familiar one. “Represent a distribution over 104710^{47}1047 sequences” becomes “build a function that maps a prefix to a distribution over 50,000 next tokens” — and that is a classification problem, the kind of thing we’ve been training networks to do since the multi-layer perceptron (MLP) chapter. Input: a variable-length prefix. Output: ∣V∣|V|∣V∣ scores, softmaxed into probabilities. The entire language modeling enterprise reduces to building a very good next-token classifier.

Second — and this is the misunderstood part — the factorization is exact. It is an identity from probability theory, not a modeling assumption. Any distribution over sequences, including the true distribution of human text in all its long-range, discourse-level, callback-to-chapter-one glory, can be written this way without losing anything. When critics say “next-token prediction is too myopic to capture real language”, they are aiming at the wrong target: the chain rule guarantees that perfectly modeling next tokens is perfectly modeling language. The approximation enters elsewhere — in the learned conditionals. Our network’s Pθ(xt∣x1,…,xt−1)P_\theta(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1})Pθ​(xt​∣x1​,…,xt−1​), with parameters θ\thetaθ, is only an approximation of the true conditional, limited by the model’s capacity, its training data, and its context length. The factorization is innocent; the fitted factors are where reality bites.

✏️Worked example

Let’s put numbers on the figure. Suppose our model assigns P(the)=0.05P(\text{the}) = 0.05P(the)=0.05 (given an empty prefix, “the” is a common opener), P(cat∣the)=0.02P(\text{cat} \mid \text{the}) = 0.02P(cat∣the)=0.02, P(sat∣the cat)=0.15P(\text{sat} \mid \text{the cat}) = 0.15P(sat∣the cat)=0.15, and P(end∣the cat sat)=0.30P(\text{end} \mid \text{the cat sat}) = 0.30P(end∣the cat sat)=0.30, where “end” is a special end-of-sequence token that lets the model say “this text is finished”. Then

P(the cat sat)=0.05×0.02×0.15×0.30=4.5×10−5.P(\text{the cat sat}) = 0.05 \times 0.02 \times 0.15 \times 0.30 = 4.5 \times 10^{-5}.P(the cat sat)=0.05×0.02×0.15×0.30=4.5×10−5.

Two lessons hide in this tiny arithmetic. First, probabilities shrink fast under multiplication — a 40-token sentence multiplies 40 numbers each below 1, so real systems work with log probabilities, turning the product into a sum: log⁡P=log⁡0.05+log⁡0.02+log⁡0.15+log⁡0.30≈−3.00−3.91−1.90−1.20=−10.01\log P = \log 0.05 + \log 0.02 + \log 0.15 + \log 0.30 \approx -3.00 - 3.91 - 1.90 - 1.20 = -10.01logP=log0.05+log0.02+log0.15+log0.30≈−3.00−3.91−1.90−1.20=−10.01 (natural logs). Second, every factor matters: swap “cat” for a token the model finds bizarre there, with conditional probability 10−710^{-7}10−7, and the whole sequence’s probability collapses by five orders of magnitude. A sequence is only as probable as its least expected step.

The generation loop

The factorization also tells us how to sample. To draw a sequence from the joint distribution, draw each token from its conditional in turn:

context ← the prompt (possibly empty)
repeat:
    p ← model(context)          # distribution over all |V| next tokens
    x ← sample from p           # roll the weighted die
    append x to context
until x is the end token (or a length limit is hit)

Predict, sample, append, repeat. Each freshly sampled token is fed back in as input for the next prediction — the model consumes its own output, which is the “auto” in autoregressive.

Now let’s say the strange part plainly, because it deserves to be said plainly. This loop is everything. When ChatGPT writes you a working Python script, it is running this loop. When Claude drafts a contract clause, plans a trip, or declines to help with something dangerous — this loop. There is no separate planning module, no lookup step, no distinct “reasoning mode” bolted onto the side; there is a next-token distribution and a die, iterated. The model never sees its whole answer in advance. It commits to token 1 without “knowing” token 50, and whatever foresight its outputs display must somehow be implicit in the conditionals — the distribution over the next token must already reflect where a good answer is heading. That a loop this simple produces behavior that coherent is, depending on your temperament, the most exciting or the most unsettling fact in this book. It’s also why interpretability, Part VII’s subject, exists: the loop is transparent, but the conditionals are computed by billions of parameters we did not program and do not fully understand.

✎Note

“Sample from ppp” hides a family of choices — temperature (which you met with softmax in Chapter 0), top-k and nucleus sampling (Holtzman et al., 2019), or greedy decoding (always take the argmax, which makes output deterministic). These knobs matter a great deal in practice and get their full treatment in the inference chapter. For this chapter, plain sampling from the model’s distribution is the conceptually honest picture.

Training: cross-entropy is maximum likelihood

We have the shape of the model: a network Pθ(xt∣x1:t−1)P_\theta(x_t \mid x_{1:t-1})Pθ​(xt​∣x1:t−1​) mapping prefixes to next-token distributions, with parameters θ\thetaθ. How do we train it? This is where three earlier chapters — probability, information theory, and loss functions — click together into a single derivation.

The guiding principle is maximum likelihood estimation (MLE): choose the parameters under which the training data was most probable. If the corpus is real human text, a model that assigns it high probability is a model whose distribution has moved toward the true distribution of human text.

∑The math

Treat the training corpus as one long token sequence x1,…,xNx_1, \dots, x_Nx1​,…,xN​ (NNN in the trillions for frontier models). Its likelihood under the model is given by the autoregressive factorization:

L(θ)=∏t=1NPθ(xt∣x1:t−1).L(\theta) = \prod_{t=1}^{N} P_\theta(x_t \mid x_{1:t-1}).L(θ)=t=1∏N​Pθ​(xt​∣x1:t−1​).

Products of trillions of sub-1 numbers underflow any computer, and sums are nicer to differentiate than products, so we maximize the logarithm instead (legitimate because log⁡\loglog is monotonic — it preserves which θ\thetaθ wins):

log⁡L(θ)=∑t=1Nlog⁡Pθ(xt∣x1:t−1).\log L(\theta) = \sum_{t=1}^{N} \log P_\theta(x_t \mid x_{1:t-1}).logL(θ)=t=1∑N​logPθ​(xt​∣x1:t−1​).

Optimizers minimize, so flip the sign and average over tokens to get the loss:

L(θ)=−1N∑t=1Nlog⁡Pθ(xt∣x1:t−1).\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \log P_\theta(x_t \mid x_{1:t-1}).L(θ)=−N1​t=1∑N​logPθ​(xt​∣x1:t−1​).

Look closely: each term is the negative log probability the model assigned to the correct next token — exactly the cross-entropy loss from the loss functions chapter, applied to a ∣V∣|V|∣V∣-way classification problem at every position. Next-token training is not “like” classification; it is classification, NNN times over.

Information theory tells us what the minimum is. As shown in Chapter 7, cross-entropy decomposes as H(p,q)=H(p)+DKL(p ∥ q)H(p, q) = H(p) + \KL(p \,\|\, q)H(p,q)=H(p)+DKL​(p∥q): the entropy of the true distribution ppp plus the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence from the model qqq to it. Minimizing our loss therefore drives DKL→0\KL \to 0DKL​→0 — the model distribution toward the true one — but can never push below H(p)H(p)H(p), the entropy of language itself: the irreducible unpredictability of what humans will say next. Shannon estimated in 1951 that printed English carries roughly 1 bit per character of genuine surprise (his estimated range was about 0.6–1.3). No model, however large, gets below the floor; training is a race toward it.

✏️Worked example

A three-token “corpus”: the model sees “the cat sat” and assigns the correct tokens probabilities 0.050.050.05, 0.300.300.30, 0.200.200.20 at their respective positions (conditioned on their true prefixes). The loss is

L=−13(ln⁡0.05+ln⁡0.30+ln⁡0.20)=−13(−3.00−1.20−1.61)≈1.94 nats/token.\mathcal{L} = -\tfrac{1}{3}\left(\ln 0.05 + \ln 0.30 + \ln 0.20\right) = -\tfrac{1}{3}\left(-3.00 - 1.20 - 1.61\right) \approx 1.94 \text{ nats/token}.L=−31​(ln0.05+ln0.30+ln0.20)=−31​(−3.00−1.20−1.61)≈1.94 nats/token.

Exponentiating gives e1.94≈6.9e^{1.94} \approx 6.9e1.94≈6.9 — the model’s perplexity, which you can read as “at each step, the model was as uncertain as if choosing uniformly among about 7 tokens”. Perplexity is the standard report card for language models and gets its own treatment in the evaluation chapter. For scale: strong 2026-era models reach low single digits on held-out web text, against a vocabulary of 100,000-plus options per step.

Teacher forcing

One subtlety in how the loss is computed matters enormously for efficiency. At every position ttt, the model is asked to predict xtx_txt​ from the ground-truth prefix x1:t−1x_{1:t-1}x1:t−1​ — the real text from the corpus — never from its own earlier predictions. This regime is called teacher forcing: like a piano teacher who corrects each wrong note before you play the next one, the training process resets the model onto the true text after every prediction, no matter what it would have guessed.

Teacher forcing is what makes training parallelizable. Since position ttt‘s prediction depends only on the fixed corpus prefix, not on the model’s own outputs, all NNN predictions are independent given the text — a single forward pass over a training sequence scores every position at once, and every token contributes a gradient signal. One 2,048-token document yields 2,048 classification problems in one pass. (How a transformer computes all of them simultaneously without letting position ttt peek at the future is the causal-masking story of the attention chapters.)

⚠️Careful

Train/generate mismatch. Teacher forcing means the model trains exclusively on human-written prefixes but generates from prefixes that are increasingly its own past output. If sampling ever wanders somewhere low-probability — an awkward phrase, a wrong fact — the model finds itself conditioned on a prefix unlike anything training prepared it for, and errors can compound token by token. This is the classic exposure bias critique of teacher forcing. How much it hurts modern large models is debated — scale and diverse data blunt it substantially, and proposed fixes (scheduled sampling, sequence-level training) never paid their way at scale — but the asymmetry is real, and it is one reason post-training, which does train on the model’s own generations, changes model behavior so much.

Before neural networks: counting

The definition of a language model is from the 1940s; neural networks are only the latest way to build one. Walking through the earlier way — the n-gram model — is the fastest route to understanding why neural language models exist, and what problem embeddings were invented to solve.

The full conditional P(xt∣x1,…,xt−1)P(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1})P(xt​∣x1​,…,xt−1​) conditions on an unbounded prefix. The n-gram move is to truncate: assume the next token depends only on the previous n−1n-1n−1 tokens (a Markov assumption — the finite-memory idea from the probability chapter):

P(xt∣x1,…,xt−1)  ≈  P(xt∣xt−n+1,…,xt−1).P(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1}) \;\approx\; P(x_t \mid x_{t-n+1}, \dots, x_{t-1}).P(xt​∣x1​,…,xt−1​)≈P(xt​∣xt−n+1​,…,xt−1​).

A bigram model (n=2n=2n=2) looks back one token; a trigram (n=3n=3n=3) looks back two. And with the context chopped down to something finite, the conditionals can be estimated by counting: the MLE for a bigram probability is exactly its relative frequency in the corpus,

P(w∣v)=count⁡(v,w)count⁡(v),P(w \mid v) = \frac{\operatorname{count}(v, w)}{\operatorname{count}(v)},P(w∣v)=count(v)count(v,w)​,

the fraction of times token vvv was followed by token www.

✏️Worked example

Toy corpus, three sentences: “the cat sat”, “the cat ran”, “the dog sat”. Counts: “the” appears 3 times, followed by “cat” twice and “dog” once, so

P(cat∣the)=23,P(dog∣the)=13.P(\text{cat} \mid \text{the}) = \tfrac{2}{3}, \qquad P(\text{dog} \mid \text{the}) = \tfrac{1}{3}.P(cat∣the)=32​,P(dog∣the)=31​.

“cat” appears twice, followed once each by “sat” and “ran”: P(sat∣cat)=P(ran∣cat)=12P(\text{sat} \mid \text{cat}) = P(\text{ran} \mid \text{cat}) = \tfrac{1}{2}P(sat∣cat)=P(ran∣cat)=21​. The bigram probability of the sentence “the cat sat” (with every sentence starting with “the”, so P(the∣start)=1P(\text{the} \mid \text{start}) = 1P(the∣start)=1) is then 1×23×12=131 \times \tfrac{2}{3} \times \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{3}1×32​×21​=31​.

Now the failure: what is P(ran∣dog)P(\text{ran} \mid \text{dog})P(ran∣dog)? The bigram “dog ran” never occurs, so the count — and the probability — is zero. The model declares “the dog ran” impossible, a perfectly ordinary sentence executed by a missing table entry. And one zero factor zeroes out any sequence containing it, no matter how good the rest is.

This zero-count problem is not an edge case; at realistic scale it is the whole story. Count the possibilities: with ∣V∣=50,000|V| = 50{,}000∣V∣=50,000, there are 2.5×1092.5 \times 10^92.5×109 possible bigrams, 1.25×10141.25 \times 10^{14}1.25×1014 trigrams, and 3.1×10233.1 \times 10^{23}3.1×1023 5-grams. A billion-token corpus contains at most a billion distinct n-grams of each order — so for 5-grams, you can have observed at most a 3.2×10−133.2 \times 10^{-13}3.2×10−13 percent sliver of the space. Nearly every grammatical 5-gram a user might produce has never been seen and gets probability zero. This is the curse of dimensionality wearing a linguistic costume: the space of contexts grows exponentially in nnn, while data grows linearly in corpus size.

The n-gram era’s answer was smoothing: shave probability mass off seen n-grams and redistribute it to unseen ones. Decades of ingenuity went into how — add-one counting, backoff to shorter contexts, interpolation across orders — culminating in Kneser–Ney smoothing, whose clever move was to back off not to how frequent a word is but to how many distinct contexts it appears in. (Chen and Goodman’s 1999 empirical study is the definitive tour, and crowned Kneser–Ney.) Smoothed n-grams powered real products — speech recognizers, spell checkers, the early Google Translate — into the 2010s. But smoothing treats a symptom. The disease is that counting cannot generalize: to an n-gram model, “cat” and “dog” are unrelated symbols, so evidence about one says nothing about the other. Having seen “the cat sat”, the model gains no confidence whatsoever about “the dog sat”.

🕰History

The prehistory runs deeper than the n-gram products. Claude Shannon’s 1951 paper had people guess the next letter of hidden text, using their error rates to estimate the entropy of English — humans were, in effect, the first language models, and “predict the next symbol” was the founding experimental method of information theory, not a 2018 invention. Shannon’s 1948 paper had already generated text from letter- and word-level n-gram statistics, producing gems like “THE HEAD AND IN FRONTAL ATTACK ON AN ENGLISH WRITER” — recognizably English-shaped nonsense, the great-grandparent of every AI-generated sentence you’ve read.

Neural language models: representations replace counts

The escape came in Bengio et al.’s 2003 “A Neural Probabilistic Language Model”, a paper whose core idea now underlies every system in this book. Instead of treating words as unrelated symbols to count, learn a vector for each word, and compute next-token probabilities with a neural network applied to the context’s vectors. Each word gets a point in a continuous space (they used 30 to 100 dimensions); the network maps the concatenated context vectors through a hidden layer to a softmax over the vocabulary; and everything — the network weights and the word vectors themselves — is trained end to end by gradient descent on exactly the cross-entropy objective we derived above.

Why does this beat counting? Because similar words end up with similar vectors, and then the network’s smoothness does the generalizing. Bengio’s own example: having seen “the cat is walking in the bedroom” in training, the model transfers probability to “a dog was running in a room” — a sentence sharing not one bigram with the original — because cat sits near dog, walking near running, bedroom near room. A count-based model sees five novel n-grams and shrugs; the neural model sees a familiar sentence in a lightly rotated part of space. One learned representation quietly does the work of the exponentially many table entries the n-gram model could never fill. This is the birth of the embedding, the idea that gets its own chapter (Chapter 20) and that you already met in geometric form in the vectors chapter: meaning lives in directions of high-dimensional space.

The next act you already know from the chapter on convolutional and recurrent neural networks (CNNs and RNNs). Bengio’s model still had a fixed context window (it was, in effect, a neural n-gram model); Mikolov et al.’s 2010 recurrent network language model removed the truncation entirely, carrying an unbounded (in principle) context in a hidden state and beating state-of-the-art smoothed n-grams in speech recognition. Through the mid-2010s, long short-term memory (LSTM) language models — and playful demonstrations like Karpathy’s char-RNN post, which generated Shakespeare, Linux kernel code, and fake math papers character by character — made it obvious that neural sequence models capture real structure. But RNNs read text through the keyhole of a fixed-size hidden state, one token at a time, in order — hard to parallelize, and prone to forgetting. The architecture that fixed both problems, the transformer, is the subject of Part IV; what matters here is that it changed how the conditionals are computed, not one word of this chapter’s definitions. The probability framework, the factorization, the cross-entropy objective — all of it transfers unchanged from 1951 to 2026.

⚙️In practice

Every frontier model of the 2020s — the GPT series, Claude, Gemini, Llama, DeepSeek-V3, Qwen, Mistral — is pretrained with precisely this recipe: autoregressive factorization over subword tokens, next-token cross-entropy, teacher forcing, on corpora of roughly 101310^{13}1013 tokens (Llama 3 reported about 15 trillion; frontier figures are approximate and fast-moving). The practical differences lie in the conditionals’ architecture and scale, the data mixture (Chapter 31), and what happens after pretraining. A few research alternatives to the left-to-right factorization exist — masked prediction for encoders (Chapter 27) and text diffusion models that denoise whole sequences at once — but as of 2026, autoregression remains the backbone of every deployed frontier system.

What a base model is — and is not

Run the recipe above at scale and what you get is a base model (or “pretrained model”): the raw artifact of next-token training, before any of the assistant-making steps. Understanding how a base model behaves is the single best inoculation against magical thinking about chatbots — and the behavior follows, once again, straight from the definition.

A base model is a document completer. Its training distribution is “text from the internet and books”, so given any prefix, it produces a plausible continuation of that document — not a response, not help, a continuation. The distinction sounds subtle and is anything but. Prompt a base model with

What is the capital of France?

and a common completion is more questions — “What is the capital of Spain? What is the capital of Italy?” — because on the internet, a lone quiz question most often lives inside a list of quiz questions. The model isn’t failing to answer; it is correctly inferring what document it’s in. Prompt it with “I hate my boss and”, and it will obligingly continue the rant, because that’s how such documents continue. Ask it for help and it may produce a forum thread in which several confident strangers argue. A base model has no notion that you exist, that this is a conversation, or that helpfulness is the goal; it has a distribution over documents and you have supplied the first half of one.

The flip side: base models are eerily skilled impersonators. Prefix a few lines of the format you want — a half-written sonnet, three question-answer pairs, the opening of a legal brief — and the completion snaps into that register, because inferring “what document am I in” is exactly the skill next-token training rewards. Every chat assistant is built on this: the chat interface wraps your message in a transcript-like template, and further training sharpens the model’s role in it. That further training — supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations, then reinforcement learning from human feedback — is how a document completer becomes an assistant that answers questions, follows instructions, and declines some requests. It is the subject of the post-training chapters; for now, hold onto the fact that underneath every polished assistant is a base model, and the assistant’s capabilities were almost all acquired during plain next-token pretraining. Post-training mostly elicits and shapes; pretraining builds.

⚠️Careful

Because a base model models what text looks like, it is at every moment answering “what would plausibly come next in this document?” — never “what is true?” A base model completes confident prose confidently, whether or not the content is correct, and will fabricate citations, statistics, and biographies with perfect fluency when the document pattern calls for them. Post-training reduces this substantially but inherits the objective’s blind spot. When a 2026 assistant hallucinates, you are seeing the document-completion substrate showing through the finish.

One more observation belongs in this chapter, stated but deliberately not explained. When OpenAI scaled the recipe to 175 billion parameters in GPT-3 (2020), they noticed that the base model could apparently learn tasks from the prompt alone: show it three examples of English-to-French translation and it continues the pattern on a fourth, with no gradient update, no training, nothing but the generation loop running on a well-chosen prefix. They called it in-context learning, and at the time it startled nearly everyone — nothing in “minimize next-token cross-entropy” self-evidently implies “becomes few-shot-programmable at sufficient scale”. Why scale produces this and other abilities is bound up with scaling laws, and the mechanisms behind in-context learning (induction heads, among other things) are a running thread of the interpretability chapters. File the observation now; the explanations come later.

💡Intuition

Why would predicting the next token require understanding? Because prediction is a ruthless auditor. To predict the next token of “The verdict, after the jury deliberated for nine hours, was”, you need grammar (a noun phrase is coming), law (juries deliver verdicts of guilt or acquittal), and discourse (nine hours of deliberation hints the case was close). To predict the next token of a Python file, you need the syntax and the variable names defined 200 lines up. Every regularity in the world that leaves a statistical trace in text — and almost all of them do — is worth loss-reduction to a model with enough capacity to capture it. “It’s only predicting the next word” is true in the same way that “chess is only picking your next move” is true: the only conceals the depth of what picking well requires.

Where this leaves us

  • A language model is a probability distribution over token sequences — one line of probability theory; everything else is machinery for making the distribution good and sampling it fast.
  • Probability is the right formalism because linguistic uncertainty is irreducible, and it makes generation automatic: generating text is sampling from the distribution.
  • The chain rule factorizes any sequence distribution, exactly, into next-token conditionals: P(x1:T)=∏tP(xt∣x1:t−1)P(x_{1:T}) = \prod_t P(x_t \mid x_{1:t-1})P(x1:T​)=∏t​P(xt​∣x1:t−1​). The factorization is an identity; the approximation lives entirely in the learned conditionals.
  • All generation is one loop — predict, sample, append, repeat — and everything a deployed assistant does is this loop iterated.
  • Training maximizes the likelihood of the corpus, which is identical to minimizing per-token cross-entropy under teacher forcing; the loss is bounded below by the entropy of language itself, and the ground-truth- prefix regime creates a real (if debated) train/generate mismatch.
  • N-gram models estimated the conditionals by counting and drowned in exponential sparsity; Bengio’s 2003 neural model escaped by learning word vectors that let similar words share evidence — the origin of embeddings.
  • A base model is a document completer, not an assistant: post-training shapes the assistant, and in-context learning at scale is an observed (not yet explained) bonus of the plain next-token recipe.
