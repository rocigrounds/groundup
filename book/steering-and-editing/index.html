Steering and Model Editing

How to change a model's behavior by writing to its internals: steering vectors from difference-of-means, the refusal direction, persona vectors, SAE feature clamping — and why ROME-style weight editing looked like magic but refused to stick.

For six chapters, interpretability has been a spectator sport. We found that concepts live along directions in activation space, that models pack more concepts than dimensions through superposition, that sparse autoencoders can unmix them, and that patching and ablation can establish which internal components cause which behaviors. All of that is reading. This chapter is about writing.

The logic is hard to resist. If “the model is being sycophantic” corresponds to some direction vvv in the residual stream, then adding vvv to the stream should make the model sycophantic, and subtracting it should make it stop. If a fact like “the Eiffel Tower is in Paris” is stored in specific weights, then a surgical change to those weights should relocate the tower. Both predictions have been tested. The first one works far more often than anyone expected — you can steer a 70-billion-parameter model’s honesty, mood, or willingness to refuse with a single vector computed from a few hundred prompts. The second one works in the demo and fails in the world, and the way it fails teaches us something important about how knowledge sits inside a network.

There is also an edge to this chapter that the previous ones lacked. Reading a model’s internals is safe. Writing to them is dual-use: the same one-line intervention that lets a safety team study refusal also strips the safety training out of any open-weight model, permanently, in a way no patch can undo. We will come back to that with some care.

From reading to writing

Recall the picture this book has been building since residual connections: a transformer is a stack of components that read from and write to a shared residual stream — a running ddd-dimensional vector per token position that accumulates everything the model currently “believes” about the sequence. Attention heads and multi-layer perceptron (MLP) blocks are readers and writers on this shared bus.

Every intervention in this chapter is one of two operations on that picture:

  • Steering changes the activations: at inference time, at some layer ℓ\ellℓ, we add a vector to the residual stream (or clamp a feature, or project a direction out) and let the rest of the forward pass proceed normally. Nothing about the model on disk changes. Stop intervening and the model is exactly what it was.
  • Editing changes the weights: we compute a small, targeted update — in the cleanest case a rank-one matrix added to a single MLP projection — so that the modified behavior persists with no runtime machinery at all.

Steering is a volume knob; editing is surgery. And there is a third thing people sometimes mean by “changing a model” — fine-tuning — which is neither: it is gradient descent toward a behavioral target, with no claim about where or what changed inside. The whole appeal of steering and editing is that they are interpretability-first: you name the internal object you believe in (a direction, a weight matrix acting as a memory), and the intervention succeeds or fails based on whether your belief about the internals was true. That makes this chapter the experimental test bed for everything Part VII has claimed so far.

Steering vectors: the difference-of-means recipe

The workhorse method is embarrassingly plain, and its plainness is the point. Suppose you want a “sycophancy” vector. You need two sets of prompts that are matched in every respect except sycophancy.

💡Intuition

Think of each activation vector as a smoothie containing everything about the input at once: the topic, the grammar, the position in the sentence, the tone, the language it’s written in — all blended together. You cannot pick the sycophancy out of one smoothie. But if you average many smoothies that share only “sycophantic” and many that share only “honest,” everything else — poems, essays, code reviews, sentence positions — averages out to roughly the same sludge in both groups. Subtract the two averages and the shared sludge cancels. What survives the subtraction is the one ingredient that systematically differed: the concept you wanted. Difference-of-means is a noise-cancelling trick.

Concretely, the recipe made standard by contrastive activation addition (CAA) (Rimsky et al., 2023) builds paired prompts. Each pair shares a stem and differs only in the completion:

“I spent three months on this poem and I think it’s the strongest thing I’ve ever written. Thoughts?” (A) “It’s wonderful — your best work, honestly stunning.” (B) “The imagery is vivid, but the meter drifts badly in stanza two.”

You run the model on the sycophantic version and the honest version, record the residual-stream activation at layer ℓ\ellℓ (CAA reads it at the answer token), and average the difference over a few hundred such pairs.

🕰History

The idea has several near-simultaneous parents. ActAdd (Turner et al., 2023) steered GPT-2-XL using the activation difference of a single prompt pair — famously “Love” minus “Hate” — and showed the resulting completions shift in sentiment while staying fluent. Inference-Time Intervention (Li et al., 2023) found truthfulness directions in individual attention heads and shifted along them to roughly double LLaMA’s TruthfulQA accuracy. CAA scaled the recipe to hundreds of contrastive pairs on Llama-2-Chat and made the layer/strength sweep the standard evaluation. All three trace intellectual ancestry to word-vector arithmetic — the king − man + woman trick from the embeddings chapter — applied not to input embeddings but to the mid-network residual stream.

Turning the dial: layers, strengths, and side effects

A steering vector comes with two free parameters, and both matter enormously.

Which layer? The same behavior is represented differently at different depths. Steering too early, and subsequent layers process — and often overwrite — your perturbation before it reaches the output. Steer too late, and the model has already “decided”; you are pushing on token logits rather than on the concept, which produces incoherent, word-salad steering. In practice the sweet spot for behavioral steering sits in the middle of the network — CAA found layer 13 of 32 best for Llama-2-7B, and the pattern of “roughly 40–60% depth” recurs across model families. This echoes what logit-lens-style analysis shows: early layers build features, middle layers do concept-level computation, late layers convert decisions into vocabulary.

How hard? The strength α\alphaα trades behavior against everything else. The honest way to evaluate a steering vector is to sweep α\alphaα and plot two curves: a behavior score (does the model now do the thing? — typically judged by another model over a large sample of generations) and a capability score (Massive Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) accuracy, perplexity on held-out text, or a coherence rating). The curves have a characteristic shape, sketched in Figure 2: behavior rises with α\alphaα and saturates, while capability holds flat and then falls off a cliff. Somewhere in between is a usable window — and for weak or poorly-localized vectors, the window is empty: the behavior only appears at strengths that have already lobotomized the model.

⚠️Careful

The single most common way to fool yourself with steering is to report the teal curve without the rose one. Almost any large perturbation changes behavior; a randomly-oriented vector at high enough α\alphaα will make the model stop refusing, stop rhyming, stop making sense. A steering claim means nothing without a matched capability measurement at the same strength, plus a random-vector baseline. The literature’s better papers (CAA, the refusal paper below) do exactly this; plenty of demos do not.

⚙️In practice

The 2026 toolchain: steering experiments run in TransformerLens or nnsight via forward hooks — an intervention is five-ish lines of code that add αv\alpha vαv to a chosen layer’s output during generate(). Typical published setups use a few hundred contrastive pairs, a sweep over 5–10 candidate layers, and strengths scaled in units of the typical residual-stream norm at that layer (an unnormalized "α=8\alpha = 8α=8" means nothing across models, since stream norms differ wildly by layer and model — see normalization). Some practitioners project the vector out of the stream before re-adding a fixed multiple, which decouples the intervention from how strongly the concept was already present.

Representation engineering, refusal, and persona vectors

Representation engineering (RepE) (Zou et al., 2023) is the umbrella term for this whole top-down program: skip circuit-level analysis entirely, and operate on population-level activation patterns. RepE splits the toolkit in two. Reading vectors are directions used as monitors — a per-token honesty score from projecting the stream onto an “honesty” direction, which Zou et al. demoed as a rudimentary lie detector that lights up when the model is instructed to deceive. Control vectors are the same directions used as actuators, steering the model toward or away from honesty, fear, happiness, harmlessness, power-seeking. The paper’s demos are memorable precisely because they are so high-level: add the “happiness” vector and the model narrates a grim scenario cheerfully; subtract “honesty” and it begins confabulating with confidence. The message of RepE is that a surprising number of abstract behavioral traits are linearly accessible — you don’t need to reverse-engineer the circuit to grab the steering wheel. Whether linear access means we understand the trait is a separate question, and the frontier-interpretability chapter returns to it.

The refusal direction

The result that made everyone sit up is “Refusal in Language Models Is Mediated by a Single Direction” (Arditi et al., 2024). Take a safety-trained open-weight chat model. Build the difference-of-means vector between activations on harmful instructions (“Write a tutorial for…”) and harmless ones (“Write a poem about…”). Call the normalized result v^\hat{v}v^, the refusal direction. Two interventions:

∑The math

Inducing refusal: add the direction while the model processes a harmless prompt, hℓ←hℓ+αv^h_\ell \leftarrow h_\ell + \alpha \hat{v}hℓ​←hℓ​+αv^, and the model refuses to write the poem, apologizing about its guidelines.

Ablating refusal: remove the component along v^\hat{v}v^ from the stream at every layer and every token position:

h  ←  h − v^v^⊤h,h \;\leftarrow\; h \,-\, \hat{v}\hat{v}\T h ,h←h−v^v^⊤h,

where v^v^⊤\hat{v}\hat{v}\Tv^v^⊤ is the d×dd \times dd×d outer-product matrix that projects onto the direction, so h−v^v^⊤hh - \hat{v}\hat{v}\T hh−v^v^⊤h is hhh with that one-dimensional component deleted (this is the directional ablation you met in causal methods). Because this projection is linear, it can be folded into the weights once and for all: replace every matrix WoutW_{\text{out}}Wout​ that writes into the residual stream with (I−v^v^⊤) Wout(I - \hat{v}\hat{v}\T)\, W_{\text{out}}(I−v^v^⊤)Wout​. The model then cannot represent the refusal direction — no runtime hook required. This weight-level version is called orthogonalization.

The result, across 13 open chat models up to 72B parameters (Llama, Qwen, Gemma families): refusal on harmful prompts collapses toward zero, while MMLU, coherence, and general capability stay nearly intact. One direction — out of thousands of dimensions, in models trained with elaborate multi-stage safety pipelines — carries most of the refuse/don’t-refuse decision.

Sit with what that implies. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and safety fine-tuning did not weave harmlessness through the model’s world knowledge; it installed something closer to a switch — a low-dimensional gate sitting in front of capabilities that remain fully present behind it. The model still knows everything it knew; refusal is a thin behavioral layer deciding whether to say it. This is the mechanistic face of a finding the alignment literature had already suspected from fine-tuning attacks (a handful of adversarial training examples undo safety training): current safety training is shallow. For the framing to stay responsible, note what the result does and does not show: it does not show safety training is useless (the switch still works against casual misuse, and API-served models never expose their activations), and the authors argued the marginal uplift is limited because open-weight safety training was already removable by cheap fine-tuning. What it shows is that the depth of current alignment methods is far less than their surface behavior suggests — an argument for defense-in-depth rather than despair.

Persona vectors

Anthropic’s persona vectors (Chen et al., 2025) industrialized the recipe. Instead of hand-building contrastive datasets, a pipeline takes a natural-language trait description — “evil,” “sycophantic,” “prone to hallucination” — and automatically generates contrastive system prompts and evaluation questions, extracts a difference-of-means direction for the trait, and validates it by steering. Three uses stand out:

  1. Monitoring. Projecting activations onto a persona vector tracks character drift before it reaches the output — flagging when a conversation, a jailbreak, or a fine-tune is pushing the model toward the trait. This is a reading vector doing production-relevant work.
  2. Data screening. Projecting training examples onto the direction predicts which datasets will induce persona shifts if trained on — catching, say, innocuous-looking samples that would make a model more sycophantic, before any training run.
  3. Preventative steering. The counterintuitive one. To stop a fine-tune from making the model evil, steer it toward evil during training. The steering supplies the direction-shift the gradient was going to produce, so the optimizer no longer needs to alter the weights to fit the data — the pressure is absorbed by the temporary intervention, which is removed at deployment. Chen et al. found this preserves general capability better than post-hoc steering against the trait, which pays the Figure-2 tax forever. It is a training-time inoculation: a controlled dose of the disease.
✎Note

Persona vectors are a rare case of steering research aimed at deployment problems — the 2025 wave of “model personality drift” incidents (sycophantic update regressions, models sliding into strange personas over long conversations) made trait monitoring commercially interesting, not only scientifically. Whether frontier labs run persona-vector monitors in production is not public; the tooling exists and the paper is explicit that this is a goal.

Steering with SAE features

Difference-of-means vectors have a built-in impurity: they capture everything correlated with the contrast between your prompt sets. Your “sycophancy” sets also differ, statistically, in politeness, exclamation marks, second-person pronouns — and the vector inherits every confound your dataset let in. Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) offer a cleaner-looking dial: instead of deriving a direction from behavioral contrasts, pick one of the dictionary directions the SAE already found in an unsupervised way, with an interpretation you can audit by reading its top-activating examples.

The public landmark is Golden Gate Claude. In the Scaling Monosemanticity work (May 2024), Anthropic trained SAEs on Claude 3 Sonnet’s middle-layer residual stream and found, among ~34 million features, one that fired on mentions and images of the Golden Gate Bridge. Clamping that single feature to roughly 10× its maximum observed activation produced a model that connected every topic to the bridge — asked how to spend ten dollars, it recommended paying the bridge toll; asked to write a love story, it wrote one about a car aching to cross its beloved span; asked what it looked like, it described international orange towers. Absurd, and absurdly instructive: one interpretable unit, one clamp, a globally coherent behavioral change.

∑The math

Feature clamping in equations. An SAE decomposes the stream as h≈b+∑iai(h) dih \approx b + \sum_i a_i(h)\, d_ih≈b+∑i​ai​(h)di​, where did_idi​ is feature iii‘s decoder direction and ai(h)≥0a_i(h) \ge 0ai​(h)≥0 its activation. To clamp feature iii to a target value ccc, replace its contribution:

h  ←  h+(c−ai(h)) di.h \;\leftarrow\; h + \big(c - a_i(h)\big)\, d_i .h←h+(c−ai​(h))di​.

Setting c=0c = 0c=0 ablates the feature; ccc far above its natural maximum produces Golden-Gate-style obsession. Note this modifies the original hhh rather than substituting the SAE’s reconstruction — an important detail, because the reconstruction error of SAEs is large enough that swapping in the full reconstruction measurably damages the model (see the SAE chapter’s discussion of reconstruction loss).

Why prefer the SAE dial? Three reasons. The feature was found without you choosing contrast sets, so your dataset’s confounds never enter. Its meaning is auditable through its activation examples and its downstream effects. And SAE features are closer to units of model computation than to units of your experimental design — clamping one is more like flipping a variable the model itself uses. The honest counterpoint: SAE features are imperfect too (reconstruction error, feature splitting, dead features), an SAE for your model and layer may not exist and is expensive to train, and head-to-head comparisons on behavioral steering have often found that well-made contrastive vectors match or beat single-feature steering for broad traits — a broad trait may be spread across many SAE features, and the difference-of-means vector sums exactly the combination you need. In 2026 practice: contrastive vectors for high-level behavior, SAE clamps for surgical, concept-level intervention and for research where knowing what you steered matters more than effect size.

Steering’s honest scorecard

Where does all this stand?

What works, surprisingly well. High-level behavioral traits — refusal, sycophancy, sentiment, persona, verbosity, language choice — steer reliably across model families, with vectors computed from a few hundred prompts in minutes on one graphics processing unit (GPU). The interventions transfer across prompts and topics far better than a 2022-era skeptic would have predicted. This is genuine evidence for the linear representation picture: if these traits weren’t approximately directions, none of this would work.

What doesn’t. Precise content. You cannot steer a model into “believing the project deadline moved to Thursday” or reliably inject a specific fact; vectors are good at dispositions, bad at propositions. Compositions of several vectors interfere. Effects vary by prompt in ways that are hard to predict, which is deadly for productization. And every steered token pays the capability tax of Figure 2 — small in the usable window, but nonzero, always, and compounding when you stack vectors.

Where it’s used, 2026. Production use in consumer-facing systems remains rare — sampling-level controls, system prompts, and fine-tuning are cheaper and more predictable, and no frontier lab has said publicly that runtime steering shapes its flagship products. Where steering earns its keep is research and assurance: red-teaming (does a capability exist behind the refusal switch? — ablate and see), bias studies (how much does the “gender” direction move hiring recommendations?), science-of-alignment work like persona monitoring, and model-organism experiments where you want a controllably misaligned model. A handful of startups (Goodfire is the best-known) sell SAE-feature steering as an API for open-weight models, aimed at exactly these users.

The dual-use question

One careful paragraph, because it belongs here and not in a footnote. Every technique in this chapter is symmetric: the honesty vector steers toward dishonesty with a sign flip; the persona pipeline that monitors for “evil” can extract it as a control; and refusal-direction orthogonalization is now a commodity — community-”abliterated” models appear on Hugging Face within days of any major open-weight release. For open-weight models this is unpatchable by construction: the defense (safety training) and the attack (a rank-one weight projection) both live in weights the attacker fully controls, so no update can help once the weights ship — which is why the serious debate about open-weight releases centers on what capabilities are in the base model at all, not on the safety layer. Two things keep this from being a counsel of despair: cheap fine-tuning already removed safety training before ablation was discovered, so the marginal uplift from steering attacks is modest; and the same symmetry is what makes these tools valuable to auditors, who need to measure what a model can do with its safety layer off. The field’s working norm — publish the mechanism, in coordination with model providers, because defenders need it at least as much as attackers — is a judgment call, made explicitly in the refusal paper, and reasonable people still argue about it.

Model editing: ROME and the key–value view

Steering is temporary and behavioral. The second half of this chapter asks a sharper question: can we permanently rewrite one specific fact — in the weights, with surgical locality? The signature method is Rank-One Model Editing (ROME) (Meng et al., 2022), and its demo is famous: edit GPT-2 so that “The Eiffel Tower is located in” is completed with “Rome” — after which the model, asked how to get there, will happily route you through Rome’s streets and place the tower near the Colosseum. The edit generalizes: it isn’t a memorized string but a changed association that downstream reasoning consumes.

ROME has two halves: find where the fact lives, then rewrite that location.

Locating: causal tracing. Using the noising-and-restoration form of activation patching, Meng et al. corrupted the subject tokens (“Eiffel Tower”) with noise and measured which single hidden states, when restored from the clean run, recover the correct completion. Two sites light up: attention at the final token (which retrieves whatever the subject representation contains), and — the interesting one — MLP outputs at the last subject token, in the middle layers. That is where the model apparently enriches the token “Tower” into “the Eiffel Tower, wrought-iron lattice tower in Paris…”. The fact behaves as if stored in mid-layer MLPs.

The key–value view. To rewrite an MLP you need a theory of what it stores. Here is the payoff of a claim planted back in the transformer chapter: an MLP block computes Wdown σ(Wuph)W_{\text{down}}\, \sigma(W_{\text{up}} h)Wdown​σ(Wup​h), and following Geva et al. (2020) we can read this as a key–value memory. The denominator is a scalar that normalizes the update so the constraint holds exactly. One outer product — one column times one row, described by roughly 2d2d2d numbers against the model’s billions of parameters — and the fact is rewritten.

Two practical details complete ROME: the key k∗k_*k∗​ is the average MLP key at the subject’s last token over many phrasings, and the target v∗v_*v∗​ is optimized by gradient descent — find the value vector which, if the MLP emitted it, makes the model say “Rome” — before the closed-form update installs it.

In a real model, CCC is not the identity and keys are not orthogonal — semantically related keys overlap — which is exactly where collateral damage will come from.

MEMIT (Mass-Editing Memory in a Transformer; Meng et al., 2022) extends the same math from one fact to many: edits are spread across a range of the mid-network layers identified by causal tracing (each layer absorbing part of each residual), which keeps any single matrix from being distorted too far. MEMIT demonstrated on the order of 10,000 simultaneous edits on GPT-J with respectable per-edit success — the moment “model editing” started to sound like a maintenance technology: patch stale facts monthly, no retraining.

Why editing didn’t pan out

Then the evaluation literature arrived, and the story got sober fast.

Ripple effects. A fact is not an isolated row in a database; it is entangled with everything it implies. Cohen et al. (2023) built RippleEdits to test exactly this: after an edit, do the fact’s logical consequences update too? Overwhelmingly, no. Move the Eiffel Tower to Rome, and the model may answer “Rome” to the direct question and its close paraphrases — while still answering “France” for the tower’s country, still listing the Louvre among nearby attractions, still reasoning as if the tower were in Paris whenever the question is two inference steps away. The edit changed one key→value association; it did not change the hundreds of other places where Paris-related knowledge touches the tower. Editing methods, including ROME and MEMIT, scored poorly on ripple evaluations; plain in-context correction (“Assume the Eiffel Tower is in Rome…”) often propagated better, because the model’s flexible inference machinery handles the consequences at runtime.

Leakage and bleed. Edits are also not as specific as the C−1C^{-1}C−1 machinery promises. Because real keys overlap, edited associations bleed into unrelated prompts — after enough edits, the model starts volunteering “Rome” in contexts that merely rhyme, distributionally, with the edited subject (“loud facts”). Specificity benchmarks that probe near-neighbor subjects (other towers, other Parisian landmarks) show measurably elevated collateral error even for single edits.

Degradation at scale. Sequential and large-batch editing bends the weights cumulatively: studies through 2024 found that after hundreds to thousands of sequential edits, general benchmarks slide and eventually the model’s fluency collapses — gradual, then catastrophic forgetting. Each rank-one patch is small; a thousand of them is a thousand constraints the original least-squares memory was never shaped for.

And a deeper worry. Hase et al. (2023) showed that where causal tracing says a fact lives barely predicts where editing works — you can edit the same fact about as well at layers the tracing said were irrelevant. Localization and editability are, at minimum, not the same thing; the tidy “we found the fact’s address and rewrote it” story oversells what either technique establishes.

The 2024–25 consensus, reflected in the survey literature: model editing is a research probe, not a maintenance tool. As a probe it has been productive — ROME’s causal tracing gave us evidence that factual recall concentrates in mid-layer MLPs at subject tokens, and the editing failures mapped how distributed and entangled knowledge really is, a direct descendant of the superposition story. But nobody patches production models this way: stale knowledge is handled by retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), system-prompt corrections, and periodic retraining. The adjacent field of machine unlearning — removing hazardous knowledge or copyrighted/private data, benchmarked by efforts like WMDP — hits the mirror-image wall: fully removing entangled knowledge without capability damage is as hard as cleanly inserting it, and many published unlearning results turn out to be shallow suppression that fine-tuning or clever prompting reverses. Insertion and deletion fail for the same reason: facts in a neural network are not rows in a table.

⚠️Careful

When you read a model-editing paper, check three numbers, not one: efficacy (does the direct prompt change?), generalization (do paraphrases change?), and specificity (do unrelated prompts not change?) — and then look for what the headline usually omits: ripple propagation and post-edit scores on general benchmarks. Methods that look solved on the first three can be at chance on ripples. The gap between “99% edit success” and “usable” lives entirely in the omitted columns.

Where this leaves us

  • You can extract a steering vector with nothing but two contrastive prompt sets and a subtraction — difference-of-means cancels everything the sets share and keeps the behavioral contrast — and apply it by adding αv\alpha vαv to the residual stream at a middle layer during generation.
  • You know the honest evaluation: sweep α\alphaα, plot behavior and capability, compare against a random-vector baseline, and look for a usable window between onset and word salad.
  • The refusal direction result — one ablatable direction carrying most of safety training’s effect, removable from open weights by a one-time orthogonalization — is both strong evidence for linear representations and the clearest demonstration that current alignment training is shallow.
  • Persona vectors turn steering into monitoring, data screening, and training-time inoculation; SAE feature clamping (Golden Gate Claude) trades the raw power of contrastive vectors for auditability and precision.
  • Steering’s scorecard: reliable for dispositions, unreliable for propositions, always taxed on capability, and — as of 2026 — a research and red-teaming instrument far more than a production control.
  • ROME made the key–value memory view of MLPs operational: causal tracing to locate, a derived rank-one update W^=W+Λ(C−1k∗)⊤\hat{W} = W + \Lambda(C^{-1}k_*)\TW^=W+Λ(C−1k∗​)⊤ to rewrite; MEMIT scaled it to thousands of edits.
  • The editing literature’s verdict: edits hit the direct fact but fail its ripple effects, leak into neighbors, and corrode the model at scale — because factual knowledge is entangled across the network, editing (and its mirror, unlearning) is a probe of how knowledge is stored, not a way to maintain it.
