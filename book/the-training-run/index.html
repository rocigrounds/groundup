The Training Run

Learning-rate schedules, loss spikes, checkpoint economics, and the dashboard — what actually happens during the weeks-long run that turns a cluster and a dataset into a base model.

In this chapter

  2. Ramps and curricula: the run changes shape mid-flight
  3. When the loss spikes
  4. Hardware fails constantly: the checkpoint arithmetic
  5. The dashboard: what the crew actually watches
  6. The lifecycle of a run, end to end
  7. Where this leaves us

Every chapter so far has described machinery: optimizers that turn gradients into updates, architectures that turn tokens into predictions, clusters that turn megawatts into matrix multiplications, data pipelines that turn the internet into token streams, and scaling laws that tell you how big to make everything. This chapter is about the event where all of it gets used at once: the training run.

A frontier pretraining run is one of the strangest artifacts in engineering. It is a single program that runs continuously for weeks or months on tens of thousands of graphics processing units (GPUs), burns computing time worth tens of millions of dollars, and cannot be meaningfully paused, refactored, or restarted from scratch once it is underway. The core loop — forward pass, loss, backward pass, optimizer step — is the same loop you met in Chapter 10, and you could write it in thirty lines. Everything that makes a run hard lives around that loop: the schedule that shapes the learning rate over a trillion tokens, the ramps and curricula that change the batch and sequence length mid-flight, the defenses against a loss curve that occasionally tries to explode, the checkpointing arithmetic forced on you by hardware that fails several times a day, and the dashboard a rotating crew of engineers watches at 3 a.m.

This is the operational chapter. By the end you will know what the learning-rate schedule of a 2026 model looks like and why, what a loss spike is and what the on-call engineer does about one, why a 16,000-GPU cluster experiences roughly one failure every three hours and how checkpoint cadence is chosen in response, and what the full lifecycle of a run looks like from “data is ready” to “hand the checkpoint to the post-training team.”

The learning-rate schedule is the run’s spine

Recall from Chapter 11 that the learning rate η\etaη — the global step-size multiplier applied to every parameter update — is the single most consequential hyperparameter in training. What we did not emphasize there is that in a real run, η\etaη is never a constant. It is a schedule: a function η(t)\eta(t)η(t) of the training step ttt, planned before the run starts and executed over the entire multi-week flight. Get the schedule wrong and no amount of clever architecture rescues you; the run either diverges, undertrains, or quietly wastes a fraction of your compute budget that you will never get back.

Modern schedules have two or three phases, and each exists for a specific mechanical reason.

Warmup: why the first steps are dangerous

Every serious schedule begins with warmup: the learning rate starts at (or near) zero and increases linearly to its peak value over the first stretch of training — typically somewhere between a few hundred and a few thousand steps, often quoted as roughly 0.1–1% of the run.

Why can’t we start at the peak? Two mechanisms, both of which you have the background to understand precisely.

First, Adam’s statistics are cold. AdamW (adaptive moment estimation with decoupled weight decay, from Chapter 11) divides each parameter’s update by v^t\sqrt{\hat{v}_t}v^t​​, where v^t\hat{v}_tv^t​ is a running estimate of the squared gradient for that parameter. At step 1, that estimate is based on a single sample. Bias correction fixes the mean of the estimate but not its variance: a parameter that happened to receive a tiny gradient on the first few batches gets a tiny v^\hat{v}v^, and dividing by v^\sqrt{\hat{v}}v^​ then produces an enormous effective step for exactly the parameters we know least about. A few such oversized steps can throw weights into regions from which the run never fully recovers. Warmup keeps the global multiplier small until v^\hat{v}v^ has averaged over enough batches to be trustworthy — a few hundred steps, which is why warmup lengths are what they are.

Second, the network itself is unsettled. At initialization (Chapter 12) the weights are random and the loss surface around them is unusually steep and ill-conditioned: gradients are large, activations have not yet found the scales the normalization layers (Chapter 14) will settle into, and attention logits can be far from their eventual well-behaved range. Early steps are therefore taken on the worst-conditioned part of the whole trajectory. Taking them at full speed is how you fall off the cliff before the race starts.

🕰History

Warmup entered deep learning folklore through large-batch ImageNet training — Goyal et al. (2017) used “gradual warmup” to make an 8,192-image batch trainable — and through the original transformer, whose famous inverse square root schedule had warmup built into its formula. The Adam-variance explanation was sharpened later (the RAdam line of work showed that warmup is, in part, a fix for the high variance of the adaptive denominator early in training). Today warmup is so universal that its absence in a config file is treated as a typo.

Cosine decay: the long default

After warmup, the classic schedule — used by GPT-3, Chinchilla, Llama 1 through 3, and most models you have heard of — is cosine decay: the learning rate traces half a cosine wave from the peak down to a small final value.

∑The math

With warmup steps twt_wtw​, total steps TTT, peak learning rate ηmax⁡\eta_{\max}ηmax​ and final learning rate ηmin⁡\eta_{\min}ηmin​, the schedule is

Every symbol: ttt is the current optimizer step, twt_wtw​ the number of warmup steps, TTT the total planned steps, ηmax⁡\eta_{\max}ηmax​ the peak rate (for a 7B-scale model, typically around 3×10−43 \times 10^{-4}3×10−4; larger models use smaller peaks), and ηmin⁡\eta_{\min}ηmin​ the floor. The near-universal convention is ηmin⁡=0.1 ηmax⁡\eta_{\min} = 0.1\,\eta_{\max}ηmin​=0.1ηmax​ — decay to 10% of peak, not to zero, though some runs do go all the way to zero or below 10% in a final stretch.

The cosine shape has no deep theoretical justification. Its virtues are that it is smooth, spends a long time near the peak (fast progress while the loss surface still tolerates big steps), and decelerates gently into the finish (small steps let the weights settle into a sharper minimum, exactly the annealing logic from simulated cooling of step sizes).

One important empirical fact about cosine schedules came out of the Chinchilla paper: the cosine cycle length must match the intended training length. If you stretch the cosine over substantially more steps than you actually train — say you planned for 400B tokens of schedule but stop at 300B — the model is undertrained relative to a schedule that was planned for 300B from the start, because it never got its low-learning-rate consolidation phase. Hoffmann et al. found that setting the cycle more than about 25% longer than the actual run measurably hurt the final loss. The uncomfortable consequence: with a cosine schedule, you must commit to the length of the run before it begins. If, six weeks in, the model looks great and you want to train on 3T more tokens — too bad. Your learning rate is already low, and rewinding it destabilizes training. This rigidity is what the next schedule exists to fix.

Warmup–stable–decay: refusing to pre-commit

The warmup–stable–decay (WSD) schedule — popularized by the MiniCPM team in 2024 and since adopted, in one variant or another, by many open models (DeepSeek’s runs, Hugging Face’s SmolLM series, and others use WSD-flavored schedules; treat any specific attribution beyond MiniCPM as fast-moving) — has three phases:

  1. Warmup, as always.
  2. Stable: hold the learning rate constant at the peak for most of training.
  3. Decay: over the final roughly 10–20% of steps, drop the rate rapidly (linearly, or following a steep exponential/1-minus-sqrt shape) to near zero.

During the stable phase, the loss decreases more slowly than a cosine run’s would at the same token count — you are taking big noisy steps and the weights are rattling around a broad basin. Then, during decay, the loss falls off a cliff, dropping in days by an amount that took weeks to achieve during the stable phase, and typically landing at or below where the equivalent cosine run would have ended. The interpretation (informal but widely held): the stable phase explores and accumulates knowledge in a wide, noisy orbit, and the decay phase cashes it in, letting the weights settle to the bottom of the basin they have been circling.

Why do labs love this? Because of what it does to optionality:

  • You never pre-commit to a run length. Any time you like, you can branch a copy of the run, decay it, and get a finished model — while the main stable-phase run keeps going. Training going better than expected? Keep feeding tokens. New data pipeline delivers 2T extra tokens mid-run? Extend. The Chinchilla pre-commitment problem disappears.
  • Scaling-law experiments get dramatically cheaper. One long stable run plus several short decays yields loss-versus-tokens points at many budgets; with cosine you must train a separate full run per budget (Chapter 32 explains why those points feed the scaling fits).
  • The decay phase is a natural home for the best data. Recall from Chapter 31 that modern runs anneal on upweighted high-quality data — curated math, code, and instruction-adjacent text — in the final stretch. WSD gives that practice a principled slot: the decay phase is exactly when small learning rates let high-quality gradients refine the model without being scrambled by further large-step exploration.
⚙️In practice

Representative 2024–2026 numbers (approximate, and they drift): peak learning rates around 3×10−43\times10^{-4}3×10−4 for ~7B dense models, down to ∼1.5×10−4\sim 1.5\times10^{-4}∼1.5×10−4 at 70B scale and ∼8×10−5\sim 8\times10^{-5}∼8×10−5 at 400B scale; warmup of 1,000–8,000 steps; cosine to 10% of peak for Llama-3-style runs; WSD variants with a decay phase of 10–20% of total tokens for MiniCPM-style and many post-2024 open runs. Increasingly, labs do not hand-pick the peak at full scale at all: they tune it on small models under maximal update parametrization (μP) (Yang et al., 2022), a way of scaling initialization and per-layer learning rates so that the optimal peak learning rate stays the same as the model grows — tune once at 40M parameters, reuse at 40B.

Learning rate and batch size: the folklore

Schedules interact with batch size, and here textbook theory shades into folklore that every practitioner should know as folklore.

The linear scaling rule (Goyal et al.) says: if you multiply the batch size by kkk, multiply the learning rate by kkk. The intuition is that a batch of kBkBkB examples averages away noise, so its gradient supports a step kkk times larger — one big accurate step replaces kkk small noisy ones. This works well for plain stochastic gradient descent (SGD) in a mid-sized regime. For Adam-family optimizers the folklore instead says square-root scaling (η∝B\eta \propto \sqrt{B}η∝B​), consistent with how Adam’s normalization already absorbs part of the noise reduction. Neither rule is a law; both fail at the extremes, and serious runs verify the pairing empirically on scaling ladders rather than trusting the rule.

The concept that explains why the rules fail at the extremes is the critical batch size, from McCandlish et al. (2018). Their gradient noise scale measures how noisy a single-example gradient is relative to the true full-dataset gradient:

Bnoise  ≈  tr⁡(Σ)∥g∥2,B_{\text{noise}} \;\approx\; \frac{\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)}{\lVert g \rVert^2},Bnoise​≈∥g∥2tr(Σ)​,

where ggg is the true gradient and Σ\SigmaΣ is the covariance matrix of per-example gradients — so the numerator is total gradient variance and the denominator is signal. When your batch BBB is far below BnoiseB_{\text{noise}}Bnoise​, gradients are noise-dominated: doubling the batch halves the number of steps needed, for free — perfect parallelism. When BBB is far above BnoiseB_{\text{noise}}Bnoise​, the batch mean has already nearly converged to the true gradient, and extra examples buy almost nothing: you burn more compute per step without taking fewer steps. The critical batch size is the knee of that curve, and — the key operational finding — it grows during training, because as the loss falls, the signal ∥g∥2\lVert g\rVert^2∥g∥2 shrinks faster than the noise, making BnoiseB_{\text{noise}}Bnoise​ larger. Early training has a small critical batch; late training has a huge one.

Ramps and curricula: the run changes shape mid-flight

That last fact explains a practice that surprises newcomers: the batch size is not constant either.

Batch-size ramping. Since the critical batch size grows as loss falls, starting at the final batch size wastes compute early (you are far above the early critical batch), while ending at the initial batch size wastes parallelism late. So runs ramp. GPT-3 ramped its batch from 32k tokens to 3.2M tokens over the first billions of tokens. Llama 3 405B trained with a batch of about 4M tokens initially, doubling to 8M and later to 16M tokens as training progressed (per the Llama 3 report; figures approximate). The ramp points are scheduled in advance, and each ramp is a small stability event of its own — the noise statistics the optimizer has adapted to change discontinuously — which is one more reason ramps are paired with conservative learning-rate choices.

Sequence-length curricula. Attention cost grows quadratically with sequence length (Chapter 28), and most of what a model must learn — grammar, facts, code syntax, local reasoning — is learnable from a few thousand tokens of context. So nearly every modern run trains short first and long later. Some runs ramp sequence length early (e.g., a few hundred tokens up to the base length over the first fraction of training); nearly all of them defer long context to a dedicated context-extension stage: Llama 3 trained almost all of its 15.6T tokens at 8K context, then extended to 128K in six staged increases over roughly the final 800B tokens, adjusting the rotary positional encoding (RoPE) base frequency at each stage so that positional geometry stretches to cover the new lengths — the RoPE-scaling machinery from Chapter 26, applied as a scheduled phase of the run. Training long-context from scratch would multiply the cost of the entire run for capabilities that the last few percent of tokens can install.

💡Intuition

A useful mental model for all of these mid-flight changes: a training run is not one fixed experiment but a flight plan — a sequence of regimes, each chosen so that the current compute buys the most learning. Small batches while gradients are informative one at a time; big batches once only averages carry signal. Short sequences while the model is learning what words are; long sequences once only document-scale structure is left to learn. Peak learning rate while the basin is wide; decayed learning rate to land. The loop never changes; the regime around it does.

When the loss spikes

Now to the part of the run that generates war stories. You are three weeks in. The loss has been grinding downward, 2.1 to 1.9 over ten days. And then, over a few dozen steps, it jumps — up 5%, up 30%, occasionally straight to NaN (not-a-number, the floating-point value that means the arithmetic itself broke). This is a loss spike, and at sufficient scale every run has them: the PaLM team reported roughly 20 spikes during the 540B run “despite the fact that gradient clipping was enabled.”

Anatomy of a spike

A spike is rarely instantaneous, and its structure is visible on the dashboard if you know where to look:

The tell is that the pre-clip gradient norm usually rises first — for dozens or hundreds of steps before the loss reacts. That is why, as Chapter 12 noted, every serious codebase logs the gradient norm before clipping at every step: the clipped norm is capped by construction and shows you nothing, while the pre-clip norm is a seismograph.

After the jump, one of three things happens. The loss decays back to its old trend over hundreds or thousands of steps (annoying, survivable). Or it plateaus onto a worse trend — the spike scrambled something the run never relearns (expensive). Or it climbs to NaN and the run is dead from that step forward (rollback mandatory).

Where spikes come from

Three broad families of cause, in rough order of how often they get blamed:

Bad data. A shard of poorly filtered content — enormous repeated strings, binary junk that survived filtering, adversarially weird text — produces a batch whose gradient is far outside the distribution the optimizer state has adapted to. The classic diagnostic result, reported by the PaLM team: replay the same batches on the model state from a checkpoint well before the spike, and often there is no spike. It is not that the data is toxic in itself; it is the combination of a particular data batch with a particular optimizer state. That interaction effect is what makes spikes so hard to reproduce and study.

Numerics. Almost all 2026 pretraining runs on the matrix math in bfloat16 (bf16) — the 16-bit format from Chapter 33 with the exponent range of fp32 (32-bit floating point) but only 7 bits of mantissa, i.e., about 2–3 decimal digits of precision. Its range means you rarely overflow (the fp16 disease), but its coarseness has its own failure modes: attention logits that grow large get rounded coarsely before the softmax, tiny gradient contributions vanish when accumulated in bf16, and low-probability tokens’ log-probabilities go unstable. The standard mitigations — keep an fp32 master copy of the weights and fp32 optimizer state, perform gradient reductions and the final softmax/cross-entropy in fp32 — remove most of the danger, but “most” is doing real work in that sentence at step three million.

State and hardware corruption. Optimizer state (those mmm and vvv buffers, sharded across thousands of devices per Chapter 33) can be corrupted by a bad collective operation or, memorably rarely, by silent data corruption — a GPU whose arithmetic is wrong without any error being raised. These are the spikes that make engineers superstitious.

There is also a school of thought — supported by small-scale reproductions of large-scale instabilities (Wortsman et al., “Small-scale proxies for large-scale Transformer training instabilities”) — that many spikes are not exotic accidents at all but ordinary edge-of-stability dynamics: large models at high learning rates operate close to the divergence boundary, and specific mechanisms (attention-logit growth, output-logit divergence) push them over. On this view, spikes are a property of where on the stability landscape you chose to fly, and the right response is architectural.

The stability toolkit

Which brings us to the toolkit — part architecture, part loss function, part protocol.

Gradient clipping (Chapter 12’s seatbelt): rescale the global gradient whenever its norm exceeds a threshold, almost universally 1.01.01.0 in modern configs. It caps single-step damage but, as PaLM demonstrated, does not prevent spikes — a run can take many maximum-length steps in a bad direction.

Z-loss on the logits. Introduced in PaLM’s lineage and now common, the z-loss is an auxiliary term added to the cross-entropy loss:

Lz  =  λ log⁡2Z,Z=∑i=1Vezi,\mathcal{L}_z \;=\; \lambda \,\log^2 Z, \qquad Z = \sum_{i=1}^{V} e^{z_i},Lz​=λlog2Z,Z=i=1∑V​ezi​,

where ziz_izi​ are the output logits over the vocabulary of size VVV, ZZZ is the softmax normalizer, and λ\lambdaλ is tiny — PaLM used λ=10−4\lambda = 10^{-4}λ=10−4. Why does this help? Recall from Chapter 9 that cross-entropy depends only on differences between logits: add a constant ccc to every logit and the softmax probabilities are unchanged. So the absolute scale of the logits is unconstrained by the main loss — a flat direction — and in practice it drifts upward over a long run. Large logits are numerically dangerous: eze^{z}ez in low precision, coarse bf16 rounding exactly where the loss is computed, saturated softmaxes with vanishing gradients. The z-loss penalizes log⁡Z\log ZlogZ away from zero, gently pinning the normalizer near 1 and the logits near a well-conditioned scale. It is a numerical guardrail dressed as a loss term.

QK-norm. The other notorious drift is inside attention: query–key dot products growing until attention saturates into near-one-hot patterns, a mechanism implicated in spikes at scale (ViT-22B documented and fixed exactly this). QK-norm applies root-mean-square normalization (RMSNorm, Chapter 14) to the query and key vectors before their dot product, bounding attention logits by construction. As covered in Chapter 12, it has spread from vision transformers into language models (Gemma-family and OLMo-2-generation models among the public adopters) because it removes an entire spike mechanism for roughly zero cost.

The rollback-and-skip protocol. When a spike does happen and does not self-heal, the operational fix — standardized since PaLM — is almost embarrassingly pragmatic: restart from a checkpoint before the spike and skip the data batches around it. PaLM restarted from roughly 100 steps before each bad spike and skipped 200–500 batches, and the spike did not recur. Given the interaction-effect nature of spikes (same data, different optimizer state, no spike), skipping a few hundred out of millions of batches costs nothing measurable, and the alternative — diagnosing the exact parameter-state pathology mid-run — is a research project you do not have time for at $2,000 per minute of cluster time.

⚠️Careful

A spike that self-recovers is not automatically harmless. The dangerous case is the quiet one: loss rejoins a track 0.005 nats worse than trend and stays there, which at frontier scale is a real capability loss you paid full price for. This is why dashboards plot loss against the projected trend and why per-domain losses (below) matter — a spike can permanently damage, say, code loss while web-text loss recovers fully, and the aggregate curve half-hides it.

Hardware fails constantly: the checkpoint arithmetic

Everything above assumed the software is running. At frontier scale, the hardware itself refuses to cooperate on a daily basis, and the numbers are worth internalizing because they reshape the entire engineering culture around runs.

The Llama 3 report is the best public accounting. During a 54-day snapshot of 405B pretraining on 16,384 H100 GPUs, the team logged 466 job interruptions — about 419 of them unexpected — i.e., roughly eight unplanned failures per day, one every three hours. About 78% of the unexpected interruptions were hardware: faulty GPUs (the largest single category, including memory failures), host components, network cables, even thermal effects — they observed a 1–2% throughput variation by time of day, from midday temperatures affecting GPU thermal throttling. Despite all this they report achieving over 90% effective training time, because the response machinery is engineered as carefully as the model.

Why is this unavoidable rather than sloppy? Independent failure arithmetic. If one GPU plus its share of host, memory, and network has a mean time between failures (MTBF) of, say, five years — good hardware — then 16,384 of them collectively fail every 5×365×24/16,384≈2.75 \times 365 \times 24 / 16{,}384 \approx 2.75×365×24/16,384≈2.7 hours. A synchronous training job (Chapter 33) has no spare-tire mode by default: every device participates in every collective operation, so one dead GPU stalls all 16,383 others. The cluster is a chain with sixteen thousand links.

The defense is checkpointing: periodically writing the full training state — weights, optimizer moments, data-loader position, learning-rate step — to durable storage, so a failure costs only the work since the last checkpoint plus restart time. Checkpoints at this scale are enormous (weights, plus Adam state at roughly two extra copies, plus fp32 masters: several terabytes for a 400B-class model), so writing one costs real time, and there is an optimization problem: checkpoint too rarely and failures destroy hours of work; too often and the writing itself eats the run.

∑The math

The classic engineering answer is the Young–Daly interval. If writing a checkpoint takes time δ\deltaδ and the system’s mean time between failures is MMM, the checkpoint interval τ\tauτ that minimizes expected waste is approximately

τopt  =  2 δ M.\tau_{\text{opt}} \;=\; \sqrt{2\,\delta\, M}.τopt​=2δM​.

Sketch of the derivation: in each interval of length τ\tauτ you pay a fixed overhead δ\deltaδ (the write), i.e., a fractional cost δ/τ\delta/\tauδ/τ that falls as τ\tauτ grows. Meanwhile a failure lands at a uniformly random point in the current interval, destroying τ/2\tau/2τ/2 of work on average, at rate 1/M1/M1/M per unit time — a fractional cost τ/(2M)\tau/(2M)τ/(2M) that rises with τ\tauτ. Total waste rate w(τ)=δ/τ+τ/(2M)w(\tau) = \delta/\tau + \tau/(2M)w(τ)=δ/τ+τ/(2M); setting w′(τ)=−δ/τ2+1/(2M)=0w'(\tau) = -\delta/\tau^2 + 1/(2M) = 0w′(τ)=−δ/τ2+1/(2M)=0 gives τ2=2δM\tau^2 = 2\delta Mτ2=2δM, hence the square root.

✏️Worked example

Plug in Llama-3-like numbers. Failures: 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days ⇒M≈54×24×60/419≈186\Rightarrow M \approx 54 \times 24 \times 60 / 419 \approx 186⇒M≈54×24×60/419≈186 minutes. Suppose a full distributed checkpoint takes δ=5\delta = 5δ=5 minutes to write (asynchronous and sharded writers make this realistic). Then

τopt=2×5×186=1860≈43 minutes.\tau_{\text{opt}} = \sqrt{2 \times 5 \times 186} = \sqrt{1860} \approx 43 \text{ minutes.}τopt​=2×5×186​=1860​≈43 minutes.

So: checkpoint roughly every three-quarters of an hour. Sanity-check the waste: checkpoint overhead ≈5/43≈12%\approx 5/43 \approx 12\%≈5/43≈12%… which is why real systems work hard to shrink δ\deltaδ — with asynchronous checkpointing (copy state to host memory in seconds, let CPUs stream it to storage while training continues), the effective δ\deltaδ drops toward tens of seconds, the optimal interval tightens, and overhead falls under 1%. The formula is approximate, but it is the reasoning every infrastructure team runs.

✎Note

Recovery is not only about restoring state — it is about diagnosis at speed. When one GPU in 16,384 goes bad, finding which one is its own discipline: NCCL (the NVIDIA Collective Communications Library) flight recorders, background health checks between steps, binary-searching the cluster with test jobs. The Llama 3 team notes that only three of their 419 unexpected interruptions required significant manual intervention; the rest were handled by automation that detects the failure, evicts the bad node, pulls in a hot spare, and resumes from checkpoint. Building that automation is a large fraction of what “training infrastructure engineer” means.

The dashboard: what the crew actually watches

A training run is monitored like a spacecraft: continuously, by rotation, against a wall of time-series. The canonical dashboard (you can inspect a real one — the OLMo project published its full training logs, and OLMo-2’s public Weights & Biases boards are the closest you can get to standing in a lab’s control room) has a familiar cast:

  • Training loss, plotted raw and smoothed, against the projected trend from scaling fits — not “is it going down” but “is it going down on schedule.”
  • Pre-clip gradient norm — the seismograph. Slow upward drift means the run is migrating toward instability; sharp motion precedes spikes.
  • Parameter norms and update-to-weight ratios, per layer. A layer whose weight norm is growing anomalously, or whose updates are large relative to its weights, is a spike being scheduled.
  • Per-domain loss: separate curves for code, math, multilingual text, web text. The aggregate loss hides regressions; a data-pipeline bug that corrupts one source shows up here first.
  • Eval canaries: a small suite of benchmarks (the evaluation machinery of Chapter 21) run automatically on periodic checkpoints. Downstream ability sanity-checks the loss curve; a run whose loss falls while its canaries stagnate has a data-contamination or eval-pipeline problem worth catching in week one, not week nine.
  • Throughput and model FLOPs utilization (MFU) — the fraction of the cluster’s theoretical floating-point capacity the run uses (Chapter 33). A dip in MFU with loss still falling means a hardware or networking degradation: a straggler node, a flapping link, midday heat.
  • Infrastructure vitals: per-node temperatures, memory-error counts, interconnect bandwidth, storage queue depths.

Frontier labs staff this the way operations teams staff anything critical: on-call rotations — “babysitting” is the universal informal term — with runbooks for the common events (spike: check grad norm history, check the data shard, decide self-heal vs. rollback-and-skip), paging thresholds on the metrics above, and a log of every intervention. The OLMo and Llama reports both make plain that a frontier run has humans making judgment calls on it approximately daily.

The limits of reproducibility

One honest caveat belongs here, because it surprises people with a software background: a large training run is not bitwise reproducible in practice. Floating-point addition is not associative ((a+b)+c≠a+(b+c)(a+b)+c \ne a+(b+c)(a+b)+c=a+(b+c) in general at finite precision), and the order in which thousands of GPUs’ partial sums are reduced depends on timing, scheduling, and which nodes are participating. Change nothing but the set of healthy nodes after a restart, and the summation order — hence the low-order bits, hence eventually the trajectory — changes. Fully deterministic kernels exist but cost throughput, so labs generally settle for statistical reproducibility: same data, same seeds, same hyperparameters give a run whose loss curve is indistinguishable in distribution, not identical in bits. The practical consequences: a spike often cannot be exactly replayed (bit-for-bit) for study, “the same run twice” is a subtle claim, and debugging leans on checkpoints and logged state rather than on rerunning from step zero. Where determinism really matters — verifying a suspected silent-data-corruption event, say — teams pay the throughput tax temporarily.

The lifecycle of a run, end to end

Zoom all the way out. The weeks of GPU time are the visible middle of a longer arc:

Data ready. The Chapter 31 pipeline finishes: sources crawled, filtered, deduplicated, tokenized, and — importantly — the mixture (what fraction code, what fraction web, what fraction multilingual) frozen for the main phase, with the anneal mixture designed separately.

Hyperparameter scaling runs. Nobody tunes a frontier model on the frontier model. Instead: a scaling ladder — a sequence of small models (tens to hundreds of millions of parameters) trained under μP or an equivalent parametrization so that learning rate, initialization scales, and batch choices transfer upward, plus fits of the loss-versus-compute curves (Chapter 32) to predict what the big run should achieve. These weeks of small runs are cheap insurance on the one run that is not.

The main run. Weeks to months of everything in this chapter: the stable phase of the schedule, the ramps, the babysitting, the eight-failures-a-day drumbeat.

The anneal. The decay phase, run on the upgraded mixture — often as several anneals from different late checkpoints or with different data recipes, because anneals are cheap relative to the main run and the best one can be selected by evals.

The checkpoint zoo. A frontier run leaves behind hundreds of saved states. They are an asset, not detritus: intermediate checkpoints feed scaling-law fits and interpretability research (how do circuits emerge over training?), late checkpoints seed anneal experiments and long-context extension, and ablation forks branch from mid-run states. Open projects like OLMo publish the zoo outright.

The handoff. One checkpoint is chosen — by canary evals, by anneal performance, occasionally by taste — and becomes “the base model.” It goes to the post-training organization, and this book follows it there in Chapter 35.

⚙️In practice

A day in the life (generic and honest — no lab publishes its diary, but public reports, talks, and the OLMo logs triangulate to something like this). Morning: the on-call engineer from the overnight rotation hands off — two hardware evictions overnight, both auto-recovered from checkpoint, eleven minutes of lost work total; one grad-norm excursion at 02:40 that self-healed and is flagged for the data team because it coincides with a specific shard. Midday: the weekly canary evals come back; code loss is on trend but one multilingual domain is flat, which triggers an investigation that finds a deduplication bug upstream — fix goes into the anneal mixture, since the main mixture is frozen. Afternoon: throughput review; MFU is down 0.7% since Tuesday, traced to one host with rising memory-error counts, scheduled for eviction at the next checkpoint boundary. Evening: the run crosses a round-number token count; someone posts the loss curve to the internal channel; the next rotation takes the pager. Multiply by ninety days.

Where this leaves us

  • You can write down the two dominant learning-rate schedules — cosine decay and warmup–stable–decay — explain the two mechanical reasons warmup exists (cold Adam second-moment estimates; the ill-conditioned landscape at initialization), and say why WSD’s no-pre-commitment property made it the schedule of the open-model era.
  • You can state the linear and square-root learning-rate scaling heuristics as heuristics, and explain the critical batch size — why batches are ramped upward during training as the gradient signal-to-noise ratio falls.
  • You know why context extension is a late, staged phase of the run rather than a from-scratch property, and how it connects to RoPE scaling.
  • You can read a loss spike off a dashboard: pre-clip grad norm as the early warning, the three outcomes, the interaction-of-data-and-state cause, and the toolkit — clipping, z-loss (λlog⁡2Z\lambda \log^2 Zλlog2Z pinning the softmax normalizer), QK-norm, and the rollback-and-skip protocol.
  • You can do the checkpoint arithmetic: cluster MTBF from per-device failure rates, and the Young–Daly interval 2δM\sqrt{2\delta M}2δM​ — for Llama-3-like numbers, a failure every ~3 hours and checkpoints every ~45 minutes unless asynchronous checkpointing shrinks δ\deltaδ.
  • You know the dashboard’s cast (loss vs. trend, grad/param norms, per-domain loss, eval canaries, MFU) and the honest limits of reproducibility at scale.
  • You can narrate the whole lifecycle: data freeze → μP scaling ladder → main run → anneal(s) → checkpoint zoo → handoff.

The run is over; a base model exists. It can continue any text on the internet, which is astonishing and also not what anyone wants to ship. Turning that raw next-token predictor into an assistant is the subject of the next part of the book: Next: Post-Training I — SFT, Reward Models, RLHF.

Key papers & readings in this chapter
  • MiniCPM: Unveiling the Potential of Small Language Models with Scalable Training Strategies — Hu et al., 2024 The paper that popularized the warmup–stable–decay (WSD) schedule; Section 3 shows the loss cliff during the decay phase.
  • Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models — Hoffmann et al., 2022 Chinchilla. Besides the scaling laws, note the finding that the cosine cycle length must match the intended training length.
  • PaLM: Scaling Language Modeling with Pathways — Chowdhery et al., 2022 Section 5.1 is the classic public account of loss spikes and the rollback-and-skip protocol; also the source of the z-loss.
  • The Llama 3 Herd of Models — Grattafiori et al. (Llama Team), 2024 The most detailed public accounting of hardware failures during a frontier run — 466 interruptions in 54 days on 16k GPUs.
  • OLMo: Accelerating the Science of Language Models — Groeneveld et al., 2024 A fully open training run — code, data, logs, and checkpoints — the closest you can get to sitting in on a real run.
  • An Empirical Model of Large-Batch Training — McCandlish et al., 2018 Defines the gradient noise scale and the critical batch size — the theory behind batch-size ramping.
  • Accurate, Large Minibatch SGD: Training ImageNet in 1 Hour — Goyal et al., 2017 Origin of the linear learning-rate scaling rule and of gradual warmup as standard practice.
  • Tensor Programs V: Tuning Large Neural Networks via Zero-Shot Hyperparameter Transfer — Yang et al., 2022 μP — how labs tune hyperparameters on small models and transfer them to the big run.
