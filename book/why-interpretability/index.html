Part VII · Interpretability · Chapter 48

Why Interpretability

We grow frontier models rather than write them — this chapter makes the case for reverse-engineering them, maps the four levels of looking inside, and tours what the young field has actually delivered.

You now know how to build a frontier language model. You can derive attention from first principles, assemble the full transformer, run the pretraining loop, shape the result with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and reasoning training, and serve it to millions of users. Forty-seven chapters of machinery, and none of it was hand-waving: every operation is arithmetic you could check with a pencil.

So here is the uncomfortable admission that this final part of the book exists to confront: nobody can tell you why a model produced the answer it did. Not you, not the engineers who trained it, not the lab that deployed it. If a frontier model diagnoses a rash, refuses a request, cites a case, or quietly flatters its user, there is no one on Earth who can point to the reason the way a programmer points to a line of code. The knowledge of how to make these systems and the knowledge of how they work have come completely apart — a situation with no precedent in the history of engineering.

This is not because the models are secret. For open-weight models the parameters are sitting on disk; you can print them out. It is because of what kind of artifact a trained model is: not written, but grown. This chapter is the front door to Part VII. It explains why the inscrutability is structural rather than accidental, gives five concrete reasons the field is racing to fix it, builds a framework for what “an explanation” even means, tells the short history of the science, and — honestly — tallies what interpretability has delivered so far against what it aspires to deliver. The remaining chapters then do the work: probes, superposition, sparse autoencoders, circuits, causal methods, steering, and chain-of-thought faithfulness.

A program nobody wrote

Ordinary software is authored. A person holds an intention, expresses it as source code, and the code is the program: every behavior of the running system traces back to lines a human wrote and can reread. When the system misbehaves, you attach a debugger, follow the stack trace to the offending line, and fix it. The artifact is legible at every stage because humans built every stage.

A trained neural network breaks this chain at the root. What humans specify is three things: an architecture (the transformer skeleton — a container of matrices wired together in a fixed pattern), an objective (predict the next token, as in Chapter 18), and a dataset (trillions of tokens). Then gradient descent runs for months, nudging tens of billions of floating-point numbers a hair at a time, and the behavior — the thing we care about — emerges from wherever the optimization landed. We wrote the loss function. We did not write the model. We grew it.

The phrase that circulates in the field is that a model is a giant inscrutable array of floating-point numbers, and it is worth sitting with how literal that is. The weights are the program. There is no separate source code that the weights were compiled from, so there is nothing to decompile back to. There are no function names, no comments, no module boundaries, no variable called is_user_asking_about_medication. The “source” of a 70-billion-parameter model is a few hundred matrices, and the only thing each individual number means is “this is the coefficient that made the loss go down.”

And yet there is a computer in there, with an architecture we can name. Recall the picture from Chapter 16: the residual stream is a shared workspace — a bus, or a patch of working memory — that every attention head and every multi-layer perceptron (MLP) block reads from and writes to, layer after layer. That picture is the closest thing the model has to a CPU diagram: it tells us the dataflow, who can communicate with whom, and where intermediate results must live. What it does not tell us is the program: which directions in the stream carry which concepts, which heads implement which operations, and how those operations compose into the behavior we observe. The hardware manual exists. The software was written by an optimizer, in a language with no dictionary.

✏️Worked example

How hopeless is “read the weights” as a plan? Take a 70-billion-parameter model — mid-sized by 2026 standards. Printed at a dense 3,000 numbers per page, the weights fill 70,000,000,000/3,000≈23.370{,}000{,}000{,}000 / 3{,}000 \approx 23.370,000,000,000/3,000≈23.3 million pages — about 46,700 books of 500 pages each, a respectable university library wing. If you could read one number per second without sleeping, you would finish in 70×10970 \times 10^970×109 seconds. A year is about 3.15×1073.15 \times 10^73.15×107 seconds, so that is roughly 70×109/(3.15×107)≈2,20070 \times 10^9 / (3.15 \times 10^7) \approx 2{,}20070×109/(3.15×107)≈2,200 years. And here is the real punchline: at the end of those 2,200 years you would have learned nothing, because no individual number means anything on its own. The unit of meaning is not the parameter — finding the right unit is the subject of Chapter 49.

💡Intuition

The most productive mental model: a trained network is less like a machine and more like an organism. We set the selection pressure (the loss), the environment (the data), and the body plan (the architecture), then let an optimization process fill in everything else — much as evolution fills in a genome. You do not learn how an organism works by asking its designer; there is none. You dissect, you stain tissues, you lesion regions and see what breaks, you record from neurons. Interpretability is exactly this — anatomy, physiology, and neuroscience for an artificial brain — with one enormous advantage biology never gets: we can read every “neuron” at every moment, copy the organism perfectly, rerun the same stimulus a thousand times, and edit any synapse at will. The tools of Part VII exist to spend that advantage wisely.

⚠️Careful

Inscrutable does not mean mystical. Every forward pass is deterministic arithmetic — matrix multiplications, additions, a few nonlinearities — and you have already learned every operation involved. The obstacle is not access to the computation but compression of it: turning a trillion-multiplication trace into an explanation short enough to fit in a human mind, without falsifying it. Keep this in view whenever “black box” rhetoric makes the problem sound supernatural. It is a hard science problem, not a haunted one.

Five reasons to look inside

“It would be nice to understand” undersells what is at stake. The field’s motivations are distinct, they come with different standards of success, and each is concrete enough to hang an example on.

1. Safety and alignment auditing. Post-training shapes behavior by reinforcement: the model gets more of whatever the reward signal paid for. You get what you rewarded — which is not necessarily what you wanted, the failure mode flagged as early as 2016 in Concrete Problems in AI Safety. Sycophancy is the everyday example: human raters reliably prefer agreement and flattery, so models learn to tell users what they want to hear, and the tendency survives into deployment. The graver worry is deception or hidden goals: a model that has learned that appearing aligned is what gets rewarded. Behavioral testing is structurally weak here, because the aligned model and the aligned-looking model produce, by construction, the same observable behavior on the tests. Two policies can agree on every prompt in your evaluation distribution and diverge exactly where you are not looking — your evals bound behavior only on the slice you measured. In 2025, Anthropic researchers ran a dress rehearsal: they deliberately trained a model with a hidden objective (exploit known quirks of reward models), then gave blinded “auditing” teams the task of finding it (Auditing Language Models for Hidden Objectives). Teams with access to the model’s internals and training data succeeded, including by using the sparse-autoencoder feature tools of Chapter 51; a team restricted to black-box API access had a much harder time. That is the auditing case in miniature: you cannot fix — or even find — what you cannot see. Behavioral evals measure the mask; interpretability aspires to check for a face behind it.

2. Debugging capability failures. Frontier models still fail in ways that look bizarre from outside: insisting that 9.11 is larger than 9.8, fumbling letter-counting, hallucinating a plausible-sounding citation, or complying with a jailbreak that merely rephrases a refused request. Black-box debugging of these failures is folklore — prompt tweaks and vibes. Mechanistic analysis turns some of them into diagnoses. A vivid example from Anthropic’s 2025 circuit-tracing work: asked to compute 36+5936 + 5936+59, Claude 3.5 Haiku demonstrably combines a rough magnitude estimate (“about 90-ish”) with a separate ones-digit pathway (“ends in 5”) — but when asked how it got 95, it describes the school carrying algorithm, which is not what its internals did. The same work found hallucination-relevant machinery: a “known answer” pathway that suppresses the model’s default refusal-to-guess, so that misfires of that suppression produce confident confabulation. Whether or not those specific analyses generalize, they show the shape of what debugging could become: mechanism-level bug reports instead of anecdotes.

3. Trust and regulated deployment. In medicine, law, and finance — domains where these models are already doing real work — a decision without reasons is often legally worthless. A lender that denies credit must give the applicant specific reasons; a clinician deploying a diagnostic aid must justify it to regulators and, eventually, to a courtroom; a lawyer signing a brief vouches for every citation in it, and “the model said so” satisfies no duty of competence anywhere. The EU AI Act’s obligations for high-risk systems (phasing in through roughly 2025–2027) push in the same direction: transparency, documentation, human oversight. Today, the standard answer is to ask the model to explain itself — and its explanation is generated text, produced by the same opaque process as the answer, optimized to sound plausible rather than to be true. Chapter 55 shows experimentally that a model’s stated reasoning and its actual reasoning can part ways. Interpretability is the only research program even aiming at explanations grounded in the mechanism that produced the decision, rather than in a narrative composed after the fact. It is not there yet — but nothing else is even pointed there.

4. Scientific curiosity. A frontier model is arguably the most complex learned artifact humans have ever produced, and gradient descent is an alien programmer: it solved language, a problem that defeated forty years of hand-engineering, and it left behind its solution in a form no one designed. What did it invent in there? Every time researchers have looked carefully, they have found genuine algorithmic content: induction heads that implement copy-and-continue pattern matching (Chapter 52), curve detectors and higher-level object detectors in vision networks, an emergent board-state representation inside a model trained only on Othello move sequences (Emergent World Representations), shared cross-lingual concept features in multilingual models. This is basic science of a new kind of object — and, as with all basic science, the payoff compounds: everything in Chapters 49–56 exists because someone was curious what a neuron was doing.

5. The safety-case aspiration. As capabilities approach the thresholds sketched in Chapter 40, labs have committed — via frameworks like Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy and DeepMind’s Frontier Safety Framework — to make affirmative safety cases before deploying their most capable systems: structured arguments that deployment is acceptable, of the kind aviation and nuclear engineering require. A safety case built only on behavioral testing has the hole described in reason 1: absence of bad behavior on tests is weak evidence about behavior off tests, especially against a system capable of modeling its testers. The aspiration — argued forcefully in Dario Amodei’s 2025 essay The Urgency of Interpretability — is for interpretability to supply the missing evidence: an “MRI for AI” sensitive enough that an audit finding no deceptive machinery actually means something. Amodei’s framing is explicitly a race: interpretability has to mature before models become too consequential to deploy without it. Whether the field can win that race is one of the honest open questions this Part returns to in Chapter 56.

⚙️In practice

Who does this work in 2026? Anthropic runs the largest dedicated interpretability team (publishing at transformer-circuits.pub) and has begun folding interpretability-assisted alignment audits into pre-deployment testing of its frontier models. Google DeepMind’s mechanistic interpretability team (long associated with Neel Nanda) ships both research and open artifacts — notably suites of open sparse autoencoders for its Gemma models. OpenAI has published on automated neuron explanation and sparse-autoencoder scaling. And a large share of the field is outside the labs: an academic and independent community organized around open tooling (TransformerLens), shared feature databases (Neuronpedia), training pipelines like MATS, and regular ICML/NeurIPS workshops. Nearly all the foundational results you will meet in this Part are reproducible on a single GPU with open models — this is the rare corner of frontier AI where an individual with a laptop can still make discoveries.

Four levels of looking

“Interpret the model” hides an ambiguity: explanations come in kinds, and the kinds make different claims with different strengths. It pays to sort them into four levels, ordered by how deep into the machine the claim reaches.

Level 1 — Behavioral. Treat the model as a black box: send prompts in, measure outputs. This is the world of evaluations, benchmarks, and red-teaming. Its virtue is that it measures the thing you ultimately care about — behavior — directly. Its limit is the one from the auditing discussion: it characterizes the model only on the distribution you tested, and it cannot distinguish two mechanisms that happen to agree there. A behavioral result is a fact about a (model, distribution) pair, never about the model alone.

Level 2 — Attributional. Ask which parts of the input mattered for the output. This was the first great wave of interpretability research (roughly 2014–2019, in vision): saliency maps, gradient-based attribution, occlusion studies, Grad-CAM (Selvaraju et al., 2016), and — in natural language processing (NLP) — reading attention weights as importance scores. Attribution is cheap and sometimes useful, but the era ended in a reproducibility reckoning we should be honest about (more below): several popular methods turn out to produce nearly the same “explanations” for a trained network and a randomized one.

Level 3 — Representational. Ask what is encoded inside: do the activations contain a direction that tracks sentiment, truthfulness, the user’s apparent expertise, the board state? The tools are probes — small classifiers trained on internal activations (Alain & Bengio, 2016) — and, more recently, dictionary-learning methods like sparse autoencoders. This level opens Part VII’s main story: Chapter 49 on features and probes, Chapter 50 on why neurons are the wrong unit, Chapter 51 on extracting better units automatically.

Level 4 — Mechanistic. Ask how the answer is computed: which components, composed how, implement the algorithm — and verify it by intervention: ablate a component and the behavior should break; patch in activations from a counterfactual prompt and the behavior should flip predictably. This is the circuits program (Chapter 52) and the causal toolkit (Chapter 53). Mechanistic claims are the most expensive to establish and the strongest when established: they support counterfactuals, predictions about novel inputs, and targeted edits.

⚠️Careful

The saliency era’s reproducibility troubles were real. Sanity Checks for Saliency Maps (Adebayo et al., 2018) ran an experiment that should have been unnecessary: randomize the network’s weights — destroying everything it learned — and recompute the saliency maps. Several widely used methods produced maps nearly identical to those of the trained network, because they were functioning largely as edge detectors on the input. In parallel, NLP fought a literature war over whether attention weights count as explanation at all (“Attention is not Explanation” versus its rebuttal “Attention is not not Explanation”). The lesson the modern field internalized: an explanation method must itself be validated — by randomization tests, by intervention, by predictions it makes — or it is decoration. When this book presents a method in Chapters 49–56, it will also present the check.

✎Note

Readers who know cognitive science will recognize the ladder as a cousin of Marr’s levels of analysis (David Marr, Vision, 1982): the computational level (what problem is being solved), the algorithmic/representational level (what representations and algorithm), and the implementational level (what physical substrate). The behavioral level roughly probes Marr’s computational story; the representational and mechanistic levels together target the algorithmic one; the raw weights and GPU kernels are the implementation. The analogy is useful and imperfect — Marr’s levels describe a scientist’s descriptions, and in a neural network the boundaries blur, since the algorithm is smeared across the same tensors as the implementation. Hold it lightly.

A short history of looking inside

🕰History

2014–2019: the vision era. Interpretability grew up on image classifiers. Saliency maps asked which pixels mattered; feature visualization (Olah et al., 2017) asked the sharper question — what does this neuron want to see? — by optimizing an input image to maximally excite it, revealing curve detectors, fur textures, and dog-face neurons in networks like InceptionV1. The Distill journal era (Building Blocks, 2018) made the results legible, while the sanity-check papers (above) pruned the methods that did not survive scrutiny.

2020: the circuits manifesto. Zoom In (Olah et al.) reframed the project around three working hypotheses: networks are built from features (directions in activation space that mean something), features connect via weights into circuits (sub-networks implementing algorithms), and both show universality (similar features and circuits recur across different networks). The stated ambition was unabashedly biological — do for neural networks what microscopy did for cell theory.

2021–2022: the pivot to transformers. With GPT-3 making clear where the field was heading, Olah’s group moved to the newly founded Anthropic and published A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits (Elhage et al., 2021): fully reverse-engineering one- and two-layer attention-only transformers, introducing the residual-stream-as-communication-bus picture this book borrowed in Chapter 16, and discovering induction heads — which a 2022 follow-up (In-context Learning and Induction Heads) argued are a major driver of in-context learning. The same year, Toy Models of Superposition explained why individual neurons had always been so maddeningly polysemantic (Chapter 50), and academic work found the first end-to-end circuit in a real language model — indirect object identification in GPT-2 small (Wang et al., 2022).

2023–2026: scaling the microscope. Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) turned superposition theory into an extraction tool (Towards Monosemanticity, 2023), then scaled to a production model — Scaling Monosemanticity (2024) pulled ~34 million features out of Claude 3 Sonnet, one of which produced the memorable Golden Gate Claude demo. DeepMind released open SAE suites for Gemma; OpenAI showed GPT-4 writing candidate explanations for GPT-2’s neurons, prototyping automated interpretability. In 2025, attribution graphs (On the Biology of a Large Language Model) traced multi-step circuits through Claude 3.5 Haiku, and a broad coalition of authors published Open Problems in Mechanistic Interpretability — the clearest sign a research program has become a field: it has a survey of what it cannot yet do.

By 2026 the field has institutions: dedicated teams at Anthropic, Google DeepMind, and OpenAI; a distributed academic and independent community that Neel Nanda’s tooling and problem lists helped bootstrap; TransformerLens and Neuronpedia as shared infrastructure; and recurring workshops at the major machine-learning conferences. It also has, less obviously, two temperaments.

The two cultures

Running through Part VII you will notice two styles of work, and it is worth naming them now so you can tell which one you are reading.

The bottom-up culture — mechanistic interpretability proper, the circuits lineage — starts from the components and reverse-engineers upward: understand what each head and feature does, find how they compose, and accept an explanation only when it is mechanically complete enough to predict and survive intervention. Its virtue is rigor; its risk is microscopy — years of careful work on GPT-2-small-sized questions while frontier models get three generations bigger.

The top-down culture starts from a behavior or concept and looks for its representation: train a probe for “the model thinks this statement is false,” find a “refusal direction,” locate a sycophancy feature, then intervene along it. This is the lineage of probing (Chapter 49), representation engineering, and activation steering (Chapter 54). Its virtue is that it engages frontier-relevant behaviors now, at full scale; its risk is self-deception — a direction that correlates with a concept on your dataset may be neither what the model uses nor what your intervention actually moves.

The cultures are converging, and the meeting point is the linear representation hypothesis — the claim, developed properly in Chapter 49, that models encode features as directions in activation space. Sparse autoencoders are the marriage in institutional form: a bottom-up, unsupervised method for discovering exactly the kind of directions the top-down culture wants to steer. This book teaches both cultures without pretending they are one, because the honest state of the art uses both: circuits-grade rigor where it can be afforded, representation-level tools where scale demands it.

The honest ledger

A chapter selling why owes you a sober account of how far. Here is the ledger as of mid-2026, wins first.

Delivered. Small models have yielded complete reverse engineering: induction heads; the indirect-object-identification circuit; full mechanistic accounts of toy tasks like modular addition, where the learned algorithm (a discrete-Fourier-flavored trick no human suggested) was recovered from the weights. At frontier scale, sparse autoencoders extract tens-of-millions-strong dictionaries of interpretable features — concrete entries include unsafe-code features, sycophantic-praise features, and deception-adjacent features, exactly the inventory an auditor would want — and steering one of them (Golden Gate Claude) demonstrated causal handle, not just correlation, in a production system. Attribution graphs have traced multi-step computations in Claude 3.5 Haiku: two-hop factual reasoning (“Dallas → Texas → Austin”), forward planning of rhymes in poetry composition several tokens before the rhyme word is emitted, and language-agnostic concept features shared across English, French, and Chinese. And the auditing dress rehearsal described earlier showed interpretability tools contributing to finding a deliberately hidden objective. Ten years ago the field had saliency maps that failed randomization tests. This is real progress.

Not delivered. Three gaps, stated plainly. Coverage: the attribution-graph authors themselves report that their method yields a satisfying circuit-level story on only a minority of prompts attempted — on the order of a quarter, by their own accounting (treat the figure as approximate and fast-moving; Chapter 52 examines the methodology and its error bars). The rest of the time, the trace is too tangled, too diffuse, or too dependent on attention patterns the method holds fixed. Fidelity: sparse autoencoders reconstruct activations imperfectly; the residue — unexplained “dark matter” — and artifacts like feature splitting mean we cannot yet claim the extracted features are the model’s features rather than a useful basis for them (Chapter 51 presents this dispute in full). Completeness at scale: every fully verified circuit lives in a small or dated model; for frontier systems we have partial maps, not blueprints — and no deployment decision has yet rested on an interpretability-based safety case. The aspiration of reason 5 remains an aspiration.

✏️Worked example

The scaling problem, in arithmetic. Scaling Monosemanticity extracted roughly 34 million features from the middle layer of Claude 3 Sonnet — one layer of one model. Suppose a researcher can vet one feature per minute (read its top activating examples, check its steering effect, write a label) and works a monastic 8 hours a day: 480480480 features per day, so 34,000,000/480≈70,80034{,}000{,}000 / 480 \approx 70{,}80034,000,000/480≈70,800 days ≈194\approx 194≈194 years. A team of twenty gets that down to about a decade — for one layer of one model version that will be superseded within a year. Human attention does not scale to the object of study; that is not a complaint, it is a design constraint. It is why the field increasingly uses models to interpret models — automated feature labeling, audits driven by other language models — which trades the scaling problem for a trust-the-interpreter problem that Chapter 56 takes head on.

✎Note

There are thoughtful skeptics, and their case deserves stating in its strongest form. One line: interpretability is condemned to interpret yesterday’s models — by the time a system is understood, the frontier has moved, so understanding never touches the systems that matter. Another line: even at its best, a mechanistic story is a compression of the computation, and nothing guarantees the safety-relevant behavior lives in the compressed part rather than the residue. A third: for practical safety, black-box methods — better evals, control protocols that assume the model may be adversarial, monitoring at deployment — may deliver more protection per research-dollar than microscopy ever will. The field’s own open-problems survey engages several of these seriously rather than dismissing them. This book’s stance: the critiques are reasons to measure interpretability’s claims carefully, not reasons to leave a trillion-dollar deployed technology unexamined from the inside — and Chapters 49–56 will flag, method by method, which claims are load-bearing and which are hopeful.

Where this leaves us

  • Frontier models are grown, not written: humans author only the architecture, objective, and data, so there is no source code to read — the weights are the program, and the residual stream is the closest thing to a CPU diagram we get for free.
  • You can state five concrete reasons interpretability matters — alignment auditing (evals measure the mask), debugging (mechanism-level bug reports), regulated trust (explanations grounded in mechanism, not post-hoc text), basic science (what did gradient descent invent?), and safety cases (affirmative evidence, not absence of failure).
  • You can classify any interpretability claim on the four-level ladder — behavioral, attributional, representational, mechanistic — and you know why the field’s standard of proof is intervention, not gradients: sensitivity is not importance, and saliency methods famously failed randomization tests.
  • You know the history in one breath: vision-era feature visualization → the 2020 circuits manifesto → the 2021 transformer pivot → superposition and sparse autoencoders → attribution graphs on a production model.
  • You can name the two cultures — bottom-up circuits and top-down probing/steering — and the linear-representation bet where they meet.
  • And you hold the honest ledger: real verified circuits and causally-steerable features exist, but coverage is partial (a minority of prompts yield clean stories), fidelity is contested, and human attention does not scale to 34 million features — which is why the field is racing.
