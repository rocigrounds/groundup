Inference: Sampling, Quantization, Serving

How a trained model becomes a product: sampling strategies from greedy to min-p, speculative decoding, quantization to 8 and 4 bits, and the batching, paging, and economics behind every API token.

In this chapter
  1. From logits to text: the decoding menu
  2. Speculative decoding: a bigger model at a smaller model’s speed
  3. Quantization: shrinking the numbers
  4. Serving: the systems layer
  5. What a token costs
  6. Where this leaves us

Everything in this book so far has been about making a model: the math, the architecture, the data, the training run, the post-training. Suppose all of that succeeded. You now own a file of, say, 140 gigabytes of numbers, and a user has typed a question. Three problems stand between the file and a good answer, and none of them were covered by training.

First, the model does not emit text — it emits a probability distribution over its vocabulary, roughly 100,000 numbers, once per token. Someone has to decide how to turn distributions into actual words, and that decision — greedy? random? something in between? — changes the output more than most people expect. Second, the file is enormous and graphics processing units (GPUs) are not: serving the model at all, let alone cheaply, means shrinking those numbers and being ruthless about how they move through memory. Third, you are not serving one user. A real endpoint juggles thousands of concurrent conversations of wildly different lengths, and the difference between a naive scheduler and a good one is an order of magnitude in cost.

This chapter is those three problems in order: decoding (the sampling knobs you have seen in every AI playground, done properly, plus speculative decoding — the closest thing to a free lunch in this field), quantization (what “int4” means and why it mostly works), and serving (continuous batching, PagedAttention, structured output, and the economics that let token prices fall more than a hundredfold between 2022 and 2026). It leans on two earlier chapters constantly: the key–value (KV) cache, which established that decoding is a memory-bandwidth problem, and compute and precision, which established what the hardware can and cannot do. If you remember one sentence from each: decode speed is set by how fast you can read the weights, and a number’s format is a budget, not a fact.

From logits to text: the decoding menu

At each step the model produces a logit — a raw score — for every token in the vocabulary, and a softmax turns the logits into probabilities. Training taught the model to make these probabilities good. It said nothing about what to do with them. That choice is the decoding strategy, and it runs entirely outside the network: same weights, same forward pass, different text.

To compare strategies fairly, we will reuse one running example throughout this section. The prompt is “The lighthouse keeper opened the door and saw”, and the model’s next-token distribution (after softmax, temperature 1) starts:

tokenprobability
” the”0.30
” a”0.21
” nothing”0.14
” her”0.09
” two”0.06
” something”0.05
” that”0.04

Seven plausible continuations carry 89% of the mass; the remaining 11% is smeared over tens of thousands of tokens that are individually absurd — ” purple”, ” therefore”, ” §”. Every decoding strategy is a different answer to the question: how much of this distribution do you trust?

💡Intuition

Think of the distribution as the model casting a weighted vote, and the sampler as the election rules. Training fixed the votes; the rules are yours to choose. “Always seat the plurality winner” (greedy) is one rule; “seat a candidate at random, in proportion to votes, but strike everyone polling below some bar first” is another — and the entire menu below is variations on where to put that bar. Nothing in this section touches the model; it is all election law.

Greedy decoding and the repetition trap

The obvious strategy: always take the argmax. Here that means ” the”, forever and deterministically. Greedy decoding is fine for one token. Over a long generation it fails in a characteristic, almost comical way: the text falls into loops. “I’m not sure what to do. I’m not sure what to do. I’m not sure…” — if you used language models in the GPT-2 era you have seen this firsthand.

The loop is self-reinforcing. Holtzman et al. (2019) showed that once a model has emitted a phrase twice, the probability of emitting it a third time goes up — repetition is evidence of a repetitive document, so the model, faithfully modeling the distribution of documents, leans in. Greedy decoding has no randomness to break the cycle, so it spirals. There is a deeper issue too: human text is not a sequence of maximally likely tokens. Measured against real prose, models assign the actually written next word middling probability all the time — surprise is a feature of natural language (recall entropy: informative text must be somewhat unpredictable). A decoder that only ever picks the mode produces text that is more probable than any human would write, and it reads that way: flat, looping, weirdly evasive.

⚠️Careful

“Temperature 0” in an API is greedy decoding (or close to it), and people reach for it wanting reliability. For short, format-constrained outputs that is reasonable; for anything longer, it courts the repetition trap — and you often do not even get determinism, because batched GPU reductions are not bit-exact across runs (floating-point addition is not associative, and the summation order varies — see Chapter 33).

That worked example contains the central tension of this whole section. Low temperature is safe but dull. High temperature is diverse but hands the microphone to a 50,000-token mob of nonsense, because flattening helps the tail far more than it helps the runners-up — there are so many tail tokens that even a tiny boost each adds up to a landslide. Every remaining sampling method is a way to get diversity without inviting the mob.

Top-k and top-p: cutting off the tail

Top-k sampling is the blunt fix: keep the kkk highest-probability tokens, zero out everything else, renormalize, sample. With k=4k = 4k=4 on our example we keep ” the”, ” a”, ” nothing”, ” her” (total mass 0.74) and renormalize to 0.41, 0.28, 0.19, 0.120.41,\ 0.28,\ 0.19,\ 0.120.41, 0.28, 0.19, 0.12. The mob is gone; you can now crank temperature within the survivors without catastrophe.

The weakness is that kkk is fixed while the distribution is not. Compare two prompts:

  • “The capital of France is” — the model puts, say, 0.97 on ” Paris”. A top-k of 40 (a common default) keeps ” Paris” plus 39 tokens the model has all but ruled out, and sampling occasionally picks one. The cutoff is too loose.
  • “My favorite color is” — dozens of tokens are reasonable, with the best around 0.08. A top-k of 5 amputates most of the legitimate options. The cutoff is too tight.

No single kkk serves both. Top-p sampling — nucleus sampling, from the same Holtzman et al. paper — makes the cutoff adaptive: sort tokens by probability, keep the smallest set whose cumulative probability reaches ppp, renormalize, sample. The kept set (the “nucleus”) stretches and shrinks with the model’s confidence.

✏️Worked example

Where top-k and top-p differ. Take p=0.75p = 0.75p=0.75 on both prompts above. For the France prompt, ” Paris” alone (0.97) already exceeds 0.75 — the nucleus is one token, and sampling is effectively greedy, which is what you want when the model is that sure. For the color prompt, reaching 0.75 might take 25 tokens — all of them reasonable — so the nucleus is wide, which is also what you want. A fixed k=5k = 5k=5 would have been simultaneously too loose for the first prompt (keeping 4 junk tokens alongside ” Paris”) and too tight for the second (cutting 20 fine answers).

On the lighthouse distribution, p=0.75p = 0.75p=0.75: cumulative mass runs 0.30→0.51→0.65→0.740.30 \to 0.51 \to 0.65 \to 0.740.30→0.51→0.65→0.74 after four tokens — still short of 0.75 — and adding ” two” brings it to 0.800.800.80. The nucleus is the top five tokens, renormalized by dividing by 0.80.

Min-p: the recent favorite

Top-p has a subtle failure mode of its own at high temperature. The nucleus is defined by cumulative mass, so when the distribution is flat-ish, the nucleus can extend deep into mediocre territory just to reach the quota — and if you then apply a high temperature to the survivors, the mediocre entries get boosted. Min-p sampling (Nguyen et al., 2024) replaces the cumulative criterion with a confidence-relative one: keep every token whose probability is at least pbase×pmax⁡p_{\text{base}} \times p_{\max}pbase​×pmax​, where pmax⁡p_{\max}pmax​ is the top token’s probability and pbasep_{\text{base}}pbase​ is the knob (typically 0.05–0.1). In words: keep everything within a fixed factor of the best option.

On the lighthouse distribution with an aggressive pbase=0.2p_{\text{base}} = 0.2pbase​=0.2: the threshold is 0.2×0.30=0.060.2 \times 0.30 = 0.060.2×0.30=0.06, keeping the five tokens at or above 0.06 and cutting the rest. On the France prompt the same setting gives a threshold of 0.2×0.97=0.1940.2 \times 0.97 = 0.1940.2×0.97=0.194 — only ” Paris” survives. The cutoff tightens automatically when the model is confident and relaxes when it is not, like top-p, but it is anchored to the shape of the head rather than a mass quota, which turns out to be much more robust when you push temperature to 1.5 or 2 for creative writing. That robustness — coherent output at temperatures where top-p degenerates — is the paper’s headline result, and it is why min-p spread through the open-source ecosystem (llama.cpp, vLLM, SGLang, most frontend UIs) unusually fast.

One mechanical detail that trips people up: in most implementations the truncation filters (top-k, top-p, min-p) are applied to the raw distribution first, and temperature reshapes the survivors last. That ordering is what makes “min-p 0.1, temperature 1.8” sane — the mob is ejected before the flattening starts. Orders do vary across APIs and libraries, though, and the same knob settings can behave differently across stacks; when a sampling config transfers badly between two serving engines, the sampler order is the first thing to check.

Repetition penalties, beam search, and the likelihood trap

Two more knobs round out the playground. Repetition penalties directly punish tokens that have already appeared: the classic version (from the CTRL paper, Keskar et al., 2019) divides the logits of already-seen tokens by a constant θ≈1.1\theta \approx 1.1θ≈1.1–1.31.31.3, while the frequency/presence penalties in OpenAI-style APIs subtract a count-scaled or flat amount from the logit instead. They are crude but useful seat belts against loops — and they backfire on text that should repeat, like a variable name appearing fifty times in generated code. Modern instruction-tuned models loop far less than their GPT-2-era ancestors, and current practice keeps these penalties at or near off by default.

Beam search is the strategy this menu conspicuously lacks, and its absence is a lesson. Greedy decoding maximizes probability locally, one token at a time, which need not find the most probable sequence — a slightly worse token now can enable much better tokens later. Beam search approximates the global argmax by carrying the BBB best partial sequences forward at each step (with a length-normalization correction, since longer sequences always have lower raw probability). For machine translation and speech recognition it wins outright, and it ruled the seq2seq era you may remember from Chapter 22: those tasks have a tightly constrained correct output, and hunting for the modal sequence is precisely the job.

For open-ended generation, beam search is a disaster, and the reason is honest and important: likelihood and quality come apart. The most probable long continuation of a story prompt is bland, hedging, and eventually repetitive — Holtzman et al. measured beam-search text assigning itself per-token probabilities far above anything humans produce, while looping visibly. The model is not broken; the objective is wrong. We trained the model to score sequences, and it scores generic text as safe and probable — correctly, as a matter of statistics on its training distribution. Good open-ended text is not the likeliest text; it is a sample from the distribution, tail responsibly removed. That single sentence is the theory of everything above: sampling methods exist because we want typicality, not maximality.

⚙️In practice

Defaults in the wild, 2026 (approximate, and every provider tinkers): chat/assistant endpoints typically run temperature 0.7–1.0 with top-p 0.9–0.95; code models run cooler (0–0.4); creative-writing setups increasingly run min-p 0.05–0.1 with temperature 1.0–1.5 instead of top-p; agentic loops (see Chapter 44) run near-greedy for tool calls and warmer for prose. Reasoning models are a special case: several providers recommend or force a fixed temperature (often 0.6–1.0) because heavy chain-of-thought reportedly degenerates at temperature 0 — a modern echo of the repetition trap.

Speculative decoding: a bigger model at a smaller model’s speed

Recall the punchline of Chapter 28: during decode, generating one token requires streaming every weight of the model through the GPU’s memory system, and the arithmetic along the way is trivial by comparison. The GPU’s compute units sit mostly idle, waiting on memory. A concrete roofline: an H100’s high-bandwidth memory (HBM) delivers roughly 3.3 TB/s, so a 140 GB model (70B parameters in bf16) can be read at most about 24 times per second — about 24 tokens per second for a single stream, no matter how clever the kernels, and real systems get a fraction of that.

Idle compute is an opportunity. Here is the key asymmetry: verifying kkk proposed tokens costs about the same as generating one, because the model can score all kkk positions in a single forward pass (that is exactly the parallelism that makes training efficient — teacher forcing, from Chapter 18). Generation is serial; checking is parallel.

Speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2022; independently Chen et al., 2023) exploits this with a draft-and-verify loop:

  1. A small, fast draft model (say 1B parameters, ideally distilled from the target) generates γ\gammaγ tokens autoregressively — cheap, because the draft’s weights are 70× smaller.
  2. The big target model runs one forward pass over all γ\gammaγ drafted positions at once, producing its own distribution at each position.
  3. A probabilistic acceptance rule walks left to right, keeping drafted tokens the target agrees with and stopping at the first rejection, where the target’s own (corrected) distribution supplies a replacement token.

If the draft guesses well — and for easy stretches of text, small models guess very well — you get several tokens per target-model pass instead of one.

💡Intuition

A junior writer and a senior editor. The junior drafts sentences quickly; the senior can judge a whole drafted passage in one read far faster than she could compose it herself, because judging is parallel and composing is serial. Wherever the junior’s draft matches what the senior would have written, it stands; at the first place it does not, the senior crosses out the rest and writes the next word herself. The final text is, word for word, text the senior fully endorses — she has simply been spared typing most of it.

With the right acceptance rule, the output tokens are distributed exactly as if the target model had done all the sampling itself — not approximately, exactly. Speculation changes the speed and nothing else.

⚙️In practice

Real speedups run 2–3× for a well-matched draft (the acceptance rate is what matters; drafts distilled from the target, or trained alongside it, do best), with easy text — boilerplate code, formatted output, quoted passages — accepting nearly everything and dense reasoning accepting less. The 2024–2026 variants mostly attack the cost or awkwardness of a separate draft model: Medusa bolts extra lightweight decoding heads onto the target itself so it drafts its own future tokens; EAGLE (the strongest family as of 2026) drafts autoregressively in the target’s feature space rather than token space, reusing the target’s top layer; self-speculative methods draft with a layer-skipping shallow copy of the target; and prompt lookup / n-gram drafting simply copies candidate continuations out of the prompt — nearly free and effective for retrieval-heavy and editing workloads, where the answer largely restates the context. One honest caveat: speculation shines at small batch sizes (latency-sensitive serving, local inference). At the huge batch sizes of a saturated production server, the “idle compute” it exploits is no longer idle, and the win shrinks or vanishes — so providers apply it selectively.

Quantization: shrinking the numbers

Chapter 33 introduced number formats as budgets: fp32 spends 4 bytes per number, bf16 spends 2. Quantization continues down the staircase — int8 spends 1 byte, int4 half a byte — and it matters twice over. Capacity: a 70B-parameter model is 140 GB in bf16, 70 GB in int8, and roughly 35 GB in int4 — the difference between needing a multi-GPU server and fitting on a single 48 GB card (or a MacBook). Speed: decode is memory-bandwidth-bound, so weights that are 4× smaller stream through memory nearly 4× faster. Quantization is the rare optimization that improves cost, latency, and accessibility at once — the catch is that it is lossy, and the loss must be managed.

What int8 and int4 storage actually means

An integer format cannot store 0.0173. The trick is to store integers plus a shared scale: pick a scale sss for a group of weights, store each weight as the integer round(w/s)\mathrm{round}(w/s)round(w/s), and reconstruct w^=s⋅q\hat{w} = s \cdot qw^=s⋅q at compute time. Int8 gives integers in [−127,127][-127, 127][−127,127]; int4 gives [−7,7][-7, 7][−7,7] (one pattern is spent on sign or a zero-point, depending on the scheme).

💡Intuition

It is shared scientific notation. Instead of every number carrying its own exponent (which is what floating point is), a whole group of neighbors agrees on one exponent — the scale — and each member stores only a small integer mantissa. The agreement is cheap when neighbors have similar magnitudes and expensive when one of them is wildly larger, because the shared exponent must stretch to cover the giant while everyone else loses resolution. Quantization schemes are, at bottom, strategies for drawing group boundaries and handling giants.

✏️Worked example

Quantize the weight group [0.2, −0.5, 1.1, −1.7][0.2,\ -0.5,\ 1.1,\ -1.7][0.2, −0.5, 1.1, −1.7] to int8 with absmax scaling. The largest magnitude is 1.7, so s=1.7/127=0.0134s = 1.7 / 127 = 0.0134s=1.7/127=0.0134. The stored integers are round(w/s)=[15, −37, 82, −127]\mathrm{round}(w/s) = [15,\ -37,\ 82,\ -127]round(w/s)=[15, −37, 82, −127], and reconstruction gives [0.201, −0.495, 1.098, −1.700][0.201,\ -0.495,\ 1.098,\ -1.700][0.201, −0.495, 1.098, −1.700] — worst-case error about s/2≈0.0067s/2 \approx 0.0067s/2≈0.0067, under 1% of the range. Cheap and nearly harmless.

The scale balloons to 20/127=0.15720/127 = 0.15720/127=0.157, and the small weights are crushed onto a coarse grid: 0.2→round(1.27)=1→0.1570.2 \to \mathrm{round}(1.27) = 1 \to 0.1570.2→round(1.27)=1→0.157, an error of 21%. One extreme value ruined the precision of everything sharing its scale. Hold that thought.

Because one scale per tensor is too coarse, practical schemes use group-wise quantization: one scale per group of (typically) 32–128 consecutive weights. The scales themselves are stored in 16-bit, adding overhead — a “4-bit” model really spends about 4.2–5 bits per weight once scales and zero-points are counted.

Why weights tolerate it — and activations resist

That quantization works at all is a statement about redundancy. A trained network’s weights are massively overparameterized for the function they compute (Chapter 13 made this point for generalization; here it pays a systems dividend), their distribution in each layer is roughly bell-shaped with few extremes, and the network’s output is the sum of so many small contributions that per-weight rounding noise largely averages out. Empirically, 8-bit weights are nearly free, and careful 4-bit costs only a little.

Activations are a different story, and the reason is one of the great empirical findings of the scaling era. Dettmers et al. (2022) — the LLM.int8() paper — found that transformers above roughly 6.7B parameters develop outlier features: a handful of hidden dimensions (a few dozen out of thousands) whose activation magnitudes run up to ~100× larger than the rest, consistently, across layers and across tokens. These are not noise; ablating them destroys the model, and they appear systematically as models grow. (You have already met their attention-side cousins: the attention sinks of Chapter 28 — current interpretability work views both as the model building high-magnitude “utility” structure into specific channels.) Our worked example above is exactly what outliers do to quantization: one 100× channel sharing a scale with ordinary channels grinds the ordinary ones into dust. LLM.int8()‘s fix was surgical — detect the outlier dimensions and compute just those columns in 16-bit, quantizing the well-behaved 99.9% — and the general lesson stuck: weights quantize easily; activations need special handling around outliers. This is why “W4A16” (4-bit weights, 16-bit activations) is the default recipe for open-model deployment, while fully quantized activations (W8A8, FP8) demand more machinery.

GPTQ and AWQ, at intuition level

Naive rounding treats every weight independently. The two methods that made 4-bit respectable both exploit the fact that we know more than that.

GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2022) descends from the classic “optimal brain surgeon” line of pruning work: what matters is not the weight error but the layer output error on realistic inputs. GPTQ quantizes each layer’s weights a column at a time and, after rounding each column, updates the not-yet-quantized weights to compensate for the error just introduced — using second-order information (a Hessian approximation built from a few hundred calibration examples) to know which compensations matter. Rounding errors are not eliminated; they are steered into directions the layer’s inputs rarely excite. The whole procedure runs layer by layer in a few GPU-hours, with no retraining.

AWQ (Lin et al., 2023) starts from an observation in the spirit of LLM.int8(): a small fraction of weight channels — roughly 1% — carry most of the quality, and you can find them by looking at activation magnitudes, not weight magnitudes (a weight matters because of what multiplies it). Rather than keeping those channels in 16-bit (mixed precision is hardware-unfriendly), AWQ rescales: multiply the salient channels’ weights up by a factor and the corresponding activations down by the same factor — mathematically a no-op, since the products are unchanged — so that after scaling, the important weights sit large relative to the quantization grid and suffer proportionally less rounding error. Then quantize everything uniformly. Both methods routinely hold 4-bit perplexity within a whisker of 16-bit; GPTQ tends to squeeze harder at very low bits, AWQ tends to generalize better off the calibration distribution — and in practice the ecosystem uses both, plus round-trip descendants of each.

Formats in the wild, and quantizing the cache

If you download open models in 2026, quantization mostly reaches you through formats. GGUF is llama.cpp’s file format and the lingua franca of local inference; its “K-quants” (names like Q4_K_M — roughly 4.8 bits per weight) mix group-wise schemes and per-layer bit allocations, and its importance-matrix variants fold in AWQ-style calibration. MLX is Apple’s framework for the same job on Apple-silicon unified memory. bitsandbytes provides on-the-fly int8 and NF4 quantization inside PyTorch — NF4 (“normal float 4”) being the information-theoretically motivated 4-bit format from QLoRA that Chapter 38 used for fine-tuning on consumer hardware. And at the frontier, quantization has moved into pretraining: DeepSeek-V3 famously trained in FP8, and FP8 serving (weights and activations, with hardware support from Hopper/Blackwell tensor cores) is now a standard production configuration rather than a compression afterthought.

The other tensor worth shrinking is the KV cache. Recall the arithmetic from Chapter 28: a Llama-3.1-70B-class model (80 layers, 8 KV heads of dimension 128, grouped-query attention) stores 2×80×8×128×22 \times 80 \times 8 \times 128 \times 22×80×8×128×2 bytes ≈0.33\approx 0.33≈0.33 MB per token in fp16 — about 43 GB for a single 128k-token context. At long context the cache, not the weights, dominates memory, and it is read every step. Quantizing cached keys and values to 8-bit is close to free; 4-bit KV costs a little quality (keys are touchier than values, because errors in keys distort which tokens get attended to, not just what they contribute) and halves memory again. Every serious serving stack and llama.cpp expose KV quantization flags, and for long-context workloads they are often the difference between fitting and not.

The honest bits-versus-quality table

Numbers below are rough consensus as of 2026 — they vary by model family, size, and task, so treat the pattern as the content:

Two honest footnotes. First, degradation is uneven: a 4-bit model matches 16-bit on easy benchmarks and gives back several points on the hardest reasoning tasks — headline “no quality loss” claims usually cite the easy ones. Long chains of generation also compound small per-step distortions in ways single-shot perplexity does not measure, which is why agentic workloads are the most quantization-sensitive. Second, bigger models degrade more gracefully — a 4-bit 70B comfortably beats a 16-bit 8B model at equal memory, which is the practical argument that usually settles the question: at a fixed memory budget, more parameters at fewer bits wins.

Serving: the systems layer

Everything so far concerned one request. A production endpoint faces thousands at once, and its performance is measured on two axes that trade off against each other. Latency is what a single user feels, and it splits into TTFT (time to first token — dominated by the prefill phase, where the prompt is processed in one compute-bound pass) and TPOT (time per output token, also called inter-token latency — dominated by the bandwidth-bound decode phase). Throughput (total tokens per second across all users) is what the operator’s accountant feels. The lever connecting them is batching: processing many requests’ decode steps together reuses one read of the weights across the whole batch, multiplying throughput enormously — recall from the roofline above that a single stream leaves the compute units almost idle, so a second stream is nearly free, and so is the sixty-fourth. But batches add scheduling delay and slow each individual stream, so serving is one long negotiation between the two axes.

Continuous batching

The naive scheduler — static batching — collects a batch of requests, runs them to completion together, then starts the next batch. The flaw is that generation lengths vary wildly: one request wants 10 tokens, its batchmate wants 800. Under static batching the short request’s slot sits idle for 790 steps, and newly arrived requests wait outside until the longest member of the current batch finishes. Utilization craters.

The fix, introduced by the Orca system (Yu et al., OSDI 2022) and now universal, is continuous batching: schedule at the granularity of a single decode step, not a whole request. Every step, the engine assembles whichever requests are active; when a request emits its end-of-sequence token it leaves the batch immediately, and a waiting request takes its place at the very next step. The batch becomes a rolling population rather than a convoy. The insight is small once seen — decode steps are independent iterations, so nothing forces requests to stay grouped — and it is worth several-fold throughput at realistic traffic all by itself.

PagedAttention and prefix caching

Continuous batching creates a memory problem. Each request’s KV cache grows token by token to an unpredictable final length, and pre-2023 systems handled this the obvious way: reserve one contiguous slab per request, sized for the maximum possible length. The result was textbook fragmentation — memory reserved but never used, plus unusable gaps between slabs — and the vLLM authors (Kwon et al., 2023) measured existing systems wasting most of their KV memory this way, with actual token data occupying as little as a fifth of the reservation.

Their fix, PagedAttention, is operating-systems thinking transplanted whole: virtual memory. Chop every KV cache into fixed-size blocks (16 tokens each, in vLLM’s default), keep a pool of physical blocks anywhere in GPU memory, and give each request a block table mapping its logical positions to physical blocks — exactly a page table. Requests grow one block at a time with zero external fragmentation and at most one partially filled block of waste each; the attention kernel follows the table indirection. Serving capacity — hence batch size, hence throughput — jumped 2–4× from memory efficiency alone, and vLLM became the reference open-source serving engine.

Paging enables a second trick with outsized economic consequences: prefix sharing. If two requests begin with the same tokens — the same 2,000-token system prompt, say, or the same conversation history in a multi-turn chat — their KV blocks for that prefix are identical, so the block tables can point at one physical copy, computed once (copy-on-write handles divergence, exactly as in an OS). SGLang generalized this with RadixAttention, which keeps a tree of all cached prefixes across requests. Commercial APIs surface the same idea as prompt caching: as of 2026, cached input tokens are typically billed at roughly 10–50% of the fresh-input price depending on provider (approximate and fast-moving) — which is why the standard advice for agents is to keep the long, stable part of the prompt (system message, tool definitions) at the front, byte-for-byte identical across calls, so it stays cache-hit.

Disaggregation, structured output, streaming

Three more pieces complete the 2026 serving picture.

Prefill/decode disaggregation. Prefill is compute-bound and bursty; decode is bandwidth-bound and steady. Batched together, a newly arrived long prompt stalls every in-flight stream’s decode step — that hiccup mid-reply you have felt in chat UIs. The trend (DistServe and Splitwise in research, Moonshot’s Mooncake and most large providers in production) is to run prefill and decode on separate GPU pools, shipping each prefill’s KV cache across the interconnect to a decode worker: each pool tunes hardware and batch size to its own bottleneck, and interference disappears at the cost of moving KV bytes around.

Structured output. When a model must emit valid JSON for a tool call, hope is not a strategy. Grammar- constrained decoding makes validity mechanical: compile the JSON schema (or any formal grammar) into a state machine over tokens, and at each step mask the logits — set to −∞-\infty−∞ — of every token that would violate the grammar from the current state, before sampling. The model cannot emit an invalid byte, by construction. Engines like Outlines and XGrammar precompile the token-level automaton so the per-step cost is negligible (the fiddly part is tokenization: grammar symbols and token boundaries do not align, so the automaton must track partial matches across tokens — llama.cpp’s GBNF grammars solve the same problem). One honest caveat: constraints guarantee syntax, not sense. Forcing the mask can push the model off its preferred phrasing into low-probability corners, measurably hurting content quality on some tasks — which is why the common pattern is to let the model reason freely first and constrain only the final structured answer.

Streaming is the humble feature that makes all of this feel fast: tokens are sent to the client the moment they are sampled (as server-sent events — those data: {"delta": ...} lines in every API’s wire format). A 600-token answer at 40 tokens/second takes 15 seconds either way, but with sub-second TTFT and visible progress, perceived latency is TTFT plus reading speed — one reason providers optimize prefill so aggressively.

What a token costs

Follow the money to see why all of this engineering exists. The dominant serving cost is GPU-time, so cost per token is GPU price divided by throughput, and everything in this chapter is a throughput multiplier.

✏️Worked example

Illustrative arithmetic, not a quote: rent 8×H100 at roughly $2–3 per GPU-hour (2026 market rates, variable), call it $20/hour for the node. A well-tuned vLLM- or TensorRT-LLM-class deployment of a 70B-class model with continuous batching, FP8/int4 weights, and paged KV can sustain aggregate output on the order of 5,000–10,000 tokens/second under load (heavily dependent on context lengths and batch mix). ✎Note

On-device inference closes the loop with where this chapter started: a 4-bit 8B-class model is roughly 4.5 GB — phone-sized. llama.cpp runs GGUF models on almost anything with a CPU or GPU; MLX exploits Apple-silicon unified memory (a 128 GB Mac Studio runs 70B-class models comfortably at 4-bit); and shipped systems — Apple’s on-device foundation models, Gemini Nano on Android — run quantized ~3B-class models inside the OS itself. The same four ideas carry the whole stack down from datacenter to phone: quantize the weights, cache the KV, exploit the bandwidth roofline, and only pay for compute when the batch earns it.

Where this leaves us

  • Decoding is a choice made outside the trained model. You can explain why greedy decoding loops, compute a temperature change by hand (p∝p1/Tp \propto p^{1/T}p∝p1/T), and say precisely how top-k, top-p, and min-p differ: cut by rank, by cumulative mass, or by height relative to the best token — with min-p the robust choice at high temperature.
  • Beam search maximizes likelihood, and for open-ended text likelihood is the wrong objective — good text is a typical sample, not the modal sequence.
  • Speculative decoding turns idle decode-time compute into 2–3× speedups, and its accept/reject rule provably leaves the output distribution exactly unchanged — you verified the arithmetic on a three-token example.
  • Quantization stores weights as integers plus shared scales; weights tolerate 4 bits because of redundancy, activations resist because of outlier features, and GPTQ (compensate the rounding error) and AWQ (rescale what activations say is salient) are the two intuitions behind every 4-bit file you download. Four-ish bits is the sweet spot; below that, degradation is real and lands on reasoning first.
  • Serving is scheduling: TTFT and TPOT name the two latencies, continuous batching keeps every batch slot full at every step, PagedAttention brings virtual memory to the KV cache, and prefix caching is why stable prompt fronts are cheap.
  • Cost per token is GPU price over throughput; batching, quantization, paging, and hardware compounded into a 100×+ price fall from 2022 to 2026.
