Regularization and Generalization

Why models that ace the training set can fail on everything else, the classical fixes — weight decay, dropout, early stopping — and the modern surprises: double descent, grokking, and why frontier LLMs barely overfit at all.

  3. Dropout: training a different network every step
  4. Early stopping, augmentation, and label smoothing

  7. Why frontier LLMs barely overfit — and what memorization looks like anyway

A model that scores perfectly on its training data has demonstrated exactly one thing: that it scores perfectly on its training data. The entire point of training — the only point — is to do well on data the model has never seen. That property is called generalization, and it is not automatic. A model can ace every example it was trained on and be useless on the very next one, the way a student who memorized last year’s exam answers aces last year’s exam.

Previous chapters gave you the machinery of fitting: losses measure wrongness, gradient descent reduces it, optimizers reduce it faster, and good initialization keeps the process stable. None of that machinery knows or cares about unseen data. This chapter is about the gap between fitting and generalizing — how we measure it, the classical tools for closing it (weight decay, dropout, early stopping), and then a strange twist: over the past decade, deep learning has repeatedly violated the classical theory of generalization. Networks big enough to memorize their entire training set — which century-old statistical intuition says should overfit catastrophically — often generalize better than smaller ones. Test error can get worse and then better again as models grow (“double descent”). A network can memorize its training set completely, sit at chance-level test accuracy for a hundred times as long, and then abruptly snap into perfect generalization (“grokking”).

By the end you will understand both stories — the classical one, which still governs the fine-tuning runs you will do yourself, and the modern one, which explains why a frontier pretraining run in 2026 uses almost no explicit regularization at all and mostly gets away with it.

Generalization, and how we measure it

Every serious training effort splits its data three ways. The training set is what gradient descent sees. The validation set (also called the development or “dev” set) is held out from training and used to make decisions about training: which learning rate, which model size, when to stop. The test set is touched as rarely as possible — ideally once — to produce the final honest number.

Why three and not two? Because every decision you make by peeking at a dataset leaks information from it. If you try fifty hyperparameter settings and keep the one with the best validation loss, your validation loss is now a slightly optimistic estimate — you selected for it. The test set exists to have one number nobody optimized against. (This is exactly the mechanism behind benchmark contamination in large language model (LLM) land, which we take up in evaluating language models — training data that accidentally contains the test set turns the test into a memory quiz.)

The quantity we care about is the generalization gap: the difference between performance on training data and performance on held-out data,

gap  =  Lval−Ltrain,\text{gap} \;=\; L_{\text{val}} - L_{\text{train}},gap=Lval​−Ltrain​,

where LtrainL_{\text{train}}Ltrain​ is the average loss on training examples and LvalL_{\text{val}}Lval​ the average loss on validation examples. Two failure modes bracket the healthy regime:

  • Underfitting: LtrainL_{\text{train}}Ltrain​ itself is high. The model lacks the capacity, the training time, or the right architecture to capture the pattern at all. Both losses are bad, and roughly equally bad.
  • Overfitting: LtrainL_{\text{train}}Ltrain​ is low but the gap is large. The model has captured the training examples — including their noise, their quirks, their accidental correlations — rather than the underlying regularity.
💡Intuition

Think of the training data as a scatter of points that came from some true pattern plus noise. A model with too little capacity — too few parameters, too rigid a functional form — cannot bend enough to follow the pattern: it underfits. A model with enormous capacity can bend to pass through every point, noise included: the classical expectation is that it overfits. The signal generalizes because it is shared across examples; the noise does not, because each example’s noise is its own. A model that has absorbed the noise has learned things that are true of the training set and false of the world.

Classical statistics summarizes this as the bias–variance trade-off, usually drawn as a U-shaped curve: as capacity grows, training error falls monotonically, while test error first falls (less bias — the model can express the true pattern) and then rises (more variance — the model’s fit swings wildly with the noise in whichever sample it saw). Somewhere in the middle sits a sweet spot.

✏️Worked example

Here is overfitting you can verify with a pencil. Suppose the truth is y=2xy = 2xy=2x plus a little noise, and we observe five points: (0,0.1), (1,2.2), (2,3.9), (3,6.1), (4,8.0)(0, 0.1),\ (1, 2.2),\ (2, 3.9),\ (3, 6.1),\ (4, 8.0)(0,0.1), (1,2.2), (2,3.9), (3,6.1), (4,8.0).

The quartic — the model with zero training error — misses the truth by 2.92.92.9. The humble line misses by 0.030.030.03. That tiny observation noise, amplified through the polynomial’s wild wiggles, wrecked the extrapolation. This is overfitting in miniature: perfect memory of the data, no grasp of the pattern.

The tools in the rest of this chapter — collectively called regularization — all do some version of the same thing: they restrict, penalize, or perturb the model so that, among all the functions that fit the training data, it lands on a simpler one. Simplicity here is a stand-in for “more likely to reflect the shared signal than the private noise.”

Weight decay: a preference for small weights

The oldest and still most widely used regularizer adds a penalty on the size of the weights to the loss. Plain English first: a network with large weights can produce steep, jagged functions — small changes in input causing large swings in output — which is exactly the wiggle-room needed to thread through noise. Keeping weights small biases the network toward smoother, more conservative functions. We do not forbid large weights; we charge rent for them, and let the data decide which weights are worth the price.

∑The math

L2 regularization modifies the objective. If Ldata(w)L_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{w})Ldata​(w) is the ordinary loss over the training data and w\mathbf{w}w is the vector of all the network’s weights, we minimize

Ltotal(w)  =  Ldata(w)  +  λ2 ∥w∥2  =  Ldata(w)  +  λ2∑iwi2,L_{\text{total}}(\mathbf{w}) \;=\; L_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{w}) \;+\; \frac{\lambda}{2}\,\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 \;=\; L_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{w}) \;+\; \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_i w_i^2,Ltotal​(w)=Ldata​(w)+2λ​∥w∥2=Ldata​(w)+2λ​i∑​wi2​,

where λ\lambdaλ (lambda) is the regularization strength — how much rent large weights pay — and the factor 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​ is there to make the derivative clean. Differentiating the penalty term with respect to a single weight wiw_iwi​ gives λwi\lambda w_iλwi​ (the 12\tfrac1221​ cancels the exponent’s 2), so the gradient of the total loss is

∇Ltotal  =  ∇Ldata+λw.\nabla L_{\text{total}} \;=\; \nabla L_{\text{data}} + \lambda \mathbf{w}.∇Ltotal​=∇Ldata​+λw.

Now watch what happens to the gradient-descent update with learning rate η\etaη:

w  ←  w−η(∇Ldata+λw)  =  (1−ηλ) w  −  η ∇Ldata.\mathbf{w} \;\leftarrow\; \mathbf{w} - \eta\left(\nabla L_{\text{data}} + \lambda \mathbf{w}\right) \;=\; (1 - \eta\lambda)\,\mathbf{w} \;-\; \eta\,\nabla L_{\text{data}}.w←w−η(∇Ldata​+λw)=(1−ηλ)w−η∇Ldata​.

Read the right-hand side as two moves: first shrink every weight by the factor (1−ηλ)(1 - \eta\lambda)(1−ηλ) — multiply it by something slightly less than 1 — then take the ordinary gradient step. The weights decay toward zero at a constant proportional rate unless the data gradient actively pushes back. This is why the technique is called weight decay: every step, every weight must re-justify its existence.

✏️Worked example

Take one weight w=2.0w = 2.0w=2.0, learning rate η=0.1\eta = 0.1η=0.1, decay strength λ=0.1\lambda = 0.1λ=0.1, and suppose the data gradient at this point is ∂Ldata/∂w=0.5\partial L_{\text{data}} / \partial w = 0.5∂Ldata​/∂w=0.5.

Without decay the update would have landed at 1.951.951.95; decay skimmed off 0.020.020.02, which is 1% of the weight’s value. That sounds negligible — but it compounds. Over 1,000 steps with no opposing data gradient, the weight would shrink by a factor of 0.991000≈4.3×10−50.99^{1000} \approx 4.3\times10^{-5}0.991000≈4.3×10−5: to zero. Weights that the data does not actively defend get eroded away.

There is a Bayesian reading of the same math, which connects to Chapter 6: minimizing loss plus an L2 penalty is exactly maximum a posteriori estimation with a Gaussian prior centered at zero on every weight. “Prefer small weights” is literally “believe, before seeing data, that most weights are probably near zero,” with λ\lambdaλ setting how strong that belief is.

⚠️Careful

Weight decay and L2 regularization are not the same thing once you leave plain stochastic gradient descent (SGD). The derivation above showed they coincide for vanilla gradient descent. But adaptive optimizers like Adam divide each gradient coordinate by a running estimate of its magnitude (see Chapter 11). If you implement L2 by adding λw\lambda \mathbf{w}λw into the gradient, Adam divides your penalty by the same denominator — so weights with large, noisy gradients get less regularization, precisely the weights you might most want to restrain. Loshchilov & Hutter (2019) showed this coupling hurts, and proposed decoupled weight decay: apply the shrink step w←(1−ηλ)w\mathbf{w} \leftarrow (1-\eta\lambda)\mathbf{w}w←(1−ηλ)w directly, outside the adaptive machinery. That is the “W” in AdamW, the default optimizer of the LLM era. When a modern paper says “weight decay 0.1,” it means the decoupled kind.

Dropout: training a different network every step

Weight decay penalizes; dropout perturbs. During training, every time an example passes through the network, each neuron is independently switched off — its activation set to zero — with some probability ppp. Which neurons die is re-randomized every forward pass. At test time nothing is dropped: the full network runs.

The plain-English motivation, from Srivastava et al. (2014), is about co-adaptation. In an unregularized network, neurons can form brittle conspiracies: neuron A’s output is only useful in combination with neuron B’s, each compensating for the other’s errors on the training set. Such conspiracies are exactly the kind of fragile, noise-fitted structure that fails off the training set. If any neuron might vanish at any moment, conspiracies are a bad strategy — each neuron is pushed to carry information that is useful on its own, producing redundant, robust representations.

∑The math

Let hih_ihi​ be a neuron’s activation, ppp the drop probability, and draw a mask mi∼Bernoulli(1−p)m_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(1-p)mi​∼Bernoulli(1−p) — that is, mi=1m_i = 1mi​=1 with probability 1−p1-p1−p (keep) and mi=0m_i = 0mi​=0 with probability ppp (drop). The naive version outputs h~i=mihi\tilde h_i = m_i h_ih~i​=mi​hi​.

But this creates a train/test mismatch. In expectation over masks, E[h~i]=(1−p) hi\E[\tilde h_i] = (1-p)\,h_iE[h~i​]=(1−p)hi​: downstream layers learn to expect signals scaled down by (1−p)(1-p)(1−p), and at test time — when nothing is dropped — every activation arrives “too large” by a factor of 1/(1−p)1/(1-p)1/(1−p). The modern fix, inverted dropout, rescales at training time instead:

h~i  =  mi1−p hi,soE[h~i]=(1−p) hi1−p=hi.\tilde h_i \;=\; \frac{m_i}{1-p}\, h_i, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \E[\tilde h_i] = \frac{(1-p)\,h_i}{1-p} = h_i.h~i​=1−pmi​​hi​,soE[h~i​]=1−p(1−p)hi​​=hi​.

The next pass will draw a different mask, so a different sub-network trains on the next example.

There is a second, deeper interpretation. A network with nnn droppable neurons defines 2n2^n2n possible sub-networks — every subset of neurons is one. Dropout trains a randomly chosen sub-network on each example, with all sub-networks sharing one set of weights. Running the full network at test time with matched scaling approximates averaging the predictions of this astronomically large ensemble — and averaging many diverse models is one of the most reliable generalization tricks in all of machine learning. You get an ensemble of 2n2^n2n networks for the price of one.

⚙️In practice

Dropout ruled the 2012–2019 era: AlexNet used p=0.5p = 0.5p=0.5 on its fully connected layers, and the original transformer used p=0.1p = 0.1p=0.1 throughout. But modern LLM pretraining has largely abandoned it — Llama-family models, DeepSeek-V3, and their peers pretrain with dropout set to 0. The reason previews this chapter’s final section: dropout fights overfitting, and a model making a single pass over trillions of deduplicated tokens barely overfits. Dropout in that regime throws away capacity and compute for a problem you do not have — each pass wastes a fraction of the network you paid to run. Where dropout survives is fine-tuning: small datasets, many epochs, real overfitting risk. Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) adapters (see fine-tuning) commonly use dropout of 0.05–0.1 in 2026.

Early stopping, augmentation, and label smoothing

Three more classical tools complete the kit. Each is modest; together with weight decay and dropout they were the standard defense for a decade.

Early stopping is the simplest regularizer that actually works: watch validation loss during training, and stop (or keep a checkpoint from) the point where it was lowest. The typical overfitting trajectory shows training loss falling forever while validation loss falls, flattens, and turns upward — the turn is the moment the model shifts from learning signal (shared, so it helps validation) to memorizing noise (private, so it does not). Stopping at the turn harvests the first phase and skips the second. Early stopping also has a quiet theoretical kinship with weight decay: for a linear model with quadratic loss, starting from small weights and stopping gradient descent at step ttt produces approximately the same solution as L2 regularization with strength λ∝1/(ηt)\lambda \propto 1/(\eta t)λ∝1/(ηt) — fewer steps act like a stronger pull toward the (small) initialization. Limited training time is limited capacity.

Data augmentation attacks generalization from the data side: instead of restricting the model, enlarge the training set with transformed copies that preserve the label. Flip a cat photo horizontally — still a cat; crop it, jitter its colors, still a cat. Each augmentation encodes an invariance you believe the true function has, and forces the model to respect it rather than discover it. Vision models rely on augmentation heavily to this day. For language model pretraining it plays little role — the web already supplies more natural variation than any transformation could add, and unlike a flipped cat, a word-shuffled sentence usually does not preserve its meaning. Augmentation reappears in natural language processing (NLP) fine-tuning (paraphrasing, back-translation) and, in a different guise, as synthetic data generation — a topic for pretraining data.

Label smoothing you have already met in the loss functions chapter; here it earns its regularization badge. Instead of training toward a target of probability 1 on the correct class and 0 elsewhere, train toward 1−ε1-\varepsilon1−ε on the correct class and ε/(K−1)\varepsilon/(K-1)ε/(K−1) spread over the other K−1K-1K−1 classes (typically ε=0.1\varepsilon = 0.1ε=0.1). Hard targets ask the model to drive logit gaps to infinity — which it can only do by growing weights without bound, becoming ever more confident about training examples, noise included. Soft targets cap the requested confidence, which restrains both the weights and the overconfidence. Introduced for image classifiers by Szegedy et al. (2015), it remains common in machine translation; frontier LLM pretraining mostly skips it, since a next-token distribution learned from trillions of ambiguous contexts comes out soft on its own.

⚠️Careful

Early stopping is only as honest as your validation set. If validation data leaks into training, or you evaluate so often and tune so much that you have effectively optimized against it, the “minimum” you stop at is a mirage. This failure mode returns at civilizational scale as benchmark contamination — see benchmarks and how to read them.

When the classical picture breaks

Everything so far is the textbook story circa 2016, and none of it is wrong — for the regimes it describes. But deep learning kept producing results the story says should not happen. Three of them matter enough to reshape how the field thinks about generalization.

Networks that memorize noise — and generalize anyway

In 2017, Zhang et al. ran a simple experiment. Take a standard image classifier that generalizes well on CIFAR-10. Now replace every training label with a random one — destroy all signal — and train again. The network fit the random labels to nearly 100% training accuracy. Pure memorization: there was nothing else to learn.

Sit with what that means. The network has enough capacity to memorize a completely arbitrary labeling of its training set. Classical learning theory measures a model’s tendency to overfit by exactly this kind of capacity — and by that measure, this architecture should overfit hopelessly on real labels too. It does not. The same network, same size, same optimizer, trained on true labels, generalizes fine. Explicit regularizers helped only marginally; removing them did not cause collapse. Conclusion: whatever makes deep networks generalize, it is not a lack of capacity to memorize, and it is not primarily the explicit regularizers. Something about the combination of architecture, gradient descent, and real data prefers generalizing solutions even when memorizing ones are available. Naming that something is still an open research program.

Double descent

The classical U-curve says test error eventually rises with capacity. Push capacity further than the classical picture ever contemplated and something stranger appears. Belkin et al. (2019) named the phenomenon double descent: as capacity grows, test error falls, rises to a peak right around the interpolation threshold — the model size just barely able to fit the training data exactly — and then falls again, often ending lower than the classical sweet spot ever reached. Nakkiran et al. (2019) showed the same shape in modern deep networks — ResNets, transformers — and showed it happens along three different axes: model size (model-wise), training time (epoch-wise), and, most counterintuitively, dataset size (sample-wise: there are regimes where more training data makes test error worse, because it moves the interpolation threshold on top of your model).

Why would test error come back down? The intuition the field currently leans on: at the threshold, exactly one configuration fits the data, and the model is forced into it however contorted it is — the quartic through five points from our worked example, wiggles and all. Past the threshold there are many — eventually astronomically many — configurations that all fit the training data perfectly, and the training process gets to choose among them. If the process is biased toward “smooth” or “small-norm” solutions (the next section takes up that bias), extra capacity means more room to find an interpolating solution that is also well-behaved between the data points. Overparameterization does not force overfitting; it can fund graceful fitting.

✎Note

Double descent is real, replicated, and important as evidence — but do not over-generalize it into “bigger is always better and the peak always appears.” The peak is most dramatic with label noise and modest datasets, and in many practical settings (including LLM pretraining, where data vastly exceeds what the model can memorize) you never operate near an interpolation threshold at all. The lasting lesson is negative: test error versus capacity is not a single U, so “the model has enough parameters to memorize the data” is not, by itself, an argument that it will overfit. The scaling behavior that does govern LLMs is the subject of scaling laws.

Grokking: generalization long after memorization

The third crack is the eeriest. Power et al. (2022) trained small transformers on tiny algorithmic datasets — for example, the full table of modular arithmetic like a×b mod 97a \times b \bmod 97a×bmod97, with some fraction of entries held out for validation. The training curves do something no classical picture predicts. Training accuracy hits 100% quickly — the model memorizes the table. Validation accuracy stays at chance. Under classical doctrine you would stop here, declare overfitting, and go home. But keep training the already perfectly fitted model for another 100× to 1,000× as many steps and validation accuracy abruptly leaps from chance to nearly 100%. The model grokked: long after memorizing the answers, it found the algorithm.

Two details make grokking more than a curiosity. First, weight decay is a protagonist: with little or no weight decay, grokking is delayed enormously or never arrives; with it, the delay shrinks dramatically. The picture this suggests: memorization and the general algorithm are both solutions with zero training loss, but the algorithmic solution uses smaller, more structured weights. Once training loss is pinned at zero, the data gradient goes quiet and weight decay becomes the loudest voice in the update — slowly draining the bloated memorization circuit until the leaner general circuit takes over the model’s behavior. Regularization here is not preventing overfitting; it is converting a memorizing network into a generalizing one, in place.

Second, we partially know what is learned. Nanda et al. (2023) reverse-engineered a grokked modular-arithmetic transformer and found it had learned a crisp trigonometric algorithm — representing numbers as rotations and composing them — and that hidden progress toward this circuit was building smoothly during the long “flat” phase, invisible to the accuracy metric. Generalization arrived suddenly only in the metric; internally it was gradual. This is one of mechanistic interpretability’s cleanest wins, and we return to it in circuits.

Taken together — memorization without overfitting, double descent, grokking — the message is that the classical bias–variance intuition is a special case, not a law. What fills the explanatory gap? The leading candidate is the optimizer itself.

The implicit regularization of SGD

Here is the hypothesis, stated plainly: gradient-based training is itself a regularizer. Among the many parameter settings that fit the training data, SGD does not sample uniformly — the trajectory it follows from a small-norm initialization, with noisy minibatch gradients and a finite learning rate, is systematically biased toward certain kinds of solutions. On real problems, those happen to be solutions that generalize. The network could memorize; the path rarely leads there first.

The best-known version of this story is about flat minima. Picture the loss landscape from Chapter 5: some minima sit at the bottom of wide, gently curved basins (flat), others at the bottom of narrow ravines (sharp). The generalization argument: the test loss surface is a slightly shifted copy of the training loss surface — different sample, different noise. Shift a wide basin a little and its bottom is still low: a flat minimum’s quality survives the shift. Shift a narrow ravine and you land on the ravine’s wall: sharp minima are precise bets on one sample’s noise. Flat minima encode solutions whose loss is insensitive to small parameter perturbations, and that robustness translates into robustness to resampling the data.

Why would SGD prefer flat minima? Minibatch gradients are noisy — each batch’s gradient is the true gradient plus sampling noise, with a scale that grows with the learning rate and shrinks with batch size (roughly η/B\eta/Bη/B for batch size BBB). That noise constantly kicks the parameters around. A narrow ravine cannot hold a jittering particle: the kicks bounce it off the walls and out. A wide basin can. Training noise acts like temperature, and flat minima are the states that survive it. Keskar et al. (2017) supplied early supporting evidence: very large batch sizes (less noise) found visibly sharper minima and generalized measurably worse than small-batch training on the same architecture and data.

✎Note

This is a hypothesis with evidence, not a settled theorem — treat it as the field does. Known complications: sharpness is not invariant to reparameterization (Dinh et al., 2017, “Sharp Minima Can Generalize for Deep Nets”, showed you can rescale a network’s weights to make any flat minimum look sharp without changing the function it computes — so naive sharpness cannot be the whole story); some interventions that flatten minima do not help generalization; and the large-batch gap can be closed with learning-rate adjustments. Yet the practical correlations are strong enough that people build on them — sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) explicitly seeks flat minima and often helps in vision. The honest summary for 2026: SGD’s implicit bias is real and demonstrably load-bearing (it is the best available explanation for Zhang et al.’s result), the flat-minima framing is the most useful current language for it, and a rigorous general theory does not exist yet.

💡Intuition

A compact way to hold the whole modern story: overparameterized training is a search among many perfect fits, and everything that matters happens in the tie-breaking. Capacity determines whether perfect fits exist; the optimizer’s implicit bias plus the explicit regularizers determine which one you get. Classical theory worried about the first question; deep learning’s surprises all live in the second.

Why frontier LLMs barely overfit — and what memorization looks like anyway

Now to the regime this book cares most about. Open a frontier lab’s training report and the regularization section is startlingly thin: no dropout, no augmentation, no label smoothing, a single weight-decay number. After five sections on the overfitting menace, why does a trillion-parameter model — the most overparameterized object ever engineered — get away with almost nothing?

Because the pretraining regime removed overfitting’s oxygen. Overfitting feeds on revisiting: gradient descent squeezing the same examples again and again until their private noise is absorbed. A classical vision model might see each of its million images hundreds of times. A frontier LLM makes roughly one pass — a single epoch, sometimes less — over a deduplicated corpus of 101310^{13}1013 tokens or more (Chinchilla-style ratios of ~20+ tokens per parameter; see scaling laws). Every batch is new data. In that regime the training loss is a validation loss: nearly every gradient step is computed on tokens the model has never seen, so the thing being minimized is already generalization to fresh text. The train/validation gap at the end of a well-run pretraining job is tiny, and the held-out loss curve looks like the training loss curve with the noise averaged out.

The load-bearing phrase is deduplicated. Raw web scrapes are wildly repetitive — boilerplate, mirrored pages, quotes of quotes — and every duplicate is a disguised second epoch on that passage. Lee et al. (2021) showed that deduplicating training data both improves language models and causes them to emit memorized training text roughly an order of magnitude less often (their headline figure; approximate). Deduplication is where the anti-overfitting budget of a modern pretraining pipeline actually goes — it is data engineering doing the job dropout used to do. The pipelines are covered in pretraining data.

But — and this is the honest part — near-zero overfitting does not mean zero memorization. LLMs verifiably memorize and can regurgitate verbatim training text: famous quotations, code licenses, Bible verses, and sometimes content whose reproduction is unwelcome — personal information, copyrighted passages. Carlini et al. (2022) quantified the pattern: memorization increases with model scale (bigger models memorize more), with duplication count (text repeated in the corpus is far more extractable — the dedup connection again), and with prompt length (longer verbatim prefixes elicit longer verbatim continuations). Note the first of those: aggregate loss curves show no overfitting while per-sequence memorization grows with scale. Both are true at once because memorizing a few thousand repeated or distinctive sequences is invisible in an average over 101310^{13}1013 tokens — the aggregate metric and the per-example behavior simply measure different things. Deduplication mitigates; it does not eliminate. Verbatim recall of well-known text is also not purely a bug — you want a model to know famous quotations exactly — which is why “memorization” in LLMs is a policy-laden measurement question, not a switch to flip.

⚠️Careful

Do not conflate “LLMs don’t overfit” with “LLMs don’t memorize.” The first is a statement about aggregate loss on held-out data, and it is approximately true in one-epoch pretraining. The second would be a statement about individual sequences, and it is false — extraction attacks recover real training text from real deployed models. When you see either claim, check which of the two quantities is actually being measured.

⚙️In practice

What regularization a 2026-era pretraining run actually uses (patterns visible across Llama-family, DeepSeek, and similar technical reports; details vary and move fast):

  • Weight decay ≈0.1\approx 0.1≈0.1 via AdamW — the workhorse, typically applied to weight matrices but not to biases, normalization gains, or (often) embeddings. Notably, at LLM scale its main observed value is as much about optimization dynamics and stability as about classical overfitting.
  • Dropout 0. Attention dropout, residual dropout, multi-layer perceptron (MLP) dropout: all off.
  • Z-loss ≈10−4\approx 10^{-4}≈10−4 in some lineages (introduced at scale in PaLM): an auxiliary penalty λlog⁡2Z\lambda \log^2 Zλlog2Z on the softmax normalizer ZZZ of the output layer, which stops logits from drifting to large magnitudes — mostly a numerical-stability device in low-precision training, with a mild regularizing side effect.
  • Deduplication and data curation as the real anti-memorization spend.
  • Early stopping: not used as a regularizer. Runs train to a planned token budget; checkpoints guard against crashes and loss spikes (see the training run), not against overfitting.

Overfitting is alive and well one step downstream, in fine-tuning: thousands of examples instead of trillions, several epochs instead of one. That is where the classical toolkit returns — early stopping on validation loss (often after 1–3 epochs), dropout on LoRA adapters, and capacity control via LoRA rank — covered in fine-tuning you can actually do. If you personally ever fight overfitting with your own hands, it will be there.

Where this leaves us

  • Generalization is the actual goal of training; train/validation/test splits exist to measure it honestly, and every decision made while peeking at a split leaks optimism into it.
  • The classical picture — capacity trades bias against variance along a U-shaped test-error curve — explains underfitting and overfitting in small-data, multi-epoch regimes, and you verified it with a quartic that hit every training point and missed the future by miles.
  • Weight decay multiplies weights by (1−ηλ)(1-\eta\lambda)(1−ηλ) each step — rent charged on every parameter — and must be decoupled from adaptive gradient scaling (AdamW) to work as intended.
  • Dropout trains a random sub-network each pass with survivors scaled by 1/(1−p)1/(1-p)1/(1−p), approximating a 2n2^n2n-model ensemble at test time; it has left pretraining but lives on in fine-tuning, alongside early stopping, augmentation, and label smoothing.
  • Zhang et al. showed deep networks can memorize pure noise yet generalize on real data; double descent shows test error can fall again past the interpolation threshold; grokking shows generalization can arrive long after memorization, with weight decay driving the transition. Classical bias–variance intuition is a special case, not a law.
  • The leading (unproven, evidence-backed) explanation is the implicit regularization of SGD: noisy gradient descent from small initializations is biased toward flat, low-norm solutions that survive the resampling of data.
  • Frontier LLMs barely overfit because one epoch over deduplicated trillions of tokens makes nearly every gradient step a test on unseen text — yet memorization still happens, growing with scale, duplication, and prompt length, with deduplication as the practical mitigation.
  • A 2026 pretraining run’s whole explicit-regularization budget is roughly: weight decay 0.1, dropout 0, maybe a z-loss — while fine-tuning still needs the full classical toolkit.

The quiet hero of stable large-scale training has appeared twice now without a proper introduction: the normalization layers that keep activations well-behaved. They get the next chapter: Normalization: BatchNorm to RMSNorm.
