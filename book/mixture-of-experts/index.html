Mixture-of-Experts

How routers and sparse expert layers let a model store hundreds of billions of parameters while paying for only a fraction per token — and why nearly every frontier model now works this way.

Every chapter so far has treated a transformer as a dense object: every token, at every layer, flows through every parameter. That coupling has a brutal consequence. If you want a model that knows ten times more — more facts, more languages, more rare idioms, more chemistry — you make it ten times bigger, and then every single token you ever train on or serve costs roughly ten times the arithmetic. Knowledge and compute are chained together, and the chain is what makes frontier-scale models so expensive.

But think about what a feed-forward layer is actually doing on any one token. The token “le” in a French sentence does not need the parameters that store Python idioms. A stretch of arithmetic does not need the parameters encoding Shakespearean meter. A dense model pays for all of it anyway, on every token, because it has no mechanism for not using a parameter. Most of the work is, in a very real sense, wasted motion.

Mixture-of-experts (MoE) is the architecture that breaks the chain. Replace each feed-forward network with a whole bench of them — the experts — plus a tiny learned dispatcher — the router — that sends each token to only a few. Now the model’s knowledge scales with the total parameter count, while the cost of each token scales with the much smaller active parameter count. Mixtral 8x7B stores 47 billion parameters but touches about 13 billion per token. DeepSeek-V3 stores 671 billion and touches 37 billion — a model with the memory of a giant and the metabolism of a mid-size. This is why, as of 2026, nearly every frontier model — open or closed — is sparse.

This chapter builds the whole machine: the router math with numbers you can check by hand, the load-balancing problem that dominates MoE training (and three competing solutions), the fine-grained-plus-shared design that modern models converged on, two detailed case studies, and the systems bill that sparsity quietly runs up. By the end you will be able to read a line like “235B-A22B, 128 experts, top-8” and know exactly what it means — and what it cost.

The bargain: knowledge without the compute bill

Start with the economics, because everything else in this chapter is downstream of one accounting identity.

Recall from the transformer chapter that a transformer block has two sub-layers: attention, and a feed-forward network (FFN) — the two-layer multi-layer perceptron (MLP) that expands each token’s vector to roughly 4×4\times4× its width and projects back down. In a large dense model, those FFNs hold roughly two-thirds of all parameters, and every token multiplies through all of them. The FFN is where the model’s factual and procedural knowledge mostly lives — and it is also where most of the FLOPs (floating-point operations) go.

MoE splits the ledger into two columns:

  • Total parameters — everything stored in memory. This is the model’s capacity: how much it can know. More total parameters means more distinct facts, patterns, and skills can be memorized without interfering with each other (a theme you will meet again in superposition: parameters compete for storage).
  • Active parameters — the subset actually multiplied against any one token. This is the model’s per-token cost: training FLOPs, inference FLOPs, latency.

A dense model has one column: total = active. An MoE model decouples them. The bargain, stated plainly: you pay memory for knowledge and FLOPs only for the slice of knowledge each token needs.

💡Intuition

A dense model is one clinician who has memorized all of medicine and re-reads their entire memory for every patient. An MoE model is a hospital: a triage desk (the router) glances at each patient and sends them to two or three relevant specialists (the experts). The hospital as a whole knows vastly more than any one clinician, but each patient only occupies a couple of doctors. The building is expensive to maintain — you must keep every specialist on staff and on site — but each visit is cheap.

The numbers make the case concrete. Mixtral 8x7B: 46.7B total, 12.9B active, and it matched or beat the dense Llama 2 70B on most benchmarks while running inference roughly 5×5\times5× cheaper in FLOPs. DeepSeek-V3: 671B total, 37B active — its per-token compute is comparable to a ~37B dense model, yet it competed with the strongest closed models of its moment. In scaling-law terms: at fixed training compute, sparse models reach lower loss than dense ones, because the extra parameters buy capacity nearly free of FLOPs. The exchange rate is not infinite — experts overlap, routing is imperfect, so a 671B/37B MoE is not worth a dense 671B — but a common rough heuristic is that an MoE behaves like a dense model of size around total×active\sqrt{\text{total} \times \text{active}}total×active​ (for DeepSeek-V3, roughly a dense 160B). Treat that as folklore-grade, not law; it varies with design.

There is no free lunch, only a restructured bill: you still need the memory to hold all 671B parameters, and, as we will see, you take on a training-stability problem and a serious networking problem. The rest of the chapter is about that fine print.

The mechanics: a router and a bench of experts

Structurally, the change is small. Take a transformer block and leave the attention sub-layer alone — attention still runs densely over every token, exactly as in Chapter 23. Replace only the FFN with:

  1. EEE expert FFNs — each one an ordinary two-layer MLP of the kind you already know, typically all the same shape, each with its own weights.
  2. A router (also called the gating network) — a single learned matrix that scores, for each token, how much each expert should be used.
  3. A top-kkk rule — keep only the kkk highest-scoring experts per token (kkk is usually 1, 2, or 8), run the token through those, and combine their outputs, weighted by the router’s (renormalized) scores.

Everything else — residual stream, normalization, positional handling — is unchanged. The sparsity lives entirely inside this one sub-layer, repeated at every (or almost every) block.

The load-bearing idea is always: score, keep few, weight, sum.

One more thing deserves emphasis because it surprises people: routing is per-token and per-layer. The same word can go to expert 7 at layer 4 and expert 91 at layer 5; two adjacent tokens in one sentence usually take different paths. A “path” through a 61-layer, top-8 model is one of an astronomically large number of expert combinations. The router itself is trained by ordinary backpropagation: gradients flow to WgW_gWg​ through the gate weights gig_igi​ (the top-kkk selection is not differentiable, but the weights of the selected experts are, and that signal is enough to teach the router which experts help).

What experts actually specialize in

The word “expert” invites a picture: one expert for law, one for medicine, one for Python. It is worth killing that picture early, because the empirical reality is stranger and more interesting.

When the Mixtral authors looked at which experts fired on which data (Section 5 of the paper), they found almost no topic-level specialization: routing distributions on ArXiv papers, biology text, and philosophy looked broadly similar. What they found instead was syntactic and positional structure: certain experts disproportionately caught indentation tokens in code, certain experts caught punctuation, and consecutive tokens were assigned the same expert far more often than chance. The router behaves less like a librarian sorting books by subject and more like a mail room sorting by envelope shape.

Later, finer-grained models muddy this in the other direction. DeepSeekMoE-style models with hundreds of small experts do show measurable domain skew — some experts light up far more on code, or on Chinese text, or on numbers — and routed-expert distributions shift visibly between, say, math benchmarks and prose. Interpretability work has found individual experts whose ablation selectively damages narrow skills. But even there, most experts resist any one-line description, many are polysemantic in the same way individual neurons are, and the shared layers below carry much of the load.

✎Note

The honest summary as of 2026: experts specialize enough for the router to find exploitable structure — mostly token-level and shallow-syntactic, with some genuine domain signal in fine-grained models — but “expert” is a capacity-partitioning mechanism, not an interpretable ontology. Nobody designs which expert learns what; the division of labor is an emergent, messy equilibrium of the training dynamics described next. Treat any diagram labeling experts “math expert” and “French expert” as a cartoon.

This opacity connects to interpretability later in the book. Sparse routing means different inputs literally execute different sub-networks, which complicates any analysis that assumes one shared computational graph.

Load balancing: the problem that defines MoE training

If MoE is architecturally a small change, why did it take from 2017 to 2023 to conquer the frontier? Because of a training pathology that every MoE system must solve, and that dense models simply do not have.

The router and the experts are trained together, and that coupling has an unstable feedback mode. Suppose, purely by the luck of initialization, expert 4 is marginally better than its peers on common tokens. The router learns to send it slightly more traffic. More traffic means more gradient updates, so expert 4 improves faster than the starved experts — which makes the router prefer it even more. Iterate, and the system collapses: a handful of experts serve almost all tokens, and the rest are dead weight, parameters you are storing but that learned nothing. Your 671B model quietly becomes a 40B model with 630B of expensive padding. This is the rich-get-richer (or “routing collapse”) dynamic, identified already in Shazeer et al., 2017.

Balance also matters for hardware, not only for learning: experts are physically spread across GPUs (graphics processing units; more on this below), so an overloaded expert means one GPU grinding while the rest idle. Three families of solutions compete.

The auxiliary balance loss

The classic fix, standardized by the Switch Transformer, is to add a second loss term that punishes imbalance, so the router is pulled toward uniform usage even as the main cross-entropy loss pulls it toward whatever routing predicts tokens best.

⚠️Careful

The auxiliary loss embodies a genuine tension, not a solved problem. The main objective wants the router to send each token wherever loss drops most — which may legitimately be unbalanced, because language itself is unbalanced (there are more commas than chemistry terms). The balance loss wants uniformity for its own sake. Every gradient step is a tug-of-war between them, tuned by α\alphaα: too small and you risk collapse; too large and the router starts making worse routing decisions to satisfy the accountant, measurably hurting the model. The DeepSeek team’s ablations found this interference significant enough to motivate abandoning the loss altogether — which is the next idea.

Auxiliary-loss-free balancing: a bias, not a loss

DeepSeek’s alternative (Wang et al., 2024, used at scale in DeepSeek-V3) removes the extra gradient entirely and replaces it with something closer to a thermostat. Give each expert a scalar bias bib_ibi​, and select the top-kkk using biased scores si+bis_i + b_isi​+bi​ — but still compute the gate weights gig_igi​ from the raw sis_isi​, so the bias influences only who is chosen, never how much a chosen expert’s output counts. Then, after each training step, apply a feedback rule with no gradients anywhere in it:

  • expert overloaded this step (received more than its fair share kT/EkT/EkT/E of tokens) →\to→ decrease bib_ibi​ by a fixed step γ\gammaγ;
  • expert underloaded →\to→ increase bib_ibi​ by γ\gammaγ.

Here γ\gammaγ is a small “bias update speed” (DeepSeek-V3 used γ=0.001\gamma = 0.001γ=0.001). A chronically ignored expert accumulates a positive bias until it starts winning top-kkk slots on the tokens where it was nearly competitive; a hog accumulates a handicap. The system self-regulates around balance, while the language-modeling gradient — the only gradient the router ever sees — stays pure. DeepSeek’s ablations show this both balances better and yields lower loss than the auxiliary-loss recipe at matched scale. (V3 keeps a vestigial sequence-level balance loss at a tiny coefficient as a guard against pathological single-sequence imbalance, but the bias controller does the real work.)

💡Intuition

The auxiliary loss disciplines the router by changing what it wants — distorting its learned preferences. The bias method changes what it can have — a queue-management layer on top of unchanged preferences, like a restaurant host steering parties away from the popular waiter’s section without ever telling diners the food there is worse. Preferences stay honest; allocation stays fair.

Expert-choice routing: let experts pick tokens

The third family, expert choice (Zhou et al., 2022), dissolves the problem instead of fighting it. Flip the direction of selection: instead of each token choosing its top-kkk experts, each expert chooses its top-ccc tokens from the batch, where the capacity c=kT/Ec = kT/Ec=kT/E is fixed. Balance is now perfect by construction — every expert processes exactly ccc tokens, every step, and no balancing machinery is needed at all. As a bonus, the model gains adaptive compute: an easy token might be picked by no expert (it rides the residual stream through unchanged), while a hard, contested token might be picked by many.

The catch is causality, and for language models it bites. Which tokens an expert picks depends on all the other tokens competing in the batch — expert 7 takes your token only if it outscores its neighbors. During autoregressive decoding, the competing “batch” at training time included future tokens of the same sequence, which do not exist yet at inference; and even across sequences, one user’s routing would depend on who else is in the serving batch. Expert choice therefore works cleanly for encoders and non-causal settings but needs awkward workarounds for decoder-only LLMs — which is why the frontier’s autoregressive flagships stayed with token-choice routing plus one of the two balancing mechanisms above.

Fine-grained and shared experts

Through 2023, the standard recipe was a handful of large experts — Mixtral’s 8, each the size of a full 7B-class FFN, top-2. DeepSeekMoE (Dai et al., 2024) argued this is the wrong shape, on two grounds, and its redesign is now the near-universal template.

Fine-grained experts. With 8 experts and top-2, there are only (82)=28\binom{8}{2} = 28(28​)=28 possible expert combinations per layer, and each expert is so large that it must jumble many unrelated skills inside itself — the router is choosing between a few big grab-bags. Split each expert into mmm smaller ones (keeping total parameters fixed) and select mkmkmk of them, and the per-token FLOPs stay identical while the combinatorics explode: split each of the 8 into four and you get 32 experts with top-8, which offers (328)≈10.5\binom{32}{8} \approx 10.5(832​)≈10.5 million combinations. Knowledge can be decomposed into finer units, and the router can compose a bespoke committee per token instead of settling for the least-bad pair of generalists. DeepSeek’s ablations show accuracy climbing as granularity rises at fixed compute (with diminishing returns and rising routing/communication overhead — you cannot shrink experts forever).

Shared experts. Watch a conventional MoE train and you find every expert partially re-learning the same universal boilerplate — basic syntax, common words, generic composition — because every expert must handle whatever tokens land on it. That is redundant storage. DeepSeekMoE carves out one or a few shared experts that every token passes through unconditionally, no router involved, alongside its routed picks. The shared experts soak up the common patterns; the routed experts, relieved of boilerplate duty, are free to specialize harder. The layer output becomes

y  =  ∑s=1SFFNsshared(x)  +  ∑i∈TopKgi FFNi(x),y \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^{S} \mathrm{FFN}^{\text{shared}}_s(x) \;+\; \sum_{i \in \mathrm{TopK}} g_i\,\mathrm{FFN}_i(x),y=s=1∑S​FFNsshared​(x)+i∈TopK∑​gi​FFNi​(x),

with SSS small (DeepSeek-V3 uses S=1S = 1S=1). You can read the shared expert as a small dense FFN living inside the MoE layer — a guaranteed common pathway, with sparsity layered on top for everything rare.

Case studies: Mixtral and DeepSeek-V3

Two models, a year apart, bookend MoE’s conquest of the open frontier.

Mixtral 8x7B (December 2023). Mistral took their dense 7B architecture and made one change: every FFN became 8 experts with top-2 routing and softmax gating over the selected pair (the paper is notably silent on training details such as the balancing recipe). Because attention and embeddings are shared, the total is 46.7B — not 8×7=568 \times 7 = 568×7=56B — and the active count is 12.9B per token. The result matched or exceeded the dense Llama 2 70B and GPT-3.5 across most benchmarks while needing roughly a fifth of the per-token FLOPs, and, released with open weights, it was the moment MoE stopped being a Google-internal curiosity and became the obvious move. “8x7B”-style names stuck as shorthand, later giving way to the clearer “total-A-active” convention (e.g. “235B-A22B”).

DeepSeek-V3 (December 2024). The full modern recipe in one system, and worth reading closely because nearly every 2025–2026 MoE reads as a variation on it:

  • Shape: 61 transformer layers; every FFN except the first three layers is an MoE layer with 256 fine-grained routed experts + 1 shared expert, routing each token to top-8 routed experts. Total: 671B parameters; active per token: ~37B.
  • Gating: per-expert sigmoid affinity scores rather than a softmax, normalized over the selected 8 — a small change that decouples experts’ scores from each other and, with hundreds of experts, avoids softmax’s vanishing per-expert probabilities.
  • Balancing: the auxiliary-loss-free bias method above (γ=0.001\gamma = 0.001γ=0.001), with only a vestigial sequence-wise balance term. No token dropping: earlier MoEs enforced hard per-expert capacity limits and simply dropped tokens that overflowed (the token skips the FFN that layer, riding the residual stream); V3’s balancing worked well enough to turn capacity limits off.
  • Multi-token prediction (MTP): an auxiliary training objective where a small extra module predicts the second next token as well as the next one — densifying the training signal and, at inference, doubling as a draft head for speculative decoding. Not an MoE technique per se, but part of why V3’s numbers land where they do.
  • The bill: trained on 14.8 trillion tokens, consuming a reported ~2.79M H800 GPU-hours — about $5.6M at their assumed $2/GPU-hour rental rate. Treat those figures as approximate and self-reported, and note they cover the final run only, not the research, ablations, and failed runs that preceded it. Even so, against the hundred-million-dollar-class budgets assumed for dense frontier training at the time, it was a shock — and MoE sparsity is a large part of the explanation, alongside 8-bit floating-point (FP8) training and the multi-head latent attention (MLA) compression you met in the key-value (KV) cache chapter.
⚙️In practice

The V3 template — a few dense layers first (routing on raw embeddings is noisy), then MoE layers with hundreds of fine-grained experts, roughly top-8, one shared expert, bias-based balancing, sigmoid or softmax gating — describes most serious open MoE models of 2025–2026: DeepSeek’s own V3.x/R1 line and Kimi K2, and — minus the shared expert, which not everyone kept — Qwen3’s MoE variants and OpenAI’s gpt-oss pair. When you read a model card, the numbers to extract are: total params, active params, expert count and size, kkk, shared experts, and the balancing method. Those six numbers are most of the architecture.

The systems bill: communication, memory, fragility

On paper, MoE looks like free lunch — same FLOPs, more knowledge. The costs are real; they have merely moved out of the FLOP column and into three others.

All-to-all communication. A 671B-parameter model does not fit on one GPU, so the experts are sharded: GPU 0 holds experts 0–15, GPU 1 holds 16–31, and so on — this is expert parallelism, one more axis alongside the data and tensor parallelism of the compute chapter. Now follow one token. Its hidden vector lives on whichever GPU is processing its sequence, but the router might select experts living on eight different GPUs. So at every MoE layer, every GPU must package up its tokens’ vectors and physically ship each one over the network to the GPUs hosting its chosen experts, and then — after the expert FFNs run — ship every result back to where it came from. Two all-to-all exchanges (a “dispatch” and a “combine”) per MoE layer, per step: everyone sends to everyone, simultaneously, over a hundred such exchanges per forward pass in a model like V3. In dense training, the network mostly moves gradients once per step; in MoE training, the network is on the critical path of every layer, and it — not arithmetic — is frequently the bottleneck. This is why MoE reports read like networking papers: DeepSeek-V3 restricts each token’s 8 experts to at most 4 server nodes (node-limited routing) to keep traffic off the slow inter-node links, overlaps communication with computation via custom pipeline schedules, and dedicated a chunk of its GPU cores purely to moving bytes.

Memory. Only 37B parameters compute per token, but all 671B must sit in GPU memory, because the next token may summon any of them. Rule of thumb: MoE buys you FLOPs, never video RAM (VRAM). This reshapes serving economics: a big MoE is awkward for single-GPU or low-traffic deployment (you hold 18× the weights you use), but excellent for high-throughput serving — with thousands of concurrent tokens per batch, every expert gets steady work, and the fleet amortizes the memory. At small batch sizes there is a partial consolation: token-by-token decoding is memory-bandwidth-bound (as the KV-cache chapter showed), and per token an MoE reads only the selected experts’ weights, so decoding speed tracks active, not total, size.

Fine-tuning fragility. The router is a discrete, delicately balanced switchboard tuned by billions of tokens. Fine-tune on a small, narrow dataset and the router sees a skewed token distribution with noisy gradients; routing patterns can drift or collapse, and because selection is discrete, small router changes cause discontinuous behavior jumps. Early MoE work found fine-tuned models overfit more readily than dense equivalents, and practitioners learned tricks: freeze the router, keep an auxiliary loss on during fine-tuning, use larger batches, or apply parameter-efficient methods that leave routing alone. Modern recipes have largely tamed this, but “MoE fine-tuning needs more care than dense” remains true in 2026.

⚠️Careful

A related trap for readers of benchmarks and price sheets: active parameter count is a good proxy for FLOPs, not for capability. A 671B/37B MoE is far stronger than a dense 37B (all that extra knowledge is real) but weaker than a dense 671B would be. Comparing models by active params flatters MoE; comparing by total params flatters dense. Honest comparisons state both — that is exactly why the “-A-” naming convention exists.

Reading the frontier

Where does this leave the field in 2026? Sparse, overwhelmingly — with instructive exceptions.

The open flagships are MoE. The roster as of mid-2026, spanning the frontier chapter’s main open families: DeepSeek-V3/V3.1 and the R1 reasoning line built on them (671B/37B); Qwen3-235B-A22B (128 experts, top-8) and its larger successors; Meta’s Llama 4 — the family’s first MoE generation — with Maverick at ~400B total/17B active; Moonshot’s Kimi K2 at roughly 1T total / 32B active, the open frontier’s first trillion-parameter flagship; and OpenAI’s gpt-oss-120b at ~117B total with only ~5.1B active — an aggressive sparsity ratio chosen so the model serves on a single 80GB GPU. Sparsity ratios (total ÷ active) have drifted steadily upward, from Mixtral’s ~3.6× to 20–30× and beyond, as balancing methods and interconnects improved.

The closed flagships are (almost certainly) MoE too. Closed labs rarely publish architecture, so the evidence is inferential but strong. Google states outright that Gemini 1.5 (and successors) use sparse MoE, citing its own MoE lineage (GShard, Switch). Widely-reported analyses of GPT-4 described a ~16-expert MoE — unconfirmed by OpenAI, but consistent with its serving economics, and OpenAI’s own open-weight releases are MoE. The strongest public evidence is pricing and latency: closed flagship models are served at prices and speeds that dense models of plausibly frontier capability would struggle to hit, given known hardware; and each generation’s price-per-token keeps falling faster than hardware alone explains. Anthropic publishes nothing either way about Claude’s architecture. The safe 2026 reading: assume any frontier-scale model is sparse unless the lab says otherwise — while remembering this is inference, not documentation.

Dense persists where it makes sense. MoE won the flagship tier, not the whole field. Small open models (roughly ≤30B: Llama 3.1 8B, Qwen3’s dense tier, Gemma, Mistral’s small line) stay dense — below a certain scale the routing overhead, balancing risk, and memory multiplier are not worth it, and a phone or a single consumer GPU wants the smallest total footprint, which is by definition dense. Dense models also remain simpler to train reliably, to fine-tune, to quantize, to distill into, and to study — interpretability work overwhelmingly uses dense models, since a shared computational graph across inputs is a methodological gift. Dense is no longer how you build the biggest model; it is how you build the simplest one that fits the deployment.

🕰History

MoE is old. Jacobs, Jordan, Nowlan, and Hinton proposed “adaptive mixtures of local experts” in 1991 — separate networks with a learned gate, softly specializing on sub-tasks. The modern era began when Shazeer et al. (2017) made the gate sparse (noisy top-k over up to thousands of expert FFNs inside long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, 137B parameters) and named the balancing problem. Google scaled it into transformers with GShard (2020, machine translation, 600B) and the Switch Transformer (2021, top-1 routing, first trillion-parameter model), but these were research systems. GPT-4 (2023) was widely believed to be MoE but said nothing; Mixtral (Dec 2023) put open weights behind the idea; DeepSeekMoE (Jan 2024) supplied the fine-grained + shared design; and DeepSeek-V3 (Dec 2024) assembled the whole modern stack at frontier scale on a startling budget. By 2026, sparse is the default and dense the deliberate exception — a 35-year arc from workshop idea to industry standard.

Where this leaves us

  • The bargain: MoE decouples knowledge from compute. Total parameters set what the model can store; active parameters set what each token costs. Mixtral: 47B/13B. DeepSeek-V3: 671B/37B. You pay the difference in memory, networking, and training care — not FLOPs.
  • The mechanics fit in one line: s=x⊤Wgs = x\T W_gs=x⊤Wg​, keep the top-kkk scores, softmax them into gates gig_igi​, output ∑igi FFNi(x)\sum_i g_i\,\mathrm{FFN}_i(x)∑i​gi​FFNi​(x) — per token, per layer, with the top-2-of-4 arithmetic above as your mental template.
  • Experts are capacity partitions, not disciplines: routing tracks token-level and syntactic structure more than topics, with modest domain skew in fine-grained models and plenty of residual opacity.
  • Load balancing is the central training problem.   • Modern shape: hundreds of fine-grained experts for combinatorial specialization, plus a shared always-on expert for common patterns — DeepSeekMoE’s template, now near-universal.
  • The systems bill: two all-to-all exchanges per MoE layer make the network the bottleneck; all experts must sit in VRAM even when idle; and routers make fine-tuning touchier than dense.
  • Reading the frontier: open flagships are MoE (DeepSeek, Qwen3-MoE, Llama 4, Kimi K2, gpt-oss), closed ones almost certainly are, and dense survives where simplicity, small total size, or latency dominate.

Sparse experts are one answer to the cost of scale — skip most of the FFN for every token.
