Evaluating Language Models

Perplexity derived and stress-tested, bits-per-byte, contamination mechanics, why the same model scores differently on the same benchmark, and when to trust humans and LLM judges.

In this chapter

  4. Downstream evaluation: prompting, choices, and generation
  5. Judges: humans and models
  6. Capability, propensity, and the lab’s dashboard
  7. Where this leaves us

You have a language model. Maybe you followed the last three chapters and trained a small one; maybe you downloaded one. Its training loss went down, which tells you it got better at something. The question this chapter answers sounds trivial and is not: how good is it?

There are two honest families of answers. The intrinsic answer measures how well the model does the one thing it was literally trained to do — assign high probability to real text it has never seen. That road leads to perplexity, a single number with a clean information-theoretic meaning and several famous ways of lying to you. The extrinsic answer measures whether the model can do things you care about — answer questions, write code, summarize a contract — which sounds more relevant and turns out to be harder to measure honestly, because now you must decide how to ask, how to score free-form text, and how to stop the test questions from leaking into the training data.

Evaluation is where language modeling stops being clean math and becomes experimental science, with all of experimental science’s classic failure modes: contaminated samples, instruments that disagree with each other, and metrics that stop meaning anything once people start optimizing against them. Every scandal of the leaderboard era — models that “beat GPT-4” and then embarrassed themselves in public, benchmark scores that collapsed when someone wrote fresh test questions — is a failure of the methodology in this chapter, not of the models.

✎Note

“Is this missing a lot?” If you are wondering where MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding), GPQA, SWE-bench, and the rest of the 2026 benchmark alphabet went — deliberately elsewhere. This book splits evaluation in two. This chapter is the fundamentals: the measuring instruments themselves and their failure modes, which have barely changed since 2020 and will still be true in 2030. Chapter 46 is the field guide to the benchmarks people cite in 2026 and how to read a model card without being fooled. The instruments first, the instrument readings later.

Perplexity, built properly

In Chapter 7 we built the chain: surprise →\to→ entropy →\to→ cross-entropy →\to→ perplexity. Let us now assemble it into the exact number that gets computed on real corpora, because the details — per-token averaging, log bases, what “held-out” means — are where all the traps hide.

A language model qqq assigns a probability to every next token given the tokens so far. By the chain rule of probability (from Chapter 6), that means it assigns a probability to any whole sequence: multiply the step-by-step conditionals together. Evaluating the model on a held-out corpus asks: how much probability did you put on the text that actually occurred?

∑The math

Take a held-out corpus of NNN tokens x1,x2,…,xNx_1, x_2, \dots, x_Nx1​,x2​,…,xN​ (one long sequence, or many documents concatenated). The model’s average cross-entropy per token is

H  =  −1N∑t=1Nlog⁡q ⁣(xt∣x1,…,xt−1),H \;=\; -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \log q\!\left(x_t \mid x_{1}, \dots, x_{t-1}\right),H=−N1​t=1∑N​logq(xt​∣x1​,…,xt−1​),

where q(xt∣x1,…,xt−1)q(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1})q(xt​∣x1​,…,xt−1​) is the probability the model assigned to the token that actually came next, given the true preceding text. Note that at every step the model is conditioned on the real history, not its own guesses — this is called teacher forcing, and it matters later. If the log⁡\loglog is natural, HHH is in nats; if base 2, bits. This is exactly the training loss from Chapter 9, computed on text the model never trained on.

Perplexity exponentiates it back out of log-space:

PPL  =  eH    (nats)  =  2H    (bits).\text{PPL} \;=\; e^{H} \;\;(\text{nats}) \;=\; 2^{H} \;\;(\text{bits}).PPL=eH(nats)=2H(bits).

One more useful reading: substitute the definition of HHH and use esum=producte^{\text{sum}} = \text{product}esum=product:

PPL  =  (∏t=1Nq(xt∣x1..t−1))−1/N.\text{PPL} \;=\; \left( \prod_{t=1}^{N} q(x_t \mid x_{1..t-1}) \right)^{-1/N}.PPL=(t=1∏N​q(xt​∣x1..t−1​))−1/N.

Perplexity is the inverse geometric mean of the probabilities the model gave to the true tokens. A perplexity of 4 means the model’s typical probability on the correct next token was 1/41/41/4.

💡Intuition

Perplexity is the effective branching factor: a model with perplexity kkk on some text is exactly as uncertain, on average, as if it were choosing uniformly among kkk equally likely next tokens. A model that knows nothing about English and guesses uniformly over a 50,000-token vocabulary has perplexity 50,000. A strong 2026 model on ordinary web prose sits in the low single digits — as if every next token were a coin flip among roughly 2 to 8 live options — a compression from 50,000 down to single digits, and also the ceiling doing the work: text has irreducible entropy (names, numbers, genuine choices), so perplexity 1 is impossible on real language.

🕰History

The word and the metric come from speech recognition, not from the deep learning era. Fred Jelinek’s group at IBM introduced perplexity in the late 1970s as a way to compare language models feeding their recognizers, precisely because it could be computed without running the whole expensive recognition pipeline. The shape of the argument — “we have a cheap intrinsic proxy and an expensive downstream task, and we hope they correlate” — is fifty years old, and so is the catch: the correlation holds within a model family and setup, and gets shaky across them. Everything in the next section is the modern version of that catch.

What perplexity is good for — and how it lies

Used correctly, perplexity is the cleanest instrument in the toolbox. It uses every token of the eval set (thousands of bits of signal per document, versus one bit for a right/wrong answer), it is smooth (small model improvements move it a little; benchmark accuracies jump in steps), and it needs no prompt engineering, no answer parsing, no judge. That is why the entire scaling-law literature (Chapter 32) is built on held-out loss — perplexity’s logarithm — and why, during a training run, loss curves are watched more closely than any benchmark.

The correct use is narrow: comparing runs that share the same held-out data and the same tokenizer. Checkpoint 40,000 versus checkpoint 80,000 of the same run. An architecture ablation where only the attention variant changed. Two learning-rate schedules on identical data. Within that fence, lower perplexity almost always means a better model. Outside the fence, three separate traps are waiting.

Trap one: the tokenizer changes the number

Perplexity is per token — and the model’s tokenizer decides what a token is. Two models can compress the same text equally well, in the fundamental bits-of-information sense, and report wildly different perplexities because one chops the text into more pieces than the other.

∑The math

Fix a piece of text. The total number of bits a model spends on it, bitstotal=N⋅Hbits\text{bits}_{\text{total}} = N \cdot H_{\text{bits}}bitstotal​=N⋅Hbits​, is tokenizer-independent in meaning: it is the length of the message under the model’s compression scheme (Chapter 7’s source-coding view). But perplexity divides those bits by the token count before exponentiating:

PPL=2 bitstotal/N.\text{PPL} = 2^{\,\text{bits}_{\text{total}} / N}.PPL=2bitstotal​/N.

Halve the number of tokens NNN (a coarser tokenizer) while holding the total bits fixed, and the exponent doubles — perplexity gets squared, not doubled: 22h=(2h)22^{2h} = (2^h)^222h=(2h)2. The same identity converts between token-level and word-level perplexity, which older papers report: if the text has WWW words and NNN tokens,

PPLword=PPLtoken N/W.\text{PPL}_{\text{word}} = \text{PPL}_{\text{token}}^{\,N/W}.PPLword​=PPLtokenN/W​.

A token perplexity of 8 at 1.5 tokens per word is a word perplexity of 81.5≈22.68^{1.5} \approx 22.681.5≈22.6. Neither number is more “true”; they are the same total bits divided by different counts.

✏️Worked example

One string of text, 30 bytes long. Model A’s tokenizer splits it into 12 tokens; Model B’s more aggressive tokenizer produces 6 longer tokens. Suppose both models are equally good compressors: each spends 36 bits in total on the string.

  • Model A: 36/12=3.036/12 = 3.036/12=3.0 bits per token ⇒PPL=23=8\Rightarrow \text{PPL} = 2^3 = 8⇒PPL=23=8.
  • Model B: 36/6=6.036/6 = 6.036/6=6.0 bits per token ⇒PPL=26=64\Rightarrow \text{PPL} = 2^6 = 64⇒PPL=26=64.

Model B looks eight times worse on the headline number while being exactly as good. This is not a contrived edge case: tokenizers differ this much across languages (Chapter 19 showed fertility ratios of 2–3× between English and, say, Thai under an English-heavy tokenizer), and every modern model family ships its own vocabulary.

Trap two: the domain changes the number

“Perplexity 3.4” is meaningless without naming the eval text. Code is far more predictable than prose (rigid syntax, repeated identifiers); a model can hit perplexity below 2 on formulaic boilerplate and above 15 on freewheeling dialogue, at the same skill level. A paper reporting perplexity on its own curated held-out split cannot be compared with a paper using a different split — which, after about 2021, is every pair of papers. Domain also interacts with the model’s data mixture (Chapter 31): a model trained heavily on code looks brilliant on a code-heavy eval set and mediocre on fiction, and neither number alone tells you which model you want.

Trap three: normalization details nobody mentions

Even with the tokenizer and the eval text fixed, the exact number depends on protocol choices that routinely differ between implementations:

  • Context and stride. A model with a context window of CCC tokens evaluating a long document must chunk it. Disjoint chunks are cheap but cruel: the first tokens of each chunk get little or no context and rack up high loss. A sliding window with stride sss re-scores tokens with at least C−sC - sC−s tokens of context, giving lower — and more representative — perplexity at C/sC/sC/s times the compute. Two papers using different strides get different numbers from the same model on the same text.
  • Boundaries. Is a beginning-of-sequence token prepended (giving the first real token something to condition on)? Are end-of-document tokens scored? Are documents concatenated so context bleeds across them? Each choice moves the third decimal — or the first, on short-document corpora.
  • Preprocessing. Classic corpora like WikiText came pre-tokenized with spaces around punctuation; evaluating a modern byte-pair-encoding model on them fairly required “detokenization” heuristics, and papers differed in those too.

None of these choices is wrong. The lesson is that a perplexity number is a measurement under a protocol, and comparing numbers means comparing protocols first.

Bits-per-byte: the tokenizer-independent fix

The tokenizer trap has a clean repair: stop dividing by the token count (model-dependent) and divide by the byte count of the raw text (model-independent). The result is bits-per-byte (BPB), popularized by the Pile evaluation suite.

∑The math

With NNN tokens covering BBB bytes of raw text, and per-token cross-entropy HnatsH_{\text{nats}}Hnats​ in nats:

BPB  =  total bitstotal bytes  =  NB⋅Hnatsln⁡2.\text{BPB} \;=\; \frac{\text{total bits}}{\text{total bytes}} \;=\; \frac{N}{B} \cdot \frac{H_{\text{nats}}}{\ln 2}.BPB=total bytestotal bits​=BN​⋅ln2Hnats​​.

The factor 1ln⁡2≈1.4427\frac{1}{\ln 2} \approx 1.4427ln21​≈1.4427 converts nats to bits; NB\frac{N}{B}BN​ is the tokenizer’s inverse compression rate (tokens per byte). Because total bits and total bytes are both properties of the text and the model’s predictions, not of the segmentation, BPB survives a tokenizer swap.

✏️Worked example

A model reports held-out loss 1.601.601.60 nats per token, and its tokenizer averages 3.83.83.8 bytes per token on this corpus. Then bits per token =1.60/0.693=2.31= 1.60 / 0.693 = 2.31=1.60/0.693=2.31, and

BPB=2.313.8≈0.61.\text{BPB} = \frac{2.31}{3.8} \approx 0.61.BPB=3.82.31​≈0.61.

Now re-run the figure’s scenario: Model A had 3.03.03.0 bits/token at 2.52.52.5 bytes/token (303030 bytes /// 121212 tokens), so BPB=3.0/2.5=1.2\text{BPB} = 3.0/2.5 = 1.2BPB=3.0/2.5=1.2. Model B: 6.06.06.0 bits/token at 5.05.05.0 bytes/token, BPB=6.0/5.0=1.2\text{BPB} = 6.0/5.0 = 1.2BPB=6.0/5.0=1.2. The 8-versus-64 perplexity gap vanishes; the models are revealed as equals. For scale: strong models of the GPT-3 era measured roughly 0.70.70.7–1.01.01.0 BPB on diverse text (approximate, and corpus-dependent); frontier 2026 models are meaningfully below that on comparable mixtures, though labs now rarely publish the number.

⚙️In practice

What 2026 labs actually track: held-out loss in nats, per data domain, not perplexity — loss is what scaling laws are fit to, and per-domain curves (web, code, math, multilingual) catch data bugs that a single average hides. Cross-paper perplexity comparisons are effectively dead; when researchers need a tokenizer-neutral comparison (for example, evaluating a Llama-family model against a Qwen-family model on the same corpus), they report BPB or total negative log-likelihood on a fixed byte stream. Perplexity survives as an internal instrument — checkpoint-to-checkpoint, ablation-to-ablation — which is exactly the fenced-in use it was always safe for.

Held-out data, and how it rots

Everything above assumed the eval text is “held out.” That phrase is doing enormous work, and this section is about the two ways it fails: you leak information into your decisions (adaptive overfitting), and the test leaks into the training data (contamination).

The classic discipline, which you met for small models in Chapter 13, splits data three ways. Training data fits the parameters. The validation set steers your many human decisions — learning rate, architecture, when to stop, which of your 40 runs to keep. The test set is touched rarely — ideally once — to report the final number. The reason for the third split is subtle: the validation set stops measuring generalization because you used it to choose. Each choice among options based on validation scores transfers a little information about the validation set’s quirks into the model. One choice is harmless; a research program of thousands of choices is not.

💡Intuition

Think of a multiple-choice exam you retake every week, with the same questions, and you never see the answer key — only your total score. At first your score measures knowledge. But you remember which overall strategies raised the total, and you drift toward whatever this particular exam rewards. After a hundred retakes, your score has risen and your knowledge has not. Nobody cheated; the instrument wore out. This is adaptive overfitting, and it happens at the level of a whole research field: when thousands of papers over five years all select ideas by the same benchmark, the field itself is the student retaking the exam. It is why a benchmark’s final years of “progress” are always suspect, and why fresh, privately-held test sets routinely score models a few points lower than the public versions — even models with no direct contamination at all.

Contamination: the mechanics

Contamination is the blunter failure: the actual test text ends up in the training corpus. Nobody has to cheat deliberately — the pipeline does it automatically, because benchmarks and training corpora are drawn from the same place: the web.

Here is the lifecycle. A benchmark is released — on GitHub, with a paper, as a comma-separated values (CSV) file of questions and answers. Within months it is republished everywhere: tutorial blog posts working through examples, GitHub repos vendoring the data, Hugging Face dataset mirrors, homework sets, forum threads discussing individual questions, the paper’s own PDF on multiple mirrors. Then a crawl like Common Crawl sweeps the web, the crawl feeds a pretraining corpus (Chapter 31), and the test questions — often with their answers adjacent — are now training data. The model does not need to “look up” anything at eval time; gradient descent has already spent real capacity making those exact strings likely, and the benchmark now measures memorization where it means to measure generalization.

Detection is heuristic, and each heuristic has holes:

  • N-gram overlap. Scan the training corpus for long token sequences shared with the test set — GPT-3’s analysis used 13-gram collisions; later efforts used exact substring matches of a few dozen characters. Cheap at scale, and blind to anything reworded: Yang et al. (2023) showed that simply rephrasing test sets before training on them evades n-gram checks entirely while preserving nearly all of the score inflation — enough for a 13B model to “beat GPT-4” on contaminated benchmarks. (The GPT-3 paper is also an honest cautionary tale: a bug caused their decontamination filter to be only partially applied, discovered too late to retrain — so they reported measured overlap and estimated impact instead. That section is still worth reading.)
  • Canary strings. Some benchmarks embed a unique random identifier (a globally unique identifier, or GUID) in every file, with an explicit request not to train on documents containing it. If the model can complete the canary string, the data was trained on — a rare high-precision signal, but only for benchmarks that planted one, and only detecting verbatim ingestion.
  • Completion probes. Prompt the model with the first half of a test item and check whether it reproduces the second half (or the gold answer) verbatim or near-verbatim, far beyond chance. Directly tests the model rather than the (often inaccessible) corpus — essential for evaluating closed models — but rephrased or partially memorized contamination again slips through.
  • Distributional forensics. Compare accuracy on the original test set versus a freshly rewritten variant of the same difficulty; a large gap is contamination’s fingerprint. This is the strongest evidence in practice and the most expensive to produce, since someone must author the fresh items and validate their difficulty.
⚠️Careful

Two mirror-image mistakes to avoid. First, treating “no detected overlap” as “clean”: every detector above has known evasions, and for closed models you cannot inspect the corpus at all — absence of evidence is weak evidence of absence. Second, treating decontamination as free: aggressively deleting everything that overlaps hundreds of benchmarks also deletes legitimate educational text (real Wikipedia articles, real textbook passages that benchmarks were drawn from), which can hurt the model. Labs navigate between these, and their choices differ — one reason identical-sized models score differently for reasons that have nothing to do with architecture.

Downstream evaluation: prompting, choices, and generation

Perplexity measures prediction. Users care about tasks. Since GPT-3, the standard way to turn a raw language model into a task-taker without any training is prompting: write the task into the context and let the model continue. In zero-shot evaluation the prompt is just an instruction and the question. In few-shot (or kkk-shot) evaluation, kkk worked examples are placed before the real question, letting the model infer the format and the task from context. The examples come from a separate dev split — never from the test items being scored — and both which examples you pick and what order they appear in measurably move the score, which is why serious reports fix the shots (or average over random draws) and always state kkk.

That settles how to ask. How to score is where the field quietly split into two methodologies that give different numbers for the same model on the same questions.

∑The math

Method 1 — log-likelihood ranking (for multiple choice). For each candidate answer aia_iai​, compute the model’s total log-probability of that answer’s tokens appended to the question QQQ:

si  =  ∑t=1nilog⁡q ⁣(ai,t | Q,ai,1..t−1),s_i \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{n_i} \log q\!\left(a_{i,t} \,\middle|\, Q, a_{i,1..t-1}\right),si​=t=1∑ni​​logq(ai,t​∣Q,ai,1..t−1​),

where nin_ini​ is the token length of answer iii. Predict arg max⁡isi\argmax_i s_iargmaxi​si​. No text is generated; every candidate is teacher-forced and scored. But raw sums favor short answers (fewer negative terms), so harnesses also compute normalized variants — per-token (si/nis_i / n_isi​/ni​), or per-byte (si/bytesis_i / \text{bytes}_isi​/bytesi​, the acc_norm of lm-evaluation-harness, byte-normalized for exactly the tokenizer-fairness reason from earlier) — and these variants can disagree with the raw sum and with each other about which answer wins.

Method 2 — generative scoring. Sample or greedily decode a free-form answer, extract the model’s choice with a parser (a regex hunting for “Answer: B”, a number-matcher for math), and string-match against the gold answer. This measures an end-to-end pipeline: the model’s knowledge and its ability to follow format, and the parser’s ability to find the answer.

✏️Worked example

Question: “Why do bridges have expansion joints?” with two candidates, and a model that assigns (natural-log) total scores:

  • Candidate A: “because the metal expands when heated” — 7 tokens, total log⁡q=−9.8\log q = -9.8logq=−9.8, per-token −1.40-1.40−1.40.
  • Candidate B: “yes” — 1 token, total log⁡q=−2.5\log q = -2.5logq=−2.5, per-token −2.50-2.50−2.50.

Raw-sum ranking picks B (−2.5-2.5−2.5 is greater than −9.8-9.8−9.8): the nonsense answer wins because short strings are cheap. Per-token ranking picks A (−1.40-1.40−1.40 beats −2.50-2.50−2.50): the model finds A’s tokens more probable, one by one. Same model, same probabilities, opposite verdicts — one normalization flag flips the measured “knowledge.” Real benchmark answers are less lopsided in length, but length still correlates with answer style, and published accuracies shift by whole points depending on the variant used.

Why do the two methods disagree systematically? Log-likelihood ranking is gentle: the model never has to produce anything, so a base model with good knowledge but no instruction-following scores well. Generative scoring folds in everything that can go wrong after the knowledge: the model answers correctly but in the wrong format, the regex misses it, the model hedges, the chat model prepends “Sure! Here’s my answer:” and the parser grabs the wrong token. Base models therefore tend to look better under log-likelihood scoring and instruction-tuned models under generative scoring — and a leaderboard that silently mixes the two is comparing apples with orchards. Since the rise of chain-of-thought and reasoning models, generative scoring has become the default for anything involving multi-step work (you cannot teacher-force your way through showing your work), which makes parsing and formatting an ever-bigger, mostly invisible slice of every headline number.

Two further sources of noise deserve names:

Sampling variance. Greedy decoding (temperature 0 — see Chapter 39 for what temperature is) makes each question’s outcome deterministic up to hardware nondeterminism. Any temperature above 0 makes the benchmark score itself a random variable: the same model, same harness, same day can move a point or two between runs on a small benchmark. Honest practice is to report the sampling configuration and either use greedy decoding or average over several seeds — and on hard-but-small sets, to report a confidence interval, because 25 questions at temperature 0.7 measure almost nothing.

Prompt sensitivity. Sclar et al. (2023) measured what happens when you change only the formatting of a prompt — separators, casing, whether options are labeled “A.” or “(a)” — and found spreads of several points on typical tasks and, for some model–task pairs, tens of points between the best and worst semantically-identical formats. There is no “neutral” format; there is only a format, disclosed or not. The same disease afflicts chat templates: an instruction-tuned model evaluated without its own chat template (the special tokens it was fine-tuned to expect) can drop dramatically for reasons that have nothing to do with capability.

⚙️In practice

All of the above is why evaluation harnesses disagree, and why the field standardized on shared ones. EleutherAI’s lm-evaluation-harness is the de facto reference: it pins the prompt template, shot count, scoring method, normalization, and parser per task, and a result is reproducible given the harness version and config. Stanford’s HELM made the complementary point that a single accuracy number under a single condition is not an evaluation at all. The cautionary tale everyone cites: in 2023 the Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard’s MMLU numbers for LLaMA models came out roughly fifteen points below the paper’s, and the postmortem found three implementations (the original benchmark code, the harness, HELM) scoring the same checkpoints very differently — different prompts, different scoring method, different normalization, all defensible. The operational rule that survives: never compare two numbers produced by different harnesses or configs. To compare models, re-run both yourself under one pinned setup.

Judges: humans and models

Multiple choice and string-matching cover tasks with one right answer. Most of what people use models for — writing, explaining, coding with taste, summarizing — has no gold string to match. For open-ended quality you need a judge, and there are two kinds on offer: humans, and other models. Both are biased instruments; the difference is that the biases are now well documented.

Human evaluation sounds like the gold standard and is merely the least bad. The workable protocol is pairwise comparison — show two anonymous responses to one prompt, ask which is better — because absolute scores (“rate 1–7”) drift across raters and sessions while comparisons are stable. Pairwise votes across many prompts and raters are then aggregated into a ranking with the Bradley–Terry model (the statistics behind chess Elo): each model gets a strength parameter, and the probability that model iii beats model jjj is modeled as eβi/(eβi+eβj)e^{\beta_i} / (e^{\beta_i} + e^{\beta_j})eβi​/(eβi​+eβj​); fitting the β\betaβs to the observed votes yields the leaderboard. Chatbot Arena runs exactly this at scale with public volunteers; the arena ecosystem, its Elo conventions, and its controversies get their full treatment in Benchmarks and How to Read Them.

But raters exhibit systematic, measured biases. They favor longer responses at equal correctness. They favor confident style — clean markdown, headers, bullet lists — over accuracy they cannot easily check. They reward sycophancy: a response that agrees with the premise embedded in the question beats one that correctly pushes back. And the population of raters is itself a distribution — Arena voters skew toward technically-minded people asking chatbot-shaped questions, so an Arena rank is a measurement on that prompt distribution, not on yours. (Arena now publishes style-controlled rankings that regress out length and formatting precisely because the raw vote conflated them with quality; models can and do move noticeably between the raw and controlled boards.)

LLM-as-judge — a large language model (LLM) as the judge — replaces the human with a strong model, and it exists because human evaluation costs dollars and days per data point while a judge model costs cents and seconds. The judge is shown the prompt and response(s) plus a rubric, and asked for a verdict — either pairwise (“which is better, A or B?”) or single-answer grading against a reference. The foundational study, MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023), found that a frontier judge’s agreement with human majority votes was roughly as high as humans’ agreement with each other (both in the low-to-mid 80s percent on their data — approximate, and setup-dependent). That is the case for the method. The same paper measured its pathologies:

  • Position bias: judges favor whichever answer appears first. Mitigation: run both orderings and average, counting disagreements as ties.
  • Verbosity bias: longer answers win at equal quality — the judge inherited the human bias from its own reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) training (Chapter 35 explains how that happens).
  • Self-preference: judges rate their own family’s outputs higher, which matters now that most labs both compete and publish evals judged by their own models.
  • Weak verification: a judge grading math or code without a reference answer or execution is fooled by confident wrong answers at a distressing rate. Grading against a reference, or better, running the code / checking the final number programmatically, fixes most of this — which is why verifiable domains migrated to programmatic checking and judges kept the unverifiable ones.

When is a judge trustworthy? The practical checklist: the judge is much stronger than the models being compared (a judge grading its peers is a coin with opinions); there is a rubric and ideally a reference answer; pairwise orderings are swapped and averaged; the judge’s identity and version are pinned (judges drift across model updates, silently rescaling every number); and some slice of judgments is spot-checked against humans. Under those conditions, LLM-as-judge is the workhorse of 2026 evaluation. Without them it is a random-number generator with excellent grammar.

⚠️Careful

Goodhart’s law applies with force: the moment a judge (human or model) becomes a training signal rather than a measurement, models learn to exploit its biases instead of improving. This is not hypothetical — it is the documented length-inflation of RLHF-trained models (reward models prefer longer, so models got longer), and it is why “our model wins X% of judge comparisons against GPT-4” claims from 2023–24 aged so badly: many of those models had been trained, directly or via distillation, on the judge’s own preferences. A metric you train against stops being an eval. Keep the two roles separate or know that you haven’t.

Capability, propensity, and the lab’s dashboard

A distinction that becomes critical the moment evaluation touches safety: capability is what a model can do under the best elicitation you can find; propensity is what it tends to do under realistic conditions. They are different quantities, they require different experimental designs, and conflating them produces nonsense in both directions.

Measuring capability means maximizing: strong prompts, few-shot examples, chain-of-thought, multiple samples with the best one kept, tools if tools are in scope. Any refusal or formatting failure is an elicitation problem, not evidence of inability — an instruction-tuned model that declines to answer a chemistry question still contains the chemistry. Under-elicited capability evals systematically underestimate, which is comfortable and dangerous in safety contexts: “the model scored low on our hazardous-knowledge eval” means little if the eval never tried jailbreaks, fine-tuning, or better prompts, because someone else will. (A newer wrinkle: a sufficiently situationally aware model could in principle sandbag — deliberately underperform when it detects it is being tested. As of 2026 this is demonstrated in contrived setups rather than observed in the wild, but it converts “the model scored low” from a measurement into a claim requiring further argument.)

Measuring propensity means sampling honestly: realistic prompts drawn from something like the deployed distribution, default settings, no adversarial pressure — because the question is not “could it?” but “does it, and how often?” Sycophancy rates, refusal rates, how often the model fabricates a citation when unsure: these are propensity numbers, they depend on the prompt distribution, and quoting one without naming that distribution is like quoting a crime rate without naming the city.

⚙️In practice

What evaluation actually looks like inside a lab during a pretraining run (the fuller picture is in Chapter 34):

  • Loss curves, always. Held-out loss per data domain, updated continuously. This is the highest-signal, lowest-noise instrument, and a kink in one domain’s curve is often the first symptom of a data-pipeline bug or a bad mixture change.
  • Canary evals at checkpoints. Every so many billion tokens, a fixed battery of small, fast, smooth benchmarks runs against the latest checkpoint. Smoothness is the selection criterion: tasks scored by log-likelihood over choices improve gradually and make good progress gauges, while hard generative tasks sit at zero for most of the run and then move late — useless as an early-warning signal, watched anyway as milestones. (This gap between smooth underlying loss and cliff-like task accuracy is the “emergence” debate; Chapter 32 takes it up properly.)
  • Internal held-out sets. Labs maintain private eval sets that are never published, precisely because publication starts the contamination clock ticking. These stay informative for years while public benchmarks rot.
  • Post-training gates. After supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and RLHF, a different battery: instruction-following, refusal behavior on both harmful and harmless-but-suspicious prompts, sycophancy, judge-based quality comparisons against the previous production model, and safety/propensity suites. Release is gated on the battery, which is why “the eval suite” at a frontier lab is closer to a department than a script.

Where this leaves us

  • You can compute perplexity from scratch — cross-entropy per token, exponentiated — and read it three ways: average surprise, inverse geometric mean probability, and effective branching factor.
  • You know its fence: perplexity comparisons are valid across checkpoints and ablations sharing data, tokenizer, and protocol — and you can convert to bits-per-byte (BPB=NB⋅Hnats/ln⁡2\text{BPB} = \tfrac{N}{B} \cdot H_{\text{nats}}/\ln 2BPB=BN​⋅Hnats​/ln2) when tokenizers differ.
  • You can explain why test sets rot (adaptive overfitting) and trace the contamination pipeline from benchmark release to inflated score, plus the four detection heuristics and the holes in each.
  • You can name why one model gets two different scores on the same benchmark: log-likelihood versus generative scoring, normalization variants, shot count and selection, prompt format, chat template, sampling temperature, harness version.
  • You know the measured biases of human raters (length, style, sycophancy) and LLM judges (position, verbosity, self-preference), the mitigations, and the Goodhart trap of training against your own judge.
  • You can distinguish capability (best-effort elicitation) from propensity (honest sampling from a realistic distribution) and say which one a given eval measures.
