Post-Training II: DPO and Its Family

The full DPO derivation, line by line — how the RLHF objective collapses into one classification loss — plus IPO, KTO, ORPO, SimPO, rejection sampling, and an honest 2026 scorecard against online RL.

The previous chapter ended with a machine that works but groans. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) needs four models in memory at once — the policy being trained, a frozen reference copy, a separately trained reward model, and a value model — plus a generation loop inside the training loop, plus the notoriously touchy machinery of proximal policy optimization (PPO). It works. It built ChatGPT. But every engineer who has run it has wondered: all this, to make the model prefer response A over response B?

In 2023, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) answered with one of the most satisfying pieces of algebra in modern machine learning. Rafailov and colleagues took the exact objective RLHF optimizes, wrote down its known closed-form solution, turned that formula inside out, and watched the single intractable quantity in the problem cancel itself out of the loss. What remains is a plain classification loss — no reward model, no sampling during training, no RL at all — that provably targets the same optimum. The paper’s subtitle says it plainly: your language model is secretly a reward model.

This chapter is a guided reading of that derivation, expanded so that every step is justified rather than teleported past. Deriving DPO carefully is worth a chapter for two reasons. First, DPO and its descendants are, alongside online RL, one of the two workhorse families of preference tuning in 2026 — you will meet them in every open-model technical report. Second, the derivation itself is a masterclass in a skill this book keeps trying to teach: taking an objective you cannot compute and transforming it, legally, into one you can. By the end you will be able to reproduce the whole argument on paper, read the DPO loss the way its authors do — gradient term by gradient term — and judge honestly when DPO is the right tool and when it is not.

The objective we are trying to optimize

Start from where RLHF left us. We have a prompt xxx, a model πθ(y∣x)\pi_\theta(y \mid x)πθ​(y∣x) that assigns a probability to each complete response yyy (a whole sequence of tokens — the product of per-token probabilities), and a frozen reference model πref\pi_{\text{ref}}πref​, which is almost always the supervised fine-tuned (SFT) checkpoint we started from. We also, for now, assume a reward function r(x,y)r(x, y)r(x,y) that scores how good response yyy is for prompt xxx — in RLHF this is a learned reward model (RM); keep it abstract for the moment.

RLHF does not maximize reward alone. It maximizes reward on a leash:

max⁡πθ    Ex∼D, y∼πθ(⋅∣x)[ r(x,y) ]  −  β DKL ⁣(πθ(⋅∣x) ∥ πref(⋅∣x)).\max_{\pi_\theta} \;\; \E_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)} \big[\, r(x, y) \,\big] \;-\; \beta \, \KL\!\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x) \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)\big).πθ​max​Ex∼D,y∼πθ​(⋅∣x)​[r(x,y)]−βDKL​(πθ​(⋅∣x)∥πref​(⋅∣x)).

Read it out: sample prompts xxx from the data distribution D\mathcal{D}D, sample responses yyy from the current policy, collect reward — but pay a penalty proportional to the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the policy and the reference model, with β>0\beta > 0β>0 setting the price (this β\betaβ is the KL-leash strength — no relation to Adam’s momentum β1,β2\beta_1, \beta_2β1​,β2​ from Chapter 11; see the notation reference). (KL divergence — the expected extra surprise from using one distribution when reality follows another — was built in Chapter 7; it is zero when the two distributions match and grows as they diverge.)

💡Intuition

The KL term is the leash, and it exists because the reward is fallible. A learned reward model is only trustworthy on responses that look like its training data. An unleashed policy will wander into weird corners of text space where the reward model assigns high scores to garbage — reward hacking. The KL penalty says: you may increase reward, but only by reweighting behaviors the reference model already considers plausible. Small β\betaβ means a long leash (chase reward aggressively); large β\betaβ means a short one (stay close to the SFT model). Every method in this chapter, DPO included, is an answer to the same constrained question: how do I move probability mass toward preferred responses without leaving the reference model’s neighborhood?

Two features of this objective matter for what follows. First, it is an expectation over responses sampled from the policy being trained — that circular dependence is why RLHF needs a generation loop and RL machinery. Second, and this is the crack DPO drives a wedge into: for this particular objective, unlike almost any other in deep learning, we can write down the optimal solution exactly.

The optimal policy has a closed form

Here is the claim, and it deserves a moment of astonishment before we prove it. The policy that maximizes the KL-regularized objective above is, for every prompt xxx:

π∗(y∣x)  =  1Z(x) πref(y∣x) exp⁡ ⁣(r(x,y)β),Z(x)  =  ∑yπref(y∣x) exp⁡ ⁣(r(x,y)β).\pi^*(y \mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)} \, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\, \exp\!\Big(\frac{r(x, y)}{\beta}\Big), \qquad Z(x) \;=\; \sum_{y} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\, \exp\!\Big(\frac{r(x, y)}{\beta}\Big).π∗(y∣x)=Z(x)1​πref​(y∣x)exp(βr(x,y)​),Z(x)=y∑​πref​(y∣x)exp(βr(x,y)​).

In words: take the reference model’s probability for each response, multiply it by an exponential boost that grows with reward, and renormalize so everything sums to one. Z(x)Z(x)Z(x) — the partition function — is exactly that normalizer: the boosted probabilities summed over every possible response yyy.

💡Intuition

You have seen this shape before: it is a softmax, the same operation that ends every forward pass — but taken over the space of entire responses rather than a vocabulary. Physicists call it a Boltzmann distribution: think of −r(x,y)-r(x,y)−r(x,y) as an energy and β\betaβ as a temperature. High β\betaβ (short leash, high temperature) flattens the exponential, so π∗\pi^*π∗ barely deviates from πref\pi_{\text{ref}}πref​; low β\betaβ sharpens it, concentrating mass on the highest-reward responses. The optimal policy is not “always output the best response” — it is “the reference model, tilted exponentially toward reward, with β\betaβ controlling the tilt.”

∑The math

Why this is the optimum. The trick is to rewrite the objective so that the candidate optimum appears inside a KL divergence. Fix one prompt xxx and write the objective for a candidate policy π\piπ (dropping the θ\thetaθ subscript for cleanliness). Expanding the KL divergence as an expectation of a log-ratio:

J(π)=Ey∼π[r(x,y)]−β Ey∼π ⁣[log⁡π(y∣x)πref(y∣x)].J(\pi) = \E_{y \sim \pi}\big[r(x,y)\big] - \beta\, \E_{y \sim \pi}\!\left[\log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}\right].J(π)=Ey∼π​[r(x,y)]−βEy∼π​[logπref​(y∣x)π(y∣x)​].

The denominator inside the log is almost a probability distribution — it is missing only its normalizer. Multiply and divide by Z(x)Z(x)Z(x), using the definition of π∗\pi^*π∗ above:

J(π)=−β Ey∼π ⁣[log⁡π(y∣x)π∗(y∣x)]  +  βlog⁡Z(x)=−β DKL(π ∥ π∗)+βlog⁡Z(x).J(\pi) = -\beta\, \E_{y \sim \pi}\!\left[ \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi^*(y \mid x)}\right] \;+\; \beta \log Z(x) = -\beta\, \KL\big(\pi \,\|\, \pi^*\big) + \beta \log Z(x).J(π)=−βEy∼π​[logπ∗(y∣x)π(y∣x)​]+βlogZ(x)=−βDKL​(π∥π∗)+βlogZ(x).

Now read the final form. The second term, βlog⁡Z(x)\beta \log Z(x)βlogZ(x), does not depend on π\piπ at all — it is a constant of the problem. The first term is −β-\beta−β times a KL divergence, which is always ≥0\geq 0≥0 and equals zero exactly when the two distributions are identical. So J(π)J(\pi)J(π) is maximized precisely when DKL(π ∥ π∗)=0\KL(\pi \,\|\, \pi^*) = 0DKL​(π∥π∗)=0, i.e. when π=π∗\pi = \pi^*π=π∗. No calculus of variations needed — the KL’s nonnegativity does all the work.

🕰History

Neither the closed form nor the trick is original to DPO, and the DPO authors say so. Exponentially tilting a prior by a reward is the standard solution throughout KL-regularized control and “RL as inference”: Todorov’s linearly solvable Markov decision processes (2006), Ziebart’s maximum-entropy inverse RL (2008), Peters’ relative entropy policy search (2010), and advantage-weighted regression all orbit the same formula, and work on RLHF’s KL penalty as Bayesian inference had already framed the RLHF optimum this way in 2022. DPO’s contribution is not the closed form — it is what comes next: noticing that the formula can be run backwards, and that preference data then makes the intractable part vanish.

So we possess an exact formula for the optimal RLHF policy. Why did anyone bother with PPO? Because of Z(x)Z(x)Z(x).

✏️Worked example

How intractable is Z(x)Z(x)Z(x), concretely? The sum runs over every response the tokenizer can express. Take Llama 3’s vocabulary of roughly 128,000 tokens and responses of a mere 200 tokens. The number of length-200 sequences is 128,000200=10200×log⁡10128,000≈101021128{,}000^{200} = 10^{200 \times \log_{10} 128{,}000} \approx 10^{1021}128,000200=10200×log10​128,000≈101021. For scale: there are about 108010^{80}1080 atoms in the observable universe. If every atom were a computer evaluating 101210^{12}1012 sequences per second since the Big Bang (≈4×1017\approx 4 \times 10^{17}≈4×1017 seconds), you would have checked about 1011010^{110}10110 sequences — a rounding error of a rounding error of the total. And this sum must be recomputed for every prompt, since ZZZ depends on xxx. Estimating it by sampling is hopeless too: the exponential tilt means the sum is dominated by rare high-reward sequences that ordinary samples never hit. The closed form is an exact formula you cannot evaluate — a map with “here be dragons” written over the only quantity you need.

Turning the formula inside out

Here is the move that makes the paper. Instead of using the closed form to compute the policy from the reward — blocked by Z(x)Z(x)Z(x) — solve it for the reward, and use it to express the reward in terms of the policy.

∑The math

Start from the closed form and isolate rrr. Multiply both sides by Z(x)Z(x)Z(x), divide by πref\pi_{\text{ref}}πref​, and take logarithms:

π∗(y∣x)=1Z(x) πref(y∣x) e r(x,y)/β    ⟹    r(x,y)β=log⁡π∗(y∣x)πref(y∣x)+log⁡Z(x),\pi^*(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\, e^{\,r(x,y)/\beta} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \frac{r(x,y)}{\beta} = \log \frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \log Z(x),π∗(y∣x)=Z(x)1​πref​(y∣x)er(x,y)/β⟹βr(x,y)​=logπref​(y∣x)π∗(y∣x)​+logZ(x),

so

r(x,y)  =  βlog⁡π∗(y∣x)πref(y∣x)  +  βlog⁡Z(x).r(x, y) \;=\; \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} \;+\; \beta \log Z(x).r(x,y)=βlogπref​(y∣x)π∗(y∣x)​+βlogZ(x).

Every reward function is thus representable as ”β\betaβ times a log-ratio of policies, plus a per-prompt constant.” The intractable Z(x)Z(x)Z(x) is still here — but notice where it sits: in an additive term that depends only on xxx, not on yyy. Anything that compares two responses to the same prompt will subtract it away.

And comparing two responses to the same prompt is exactly what preference data does. Recall from Chapter 35 the Bradley–Terry model of pairwise choices: given a prompt xxx and two responses, the probability that a human prefers ywy_wyw​ (the “winner,” or chosen response) over yly_lyl​ (the “loser,” or rejected one) depends only on the difference of their rewards, squashed through a sigmoid:

P(yw≻yl∣x)  =  σ(r(x,yw)−r(x,yl)),σ(u)=11+e−u.\P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) \;=\; \sigma\big(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)\big), \qquad \sigma(u) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-u}}.P(yw​≻yl​∣x)=σ(r(x,yw​)−r(x,yl​)),σ(u)=1+e−u1​.

(σ\sigmaσ is the logistic sigmoid from Chapter 9; ≻\succ≻ reads “is preferred to.”) RLHF fits a reward model by maximizing the likelihood of observed preferences under this model. Now substitute our inverted formula for the reward — evaluated at the optimal policy — into the difference:

∑The math

Line by line, with the cancellation in view:

r(x,yw)−r(x,yl)=βlog⁡π∗(yw∣x)πref(yw∣x)+βlog⁡Z(x)⏟r(x, yw)  −  (βlog⁡π∗(yl∣x)πref(yl∣x)+βlog⁡Z(x))⏟r(x, yl)r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l) = \underbrace{\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)}_{r(x,\,y_w)} \;-\; \underbrace{\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)\right)}_{r(x,\,y_l)}r(x,yw​)−r(x,yl​)=r(x,yw​)βlogπref​(yw​∣x)π∗(yw​∣x)​+βlogZ(x)​​−r(x,yl​)(βlogπref​(yl​∣x)π∗(yl​∣x)​+βlogZ(x))​​

Distribute the minus sign: the first reward contributes +βlog⁡Z(x)+\beta \log Z(x)+βlogZ(x), the second contributes −βlog⁡Z(x)-\beta \log Z(x)−βlogZ(x) — same prompt, so the same number both times. They annihilate:

r(x,yw)−r(x,yl)=βlog⁡π∗(yw∣x)πref(yw∣x)−βlog⁡π∗(yl∣x)πref(yl∣x).r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}.r(x,yw​)−r(x,yl​)=βlogπref​(yw​∣x)π∗(yw​∣x)​−βlogπref​(yl​∣x)π∗(yl​∣x)​.

The only quantity that made the closed form unusable is gone. What remains involves nothing but log-probabilities of two specific, observed responses — four forward passes: policy and reference, chosen and rejected.

Now the endgame. The Bradley–Terry likelihood of the human preference data, written with rewards, has become a likelihood written directly in terms of the optimal policy. So instead of the RLHF two-step — fit a reward model to preferences, then run RL to find π∗\pi^*π∗ — parameterize the policy as πθ\pi_\thetaπθ​ and fit it to the preferences directly, by maximizing that same likelihood. Minimizing the negative log-likelihood over a preference dataset D={(x,yw,yl)}\mathcal{D} = \{(x, y_w, y_l)\}D={(x,yw​,yl​)} gives the DPO loss:

LDPO(θ)=− E(x, yw, yl)∼D[log⁡σ ⁣(βlog⁡πθ(yw∣x)πref(yw∣x)−βlog⁡πθ(yl∣x)πref(yl∣x))].\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) = -\,\E_{(x,\, y_w,\, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \sigma\!\left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)} \right)\right].LDPO​(θ)=−E(x,yw​,yl​)∼D​[logσ(βlogπref​(yw​∣x)πθ​(yw​∣x)​−βlogπref​(yl​∣x)πθ​(yl​∣x)​)].

This is a supervised loss. No sampling, no reward model, no value function, no PPO clipping. You could implement it in a dozen lines on top of an ordinary training loop: compute the summed token log-probabilities of the chosen and rejected responses under both models, combine, sigmoid, log, backpropagate (Chapter 10).

✎Note

One honest caveat about what was proved. The derivation shows that if the Bradley–Terry model describes the preferences, and if the policy class can represent π∗\pi^*π∗, then the DPO optimum coincides with the RLHF optimum. It does not promise that gradient descent on finite, off-policy preference data reaches that optimum, nor that the two methods behave the same along the way. The gap between “same optimum in theory” and “same behavior in practice” is where most of the rest of this chapter lives.

Reading the loss: the implicit reward and its gradient

A loss function is a policy about what to learn. Having derived DPO’s, we should now read it, and the key to reading it is a definition the derivation handed us for free. Define the implicit reward of the model πθ\pi_\thetaπθ​:

r^θ(x,y)  =  βlog⁡πθ(y∣x)πref(y∣x).\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) \;=\; \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}.r^θ​(x,y)=βlogπref​(y∣x)πθ​(y∣x)​.

This is the reward-minus-constant from the inversion step, with πθ\pi_\thetaπθ​ standing in for π∗\pi^*π∗. It is positive where the policy has raised a response’s probability above the reference model’s, negative where it has suppressed it. In this precise sense the language model is a reward model: any policy, paired with a reference, defines a scoring function over responses, and the DPO loss is nothing but Bradley–Terry logistic regression on that score: L=−log⁡σ(r^θ(x,yw)−r^θ(x,yl))\mathcal{L} = -\log \sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l))L=−logσ(r^θ​(x,yw​)−r^θ​(x,yl​)).

What does its gradient actually do to the model?

∑The math

A gradient-descent step therefore pushes log⁡πθ(yw∣x)\log \pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)logπθ​(yw​∣x) up and log⁡πθ(yl∣x)\log \pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)logπθ​(yl​∣x) down — but scaled, pair by pair, by σ(r^θ(x,yl)−r^θ(x,yw))\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w))σ(r^θ​(x,yl​)−r^θ​(x,yw​)): the sigmoid of the backwards margin, which is precisely the probability the implicit reward model currently assigns to the wrong ranking.

That weighting factor is the intelligence of the method, so dwell on it. When the model already ranks the pair correctly by a wide margin, the weight is near 0 and the pair is left alone. When the model confidently prefers the rejected response, the weight approaches 1 and the pair gets the full force of the update. Every batch automatically triages itself toward the examples where the implicit reward model is most mistaken — the same “learn from your errors, proportionally” behavior that made logistic regression and cross-entropy loss robust long before deep learning, here inherited by preference tuning.

The loss is −log⁡σ(0.1)=−log⁡(0.525)≈0.644-\log \sigma(0.1) = -\log(0.525) \approx 0.644−logσ(0.1)=−log(0.525)≈0.644, and the gradient weight is σ(−0.1)≈0.475\sigma(-0.1) \approx 0.475σ(−0.1)≈0.475 — nearly full strength, because a margin of 0.1 is nearly a coin flip.

Now a pair the model gets badly wrong: r^w=−0.5\hat{r}_w = -0.5r^w​=−0.5, r^l=0.2\hat{r}_l = 0.2r^l​=0.2, margin u=−0.7u = -0.7u=−0.7. Loss −log⁡σ(−0.7)=−log⁡(0.332)≈1.103-\log \sigma(-0.7) = -\log(0.332) \approx 1.103−logσ(−0.7)=−log(0.332)≈1.103; weight σ(0.7)≈0.668\sigma(0.7) \approx 0.668σ(0.7)≈0.668 — this pair pulls about 1.4 times harder than the first. And a pair the model has already mastered, margin u=4u = 4u=4: loss ≈0.018\approx 0.018≈0.018, weight σ(−4)≈0.018\sigma(-4) \approx 0.018σ(−4)≈0.018 — effectively retired from training.

What β\betaβ does. β\betaβ appears twice, and the two roles are one role. Inside the margin, it scales log-probability ratios into rewards: with β=0.5\beta = 0.5β=0.5 instead of 0.10.10.1, the same log-ratios produce margins five times larger, so the sigmoid saturates sooner — pairs count as “learned” after much smaller policy movements, and training leaves the reference model’s neighborhood more reluctantly. That is exactly the original meaning of β\betaβ as the KL price: large β\betaβ, short leash; small β\betaβ, long leash. Practical values run from about 0.01 (aggressive) to 0.5 (conservative), with 0.1 the most common default — but the right value interacts with data quality and epochs, and is worth sweeping.

⚠️Careful

Both log-probabilities usually go down. Nothing in the gradient constrains the chosen response’s absolute probability — only the margin. In practice a well-known and initially alarming dynamic appears: log⁡πθ(yw∣x)\log \pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)logπθ​(yw​∣x) and log⁡πθ(yl∣x)\log \pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)logπθ​(yl​∣x) both fall during DPO training, with the rejected one falling faster. The freed probability mass migrates to sequences outside the dataset entirely — sometimes toward better responses, sometimes not; researchers have documented cases where it lands on the opposite of what was intended. If you monitor only the margin, training looks healthy while the model drifts away from your actual chosen responses. Log all four quantities, not only the loss — and this is one reason production recipes (Llama 3’s among them) add a small standard negative log-likelihood (NLL) term on the chosen response to the DPO loss, anchoring the winners while the margin machinery separates the pairs.

DPO in practice

The derivation is clean; running DPO well involves a handful of judgment calls the equations do not make for you.

The reference model. Theory says πref\pi_{\text{ref}}πref​ is the distribution whose neighborhood you trust — in practice, use the SFT checkpoint that initializes πθ\pi_\thetaπθ​, frozen. Using anything else (say, a generic base model while training a heavily fine-tuned policy) makes the implicit reward measure divergence from the wrong anchor. Two engineering notes: since πref\pi_{\text{ref}}πref​ never changes, its log-probabilities for the whole dataset can be precomputed once, halving training-time memory and compute — DPO then needs only one live model, versus RLHF’s four. And if you DPO a model that was itself DPO’d (iterative rounds, below), the reference should generally be updated to the latest checkpoint, or the leash tethers you to a stale past.

Where the pairs come from. The derivation quietly assumed the preference data reflects the distributions in the loss. In reality, much open preference data (UltraFeedback and its descendants) contains responses generated by other models entirely. DPO still runs — the loss only needs log-probabilities — but the implicit reward is then being fit far off-policy: you are adjusting πθ\pi_\thetaπθ​‘s rankings of sequences it might never produce, and the “raise/lower” pressure applies in a region of text space the model does not inhabit. Preference pairs sampled from the SFT model itself (then labeled by humans or a judge) consistently work better, and this on-policy versus off-policy distinction is the single deepest divide in the method family — we return to it below.

Overfitting to preferences. Preference datasets are small — typically 10410^4104 to 10610^6106 pairs against the trillions of tokens of pretraining — and the Bradley–Terry loss, as the next section makes precise, is happiest driving margins toward infinity. DPO therefore overfits fast and hard: one epoch is the norm, two is bold, and the symptom is a model whose margins keep improving while its actual generations develop tics — most famously length.

⚠️Careful

Length exploitation. In most human- and AI-labeled preference data, longer answers win more often (raters reward thoroughness, and judges like GPT-4 have a measured verbosity bias). DPO’s implicit reward is a sum over token log-ratios, so length is an easy direction in which to grow margins: models routinely emerge from DPO 1.5–2× more verbose with no quality gain — the SimPO paper documents this cleanly across setups. This is not a bug in the algebra; it is the implicit reward model faithfully learning a real bias in the data. Mitigations: length-balance or length-penalize the pairs, add explicit length regularization, or use a length-normalized objective like SimPO. If you evaluate a DPO’d model with a large language model (LLM) judge that also loves length, the bias compounds and your win rates are partly fiction — see Chapter 46.

⚙️In practice

A representative 2026 open-model DPO setup: start from the SFT checkpoint; β≈0.1\beta \approx 0.1β≈0.1 (sweep 0.01–0.5); learning rate 10−710^{-7}10−7 to 10−610^{-6}10−6 — roughly 10–100× below SFT rates, because you are sculpting a finished model, not teaching it; one epoch over 10410^4104–10610^6106 pairs; global batch 64–512 pairs; cosine decay; sequence-summed log-probs with prompt tokens masked out. The Llama 3 report adds two production details worth stealing: mask the special formatting tokens out of the DPO loss (otherwise the margin machinery latches onto header tokens and causes repetition or abrupt truncation), and add a small NLL term on chosen responses (the report gives it a scaling coefficient of 0.2) to hold absolute likelihoods up. Hugging Face’s TRL (Transformer Reinforcement Learning) library and its DPOTrainer are the de facto standard implementation and pair naturally with low-rank adaptation (LoRA) (Chapter 38) — with a quantized base and precomputed reference log-probs, DPO on a 7B model fits on one consumer GPU, which is much of why the open community adopted it overnight.

The family

DPO’s publication set off two years of variations, each isolating one weakness and fixing it. The family is best held in your head as a tree: preference signal at the root, then a fork — train an explicit reward model and use it (the left branch RLHF pioneered), or let the policy be the reward model (the DPO branch), with the DPO branch then splitting on what to do about overfitting, pairing, and the reference model.

IPO — bound the margin. Azar et al. put their finger on why DPO overfits: with finite data, most pairs are effectively deterministic (labelers agreed), and the Bradley–Terry likelihood of a deterministic preference is maximized only as the margin r^w−r^l→∞\hat{r}_w - \hat{r}_l \to \inftyr^w​−r^l​→∞. The sigmoid never says “enough” — it merely saturates slower and slower while the policy keeps shoving πθ(yl)\pi_\theta(y_l)πθ​(yl​) toward zero, drifting arbitrarily far from the reference despite the KL machinery in the derivation. Identity Preference Optimization (IPO) swaps the log-sigmoid for a squared loss that regresses the margin toward a fixed target: minimize (hθ(x,yw,yl)−12τ)2\big(h_\theta(x, y_w, y_l) - \tfrac{1}{2\tau}\big)^2(hθ​(x,yw​,yl​)−2τ1​)2, where hθh_\thetahθ​ is the same log-ratio margin (without β\betaβ) and τ\tauτ plays the leash-strength role. Once a pair’s margin reaches the target, its gradient is zero — overshooting is penalized, not rewarded. One key idea: a bounded target margin makes “done” a place the optimizer can actually reach.

KTO — drop the pairs. Every method so far needs paired comparisons — two responses to the same prompt, one labeled better. Real products rarely have that; they have a thumbs-up/thumbs-down button. Kahneman–Tversky Optimization (KTO) targets exactly this unpaired setting, borrowing its loss shape from prospect theory — Kahneman and Tversky’s account of how humans value outcomes relative to a reference point, with losses looming larger than gains. KTO scores each single response by its implicit reward, compares it not to a partner response but to a reference point (an estimate of the policy’s average drift from πref\pi_{\text{ref}}πref​, in practice computed over the batch), and asks desirable responses to sit above that point and undesirable ones below it, with separately tunable weights for the two classes since thumbs-down data is usually scarcer. One key idea: judge each response against the policy’s average behavior, and unpaired binary feedback — the signal products actually collect — becomes trainable.

ORPO — drop the reference model and the extra stage. Odds Ratio Preference Optimization (ORPO) asks a blunter question: why run SFT and preference tuning as separate phases at all? ORPO trains in one stage with the ordinary SFT cross-entropy loss on chosen responses plus a small penalty term: the log odds ratio between chosen and rejected under the current model (odds meaning p/(1−p)p/(1-p)p/(1−p), computed from per-token average probabilities), pushed through a log-sigmoid. The SFT term anchors fluency and absolute likelihood — playing the role the reference model played in DPO — while the odds-ratio term supplies the pairwise separation. One key idea: the SFT loss itself can be the leash, making a separate frozen reference model unnecessary.

SimPO — normalize by length. Simple Preference Optimization (SimPO) redefines the implicit reward as the average log-probability per token, β∣y∣log⁡πθ(y∣x)\tfrac{\beta}{|y|} \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)∣y∣β​logπθ​(y∣x), with no reference model, and adds a fixed target margin γ\gammaγ inside the sigmoid. Dividing by length ∣y∣|y|∣y∣ removes the structural advantage long responses enjoy in every summed objective — attacking the length pathology at its root rather than patching it — and this reward matches what actually governs generation at inference time (a model generates from its own probabilities; no reference model is present then). SimPO posted strong wins on AlpacaEval 2 and Arena-Hard with less verbosity. The trade: with no reference term at all, nothing in the loss anchors the policy to its starting point, so learning rate and epochs carry the entire burden of not drifting — SimPO is powerful and comparatively easy to overcook. One key idea: score responses the way you will sample them: per token, reference-free.

Rejection sampling and best-of-N — the workhorse nobody blogs about. The least glamorous member of the family may be the most used. Sample NNN responses per prompt from the current model (typically 10–30), score them with a reward model, keep the best (optionally also the worst, as a DPO pair), and run plain SFT on the winners. That is the whole method. It is best-of-N distillation: best-of-N sampling is a strong but expensive policy — NNN forward passes per answer — and rejection-sampling training teaches the model to produce its best-of-NNN answer in one pass. It requires no new math, parallelizes perfectly (generation is offline and embarrassingly parallel), tolerates a mediocre reward model better than PPO does (you only need the argmax over NNN to be decent, not calibrated scores everywhere), and it compounds: Llama 2’s chat models leaned on it, and Llama 3 made it a pillar.

Iterative and online variants. Everything above defaults to offline training: a fixed dataset of pairs, one pass, done. The alternative is to close the loop: train on pairs, sample fresh responses from the improved model, label them (with a reward model, an LLM judge, or humans), and train again. Run at a round granularity this is iterative DPO; run continuously it becomes online DPO, and the closer you push it, the more it resembles RL. The distinction matters because offline DPO’s implicit reward is only trustworthy near its data: once the policy moves, it is being graded on a rubric written for a model that no longer exists. Fresh on-policy pairs re-lay the rails in front of the train. Empirically the gap is consistent and sometimes large — most of the headline results for “DPO-trained” open models of the last two years are actually iterative pipelines.

DPO versus online RL: a 2026 scorecard

So which wins — the cheap shortcut or the heavy machinery? The field has run this experiment at scale for three years now, and the honest answer has more texture than either camp’s blog posts.

InfrastructureOne live model + precomputed ref log-probs; a supervised loopGeneration inside training; 2–4 models; RL plumbing
StabilityHigh — deterministic loss, few knobs (β\betaβ, LR, epochs)Historically touchy; GRPO much improved but still needs care
CostRoughly SFT-like; runs on one GPU with LoRA3–10× more compute per improvement, dominated by sampling
CeilingLimited to reweighting behaviors in / near the dataCan discover new behaviors via exploration; higher ceiling
Reasoning / verifiable tasksWeak fit — no way to exploit checkable rewardsThe clear winner; the engine of the o1/R1 era (Ch. 37)

Failure modeOverfits data biases (length, sycophancy, style tics)Hacks the reward model; needs KL + RM refreshes

The pattern behind the table: DPO-style methods reweight — they move probability among responses the model (or the dataset) already contains, which is exactly what chat alignment needs, since the SFT model already “knows” both the helpful answer and the lazy one. Online RL explores — it can stumble onto a chain-of-thought strategy no dataset contained and reinforce it, which is why the reasoning revolution of 2024–2025 (o1, DeepSeek-R1) ran on online RL with verifiable rewards, not on DPO. When the reward is checkable — the proof verifies, the tests pass — the ceiling argument becomes decisive, and that story gets the next chapter to itself.

⚙️In practice

What labs actually shipped is neither purism. The Llama 3 recipe is the canonical public example of the mixed pipeline: six rounds of (train a reward model on fresh human preferences → rejection-sample the current best model → SFT on the winners → DPO on the pairs), with the DPO modifications noted earlier (reported β=0.1\beta = 0.1β=0.1, formatting-token masking, NLL anchor on chosen). Note what the reward model is doing there: not driving PPO, but curating data — the left and right branches of the family tree welded together. The Qwen2.5 report describes the same architecture with the RL half modernized: an offline DPO stage on curated/verified pairs, then an online stage using group-relative policy optimization (GRPO). Allen AI’s Tülu 3 pipeline — the most fully documented open recipe — runs SFT → DPO → RL with verifiable rewards. Frontier closed labs publish less, but their disclosed pipelines (Anthropic’s lineage of RLHF and reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF), OpenAI’s RLHF-plus-reasoning-RL) are all online-RL-centric with preference-tuned foundations. The 2026 rule of thumb: DPO variants own the cheap, stable, style-and-safety middle of post-training; online RL owns the frontier of capability; serious pipelines run both, usually in that order.

✎Note

A fair reading of the scoreboard also credits DPO with a sociological result: it made preference tuning accessible. Before DPO, aligning a model on preferences required RL expertise few teams had; after, it required a GPU and an afternoon with TRL. Thousands of fine-tuned open models — nearly every community model card that mentions “DPO” or its cousins — exist because the barrier dropped. The cheap shortcut did not replace the heavy machinery at the frontier, but it democratized the second-most-important stage of modern training. Both things are true.

Where this leaves us

  • The RLHF objective — expected reward minus β×\beta \timesβ× KL-to-reference — has an exact closed-form optimum: the reference policy exponentially tilted by reward, π∗∝πref er/β\pi^* \propto \pi_{\text{ref}} \, e^{r/\beta}π∗∝πref​er/β, normalized by a partition function Z(x)Z(x)Z(x) that sums over all ∼101000\sim 10^{1000}∼101000 possible responses and is hopelessly intractable.
  • DPO’s move: invert the closed form to write the reward as βlog⁡(π∗/πref)+βlog⁡Z(x)\beta \log(\pi^*/\pi_{\text{ref}}) + \beta \log Z(x)βlog(π∗/πref​)+βlogZ(x), substitute into the Bradley–Terry preference likelihood, and watch Z(x)Z(x)Z(x) cancel in the difference of rewards — leaving a supervised classification loss on the policy itself.
  • You can read the DPO gradient: raise the chosen response, lower the rejected, weighted per pair by σ(r^l−r^w)\sigma(\hat{r}_l - \hat{r}_w)σ(r^l​−r^w​) — the probability the implicit reward model assigns to the wrong ranking — so training auto-focuses on its own worst mistakes, with β\betaβ setting the leash length.
  • You know DPO’s pathologies by name: margin-chasing overfitting, the both-log-probs-fall dynamic, and length exploitation — and the fixes: one epoch, low learning rate, NLL anchoring, length control, on-policy pairs.
  • You can place the family: IPO (bounded margin), KTO (unpaired thumbs, prospect theory), ORPO (reference-free, single stage), SimPO (length-normalized, reference-free), rejection sampling (best-of-N distilled into SFT), and the offline-versus-online axis that governs all of them.
  • You can defend the 2026 verdict: DPO variants for cheap, stable preference shaping; online RL for the capability ceiling; production pipelines — Llama 3, Qwen, Tülu 3 — interleave both.

The scorecard kept deferring to one claim: that online RL with verifiable rewards unlocked a qualitatively new capability — models that reason. How that works is next: Reasoning Training: RLVR, GRPO, and the o1/R1 Era.
