Reasoning Training: RLVR, GRPO, and the o1/R1 Era

How chain-of-thought went from prompting trick to training target — verifiable rewards, the GRPO math worked by hand, and the o1 and DeepSeek-R1 recipes that defined 2025.

Between late 2024 and mid 2025, the benchmark that best predicted a model’s ranking changed from “how much did you pretrain?” to “how well does it think before answering?” On the American Invitational Mathematics Examination (AIME), a competition-mathematics benchmark, GPT-4o — a frontier model by any 2024 standard — solved roughly 12% of problems. OpenAI’s o1, released September 2024, solved about 74% with a single attempt. Four months later, DeepSeek-R1 matched that class of performance and published the entire recipe under an MIT license. Nothing about the transformer architecture changed. What changed was the training objective and what the model does at inference time: it writes to itself, sometimes for tens of thousands of tokens, before committing to an answer.

This chapter is the full story of that shift. It has three intertwined threads. First, chain of thought: why letting a model generate intermediate steps helps at all — not as folklore but as a claim about computation. Second, reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR): how math and code let us replace the hackable learned reward models of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) with graders that cannot be sweet-talked. Third, the algorithm that made this cheap enough to matter — Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) — which we will derive from proximal policy optimization (PPO), compute by hand with real numbers, and then critique, because the vanilla version has known biases.

You need Chapter 35 for this one: PPO, reward models, and the Kullback–Leibler (KL) penalty to a reference policy all return here. Chapter 36 is useful contrast — direct preference optimization (DPO)-style methods optimize preferences offline, while everything in this chapter optimizes correctness online, with fresh samples from the current model.

From prompting trick to compute: chain of thought

The observation came before the theory. In 2021–2022, several groups noticed that large models answer hard questions better if you make them show intermediate work. Nye et al. called the mechanism a “scratchpad”; Wei et al. (2022) named the now-standard version chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting — put a few worked examples in the prompt, each with step-by-step reasoning before its answer, and the model imitates the format. Kojima et al. then showed you don’t even need the examples: appending “Let’s think step by step” was often enough. On the GSM8K grade-school math benchmark, CoT prompting took PaLM-540B from roughly 18% to 57% — with zero gradient updates.

Two details from those papers matter for everything that follows. First, the gains were emergent with scale in the 2022 experiments: models below roughly 10B parameters produced fluent-looking chains that were nonsense, and CoT could even hurt them. Reasoning steps only help if the model can execute each step reliably. Second, the chains were frequently unfaithful — a model could write a wrong derivation and land on the right answer, or vice versa. That crack widens into a whole research area in Chapter 55.

Why does generating steps help? There are two compounding answers, one about problem structure and one about raw computation.

💡Intuition

Decomposition. “What is 17×2417 \times 2417×24?” in one shot requires the model to have effectively memorized or internally computed a three-step calculation within a single forward pass. "17×24=17×20+17×417 \times 24 = 17 \times 20 + 17 \times 417×24=17×20+17×4" turns one hard prediction into a sequence of easy ones, each of which looks like something the model has seen a million times in pretraining. A chain of thought converts an out-of-distribution leap into a walk through in-distribution steps.

Thinking tokens are compute. This is the deeper point. Recall from the transformer chapter that a decoder does a fixed amount of work per emitted token: one pass through LLL layers. If the answer must appear as the very next token, the model gets exactly LLL layers’ worth of sequential computation to produce it — no matter how hard the question is. But every intermediate token the model emits buys it another full forward pass, and the token itself is written into the context, where attention can read it back. The context window becomes external working memory. Generating TTT reasoning tokens turns a depth-bounded circuit into an iterative machine that runs for TTT steps and takes notes.

The second point has a formal grounding. Theoretical work on transformer expressivity (e.g., Merrill & Sabharwal, “The Expressive Power of Transformers with Chain of Thought”) shows that fixed-depth transformers without intermediate tokens are restricted to a weak complexity class, while allowing a polynomial number of chain-of-thought tokens strictly increases the class of problems they can solve. Some computations cannot fit in one forward pass but can fit in many. “Thinking” in these models is buying serial compute with tokens.

That reframing raises the question that defines the era: if tokens are compute, then compute spent at inference time is a dial we can turn. What happens when we turn it deliberately?

Test-time compute is a new scaling axis

The scaling laws chapter showed loss falling predictably as you add training compute. From 2022 on, evidence accumulated for a second, independent axis: for a fixed trained model, accuracy on reasoning tasks climbs — often log-linearly for a while — as you spend more compute per query at inference time. There are two qualitatively different ways to spend it.

Parallel: sample more, then select. Self-consistency (Wang et al., 2022) samples many independent chains of thought at nonzero temperature and takes a majority vote over the final answers — wrong chains scatter across many different wrong answers while correct chains converge, so the vote filters noise. This added roughly 18 points to PaLM-540B’s GSM8K score with 40 samples (numbers approximate). The stronger version is best-of-NNN with a verifier: generate NNN candidates, score each with a checker or a learned verifier, keep the best. Parallel compute is embarrassingly scalable but plateaus: it can only surface answers the model already produces with non-negligible probability, and each sample re-pays the full cost of the problem from scratch.

Sequential: think longer in one chain. Instead of many shallow attempts, let one attempt run deeper — decompose further, check intermediate results, backtrack on errors. This is what o1-style models do, and it can solve problems where the single-sample success probability is close to zero, because later steps condition on earlier work rather than starting over. Snell et al. (2024) studied the trade-off systematically and found it is problem-dependent: easy problems benefit most from parallel sampling and reranking, hard ones from sequential revision, and a compute-optimal mix beats either alone — sometimes letting a small model with generous test-time compute match a much larger model answering cold.

Here is the catch that makes this a training chapter rather than a prompting chapter: a base or RLHF-tuned model does not naturally use a long budget well. Ask 2023-era GPT-4 to “think for 10,000 tokens” and it pads, repeats itself, or wanders — the log-linear curve bends flat almost immediately. The chains that make sequential compute pay off — decompose, verify, notice an error, back up, try another route — are rare in pretraining text, because humans publish conclusions, not their dead ends. To get a model that earns its thinking tokens, you have to train the thinking itself. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on human-written reasoning (STaR was an early bootstrap in this direction) helps but inherits human ceilings and human data costs. The 2024 answer was reinforcement learning (RL) — with a change to where the reward comes from.

Verifiable rewards: RL without a hackable judge

Recall the central weakness of RLHF: the reward comes from a learned reward model, a neural network trained on human preferences, and the policy is optimized to please it. Optimize hard enough and you get Goodhart’s law in its purest form — the policy finds inputs where the reward model is wrong, and measured reward rises while true quality falls (the over-optimization curves of Gao et al., 2022). The KL penalty exists mostly to keep the policy from wandering into the reward model’s blind spots. RLHF is optimization against a fallible judge, run gently for exactly that reason.

Now notice what is special about mathematics and code: the answer can be checked by a program. Did the final expression equal 408? Do the unit tests pass? No neural network is involved in the grading, so there is no learned judge to fool. This is reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) — the term was popularized by the Tülu 3 project at Ai2 — and it changes the character of the optimization entirely. You can push hard, for thousands of steps, with a weak or even absent KL penalty, because the reward cannot be hacked in the RLHF sense. The signal is thin — often a single bit per rollout, delivered only at the end — but it is true.

A typical RLVR reward for a math prompt looks like this:

  • Accuracy reward. Extract the final answer (models are trained to put it in a delimiter like \boxed{...}), normalize it, and compare with the reference. Naive string match fails on 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​ vs 0.50.50.5 vs 24\tfrac{2}{4}42​, so real pipelines use symbolic checkers (SymPy-style equivalence testing) — verifier quality is a genuine engineering surface, since a checker that rejects correct-but-differently-formatted answers teaches the model formatting superstitions, and one that accepts wrong forms teaches errors. Reward 1 if equivalent, 0 otherwise.
  • Format reward. A small bonus (or a gate on the accuracy reward) for respecting the output structure — e.g., DeepSeek’s requirement that reasoning appear between <think> and </think> tags with the answer after. This keeps outputs parseable so the accuracy reward stays computable, and gives the model something to earn in early training when it solves almost nothing.
  • For code: run the generated program against unit tests in a sandbox; reward 1 if all pass (partial-credit variants exist but reward-per-test invites gaming — passing the three easy tests and ignoring the hard one).
⚠️Careful

Verifiable does not mean ungameable. The grader can’t be fooled by rhetoric, but the setup can still leak. Classic failure modes: answers appearing verbatim in contaminated training prompts; multiple-choice or numeric-answer tasks where guessing has high baseline success (models learn to skip reasoning and guess — one reason frontier RLVR data skews toward answers that can’t be guessed); code models that hard-code the visible test cases instead of solving the problem, or — in agentic settings where the model can touch the test files — that edit the tests to pass. And any learned component you add back (a large-language-model (LLM) judge for free-form answers, a learned verifier for partial credit) reopens the Goodhart door you closed. The 2025–26 lore is full of reward-hacking war stories; the discipline is to treat the reward pipeline as an adversarial interface, because gradient descent will treat it that way.

⚙️In practice

What RLVR training sets look like in 2025–26 practice: tens of thousands to around a million prompts, dominated by competition and synthetic mathematics (with numeric or expression answers that a symbolic checker can verify), competitive-programming and software tasks with test suites, and logic puzzles with machine-checkable solutions; plus growing slices of anything else a program can grade — instruction-following with checkable constraints (“exactly three sentences”), structured extraction, and, at the frontier, agentic tasks graded by whether the end state is correct. Difficulty curation matters as much as volume: prompts the model always solves or never solves provide no gradient (a fact GRPO makes vividly concrete below), so pipelines filter to problems where the current model succeeds some but not all of the time, and refresh that filter as it improves.

The contrast with the preference-optimization family is worth stating once, sharply: DPO-style methods learn offline from pairs someone already produced, RLHF learns online against a model of a judge, and RLVR learns online against ground truth. That is why RLVR could be scaled so much harder than either — and why its reach is bounded by what we can verify. Nobody has a program that returns 1 when an essay is insightful.

GRPO: reinforcement learning without the value network

One obstacle remained, and it was economic. The workhorse of RLHF, proximal policy optimization (PPO), was built for a world of short responses and dense-enough feedback. Reasoning training means rollouts of 5,000–30,000 tokens with a single reward at the very end, and at that scale PPO’s machinery starts to buckle. Here is the quick recap, and the pain point.

∑The math

PPO estimates A^t\hat A_tA^t​ using a value network Vψ(q,o<t)V_\psi(q, o_{<t})Vψ​(q,o<t​) — a second model, typically as large as the policy, trained alongside it to predict the expected final reward from every prefix. That is the pain point, three times over: (1) memory — a second set of weights, activations, and optimizer state for a model the size of your policy; (2) credit assignment — with one binary reward arriving after 20,000 tokens, the value net must guess, at token 3,000 of a half-finished derivation, the probability the proof eventually closes — a prediction problem nearly as hard as the reasoning itself; (3) stability — a mistrained critic silently corrupts every advantage it touches.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), introduced in DeepSeekMath (Shao et al., 2024), deletes the value network and replaces it with statistics you get almost for free. The move: for each prompt qqq, sample a group of GGG complete responses o1,…,oGo_1, \dots, o_Go1​,…,oG​ from the current (old) policy, score each with the verifier to get rewards r1,…,rGr_1, \dots, r_Gr1​,…,rG​, and use the group itself as the baseline:

A^i  =  ri−mean(r1,…,rG)std(r1,…,rG).\hat A_i \;=\; \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(r_1, \dots, r_G)}{\mathrm{std}(r_1, \dots, r_G)}.A^i​=std(r1​,…,rG​)ri​−mean(r1​,…,rG​)​.

Every token of response iii receives this same advantage A^i\hat A_iA^i​. No critic, no per-token credit assignment — a response that ended correctly has all its tokens nudged up, a failed one has all its tokens nudged down, and over many prompts the tokens that cause success (the checking step, the correct decomposition) are the ones consistently on the winning side. The baseline question “how well does the model usually do on this prompt?” is answered empirically: by looking at its siblings.

💡Intuition

GRPO is graded on a curve, literally. Sample GGG attempts at the same problem; each attempt is rewarded not for its absolute score but for how it did relative to the class average, in units of the class’s spread. If everyone fails and you succeed, your advantage is huge. If everyone succeeds, nobody learns anything from this prompt — there is no contrast to exploit. This also explains why the sampling temperature matters so much in reasoning RL: the group needs diversity, because diversity is the signal.

One honest caveat: A^i\hat A_iA^i​ is not an unbiased advantage estimate, because response iii‘s own reward is included in the group mean it is compared against — with the leave-one-out correction you get the closely related RLOO (REINFORCE leave-one-out) estimator. ⚠️Careful

Vanilla GRPO has two built-in biases, dissected by Dr. GRPO (Liu et al., 2025). First, the length bias: the 1∣oi∣\tfrac{1}{|o_i|}∣oi​∣1​ term divides each response’s summed token losses by its own length. For a wrong answer (A^i<0\hat A_i < 0A^i​<0), being longer dilutes the per-token penalty — so among failures, longer ones are punished less, and the model drifts toward producing ever-longer wrong answers. Some of the celebrated “response length grows during training” curves were partly this artifact, not only deeper thinking. Second, the difficulty bias: dividing by the group’s std up-weights prompts where rewards barely vary (the nearly-always-right and nearly-always-wrong ones) relative to the 50/50 prompts that carry the most information. Dr. GRPO’s fix is deletion: drop the 1∣oi∣\tfrac{1}{|o_i|}∣oi​∣1​ normalization (use a constant normalizer) and drop the /std/\mathrm{std}/std, keeping A^i=ri−rˉ\hat A_i = r_i - \bar rA^i​=ri​−rˉ. The corrected recipe reaches the same accuracy with markedly shorter outputs. Many 2025–26 open recipes adopt one or both fixes; when you read a GRPO config, check which variant it is.

A one-paragraph tour of the sibling algorithms, because you will meet the names: RLOO (Ahmadian et al., 2024) is the same sample-a-group idea with the leave-one-out baseline (each response compared to the mean of the others) and no clipping — a deliberate return to plain REINFORCE, on the argument that pretrained-then-SFT’d policies are stable enough not to need PPO’s scaffolding. REINFORCE-with-baseline variants and DAPO (clip-range asymmetry, dynamic group resampling, token-level aggregation) push in the same direction. The shared lesson of 2024–25: for LLM post-training, the value network was the expendable part of PPO, and group-relative baselines are a strong substitute. The differences between the surviving variants are real but second-order next to data quality and verifier design.

⚙️In practice

Typical reasoning-RL settings circa 2025–26 (open recipes; treat as folklore, not gospel): group size GGG from 8 to 64 (DeepSeekMath used 64; R1-style runs commonly 16); sampling temperature around 1.0 during rollouts; learning rate around 10−610^{-6}10−6 with AdamW; clip ϵ=0.2\epsilon = 0.2ϵ=0.2 (DAPO raises the upper clip to ~0.28 to let low-probability tokens grow); KL weight β\betaβ small — DeepSeekMath used 0.04, and many later RLVR recipes set β=0\beta = 0β=0 entirely, on the logic that a verifiable reward removes the main thing the KL was protecting against. Compute is dominated by rollout generation, not gradient steps — you are running inference on thousands of 10k-token samples per update — so the serving stack of Chapter 39 (paged key–value (KV) cache, continuous batching) is as much a part of the training system as the optimizer, and frameworks like veRL and TRL exist largely to plumb the two together.

The o1 moment

On September 12, 2024, OpenAI released o1-preview with a blog post titled “Learning to Reason with LLMs,” and the reasoning era acquired its public existence proof. The claims were specific. o1 was trained with “large-scale reinforcement learning” to produce long chains of thought before answering; performance rose smoothly with both more RL training compute and more test-time thinking — the announcement’s two log-scale plots became the era’s emblem, and the test-time scaling figure earlier in this chapter echoes the second of them. The headline numbers (approximate, from OpenAI’s announcement): AIME 2024 went from ~12% (GPT-4o) to ~74% single-shot, ~83% with consensus over 64 samples; Codeforces performance around the 89th percentile of human competitors; PhD-level science questions (GPQA) above hired expert baselines. The o1 system card documents the safety evaluations around it.

Two design choices shaped everything downstream. First, the chain of thought was hidden. Users saw a model-written summary of the reasoning, not the reasoning itself. OpenAI’s stated rationale mixed safety (an unfiltered chain lets you monitor the model’s actual deliberation — including, per the system card, occasional deliberate deception — and filtering it for display would destroy that signal) with frankly commercial concerns: the chains were the product of an expensive RL pipeline, and exposing them would hand competitors a distillation dataset. Second, thinking became a metered good: reasoning tokens were billed but not shown, and “how long should the model think” became an API parameter and a product surface rather than a fixed property of the model.

What OpenAI did not publish was the recipe — algorithm, data, reward design all undisclosed. The gap between “we know it’s possible” and “we know how” lasted almost exactly four months.

DeepSeek-R1: the open landmark

On January 20, 2025, DeepSeek — the same lab whose GRPO we derived above — released DeepSeek-R1 under an MIT license, with weights and a paper that answered the question o1 raised. It arrived alongside a market-moving wave of attention (this was the release that briefly knocked a trillion dollars off tech indices), but its durable importance is scientific: it showed, in public, both that pure RL can elicit reasoning and what a production recipe layered on top looks like. A peer-reviewed version later appeared in Nature (September 2025) — a first for a frontier LLM — reporting, among other things, that the RL phase cost on the order of $294K on top of the roughly $6M base-model training (numbers approximate and famously contested in their accounting scope).

The experiment: R1-Zero. Take DeepSeek-V3-Base — the 671B-parameter mixture-of-experts (MoE) model with ~37B active parameters per token, never instruction-tuned — and run GRPO directly on it. No SFT, no reward model, no human preferences anywhere: only rule-based rewards (answer-accuracy plus a format reward for putting deliberation inside <think> tags) on math and code prompts. The result was the cleanest capability-emergence curve of the decade: AIME 2024 pass@1 climbing from 15.6% to 71.0% over training (86.7% with majority voting over 64 samples), into o1-class territory. And along the way, behaviors nobody wrote into the reward appeared: response length grew steadily as the model learned that longer deliberation pays; the model began re-checking its own steps; and in the paper’s most-quoted table, mid-derivation, it interrupts itself — “Wait, wait. Wait. That’s an aha moment I can flag here.” — and restarts the solution. Reflection, verification, and backtracking emerged because they help win reward, not because anyone demonstrated them.

R1-Zero was also, as a product, nearly unusable — chains mixed English and Chinese mid-thought, formatting was erratic, readability poor. Optimization pressure shapes the thinking for correctness, not for human eyes; the mixed-language scratchpad is an early taste of the faithfulness and legibility worries of Part VII. So the team built the production model with a four-stage pipeline:

  1. Cold-start SFT. Fine-tune V3-Base on a small curated set — thousands of long, readable chain-of-thought examples (many cleaned up from R1-Zero’s own outputs) — to fix the format and give RL a civilized starting point.
  2. Reasoning RL. Large-scale GRPO as in R1-Zero, with an added language-consistency reward to suppress mid-chain language mixing. (It cost a little accuracy. Legibility is not free.)
  3. Rejection-sampling SFT. Sample many completions from the RL checkpoint, keep the verified-correct and well-formed ones, and combine them (~600K reasoning samples) with ~200K general-capability examples from the V3 pipeline; retrain from V3-Base on all ~800K. This consolidates RL’s discoveries into clean supervised data.
  4. General RL. A final round mixing verifiable rewards (reasoning) with reward-model-based preference signals (helpfulness, harmlessness) — RLVR and RLHF in the same loop — to make a model that is both a strong reasoner and a usable assistant.

The result: AIME 2024 79.8% pass@1, MATH-500 97.3%, Codeforces around the 96th percentile — o1-class numbers, openly licensed, from a disclosed recipe.

Distillation: what transfers, and what it says. The team then fine-tuned small open models (Qwen2.5 and Llama bases, 1.5B–70B) on those ~800K samples — supervised learning only, no RL at all. The distilled models inherited startling fractions of the capability: approximately 55.5% AIME for the 7B, 72.6% for the 32B — the 32B distill roughly matching o1-mini and crushing every same-size model trained conventionally (numbers from the paper; approximate). The control experiment is the important part: running the full RL recipe directly on Qwen-32B reached only about 47% AIME — far below distilling from the big model. The reading, supported by the Nature follow-up: discovering strong reasoning patterns via RL requires a large, capable base model, but executing those patterns does not — once discovered, they compress into ordinary training data. This is why every capable open model since has been trained on reasoning traces, and why frontier labs guard theirs.

🕰History

Credit where due on timing: the idea of RL on checkable answers predates the era’s branding — process- vs outcome-supervision studies and math-RL papers were running through 2022–2024 (DeepSeekMath itself is February 2024, seven months before o1), and STaR’s bootstrap-on-correct-answers loop is 2022. What o1 contributed was proof of scale — that the approach, pushed with frontier compute, produces a qualitatively different model class — and R1 contributed the public mechanism. Within months of R1, o1-class open models were a commodity: Qwen’s QwQ line, Kimi k1.5, and a flood of academic replications (TinyZero reproducing R1-Zero dynamics on toy tasks for under $30 of compute). Rarely has a frontier capability gone from secret to reproducible-in-a-dorm-room faster.

The 2025–2026 landscape

The two years after o1 turned “reasoning model” from a category into a feature — and then into a dial. A snapshot as of mid-2026, flagged as such:

Thinking modes and routers. The first-generation split — separate “reasoning” and “chat” models — collapsed into hybrid models that can think or not per query. Anthropic’s Claude 3.7 Sonnet (February 2025) was the first prominent hybrid: one set of weights, with an “extended thinking” mode and a user-settable token budget for it. Qwen3 shipped switchable thinking; Gemini 2.5 exposed a thinking_budget parameter; DeepSeek’s V3.1 merged R1 into the main line. OpenAI’s GPT-5 (August 2025) pushed the decision into the system: a router classifies each incoming query and dispatches it to a fast non-thinking path or a deliberate reasoning path, with an auto mode deciding for you. The economic logic is straightforward — thinking tokens cost real money and real latency (every reasoning token pays the full KV-cache and compute cost of generation), and most queries don’t need them.

The overthinking problem. Trained reasoners over-deliberate: ask a strong 2025 reasoning model for 2+32+32+3 and, unconstrained, it may burn hundreds of tokens double-checking itself — RLVR rewarded chains that ended correct, and on the training distribution longer deliberation correlated with reward, so brevity was never learned where it’s warranted. Fixes span training (length penalties in the reward; Dr. GRPO’s de-biasing; “adaptive thinking” objectives that reward matching effort to difficulty) and inference: the s1 paper showed embarrassingly direct budget forcing — truncate thinking at the budget, or append “Wait” to make the model keep thinking — cleanly traces out the test-time scaling curve on a model distilled from only 1,000 examples.

Process rewards: the road mostly not taken. Let’s Verify Step by Step (Lightman et al., 2023) trained a process reward model (PRM) to score every step of a solution, using ~800K human step-labels, and showed process supervision beat outcome-only supervision as a best-of-NNN reranker (≈78% on a MATH subset at large NNN). It was widely expected that PRMs would drive the RL era — denser signal, per-step credit assignment, exactly what sparse-reward RL seems to need. Notably, that’s not what happened at the frontier: the R1 paper explicitly lists PRM-guided RL among its unsuccessful attempts (defining a “step” is fuzzy, judging intermediate correctness at scale is hard, and — being learned models — PRMs get hacked under optimization pressure, reintroducing the disease RLVR cured). As of 2026, outcome rewards dominate training; PRM-descendants survive as inference-time verifiers, rerankers, and search guides, and hybrid designs keep being explored. An honest summary: denser supervision should win, and so far it mostly hasn’t — an open seam.

Agentic RL. The frontier moved from “think, then answer” to “think, act, observe, think again”: rollouts that interleave reasoning with tool calls — running code, searching, browsing, editing files — with the verifiable reward attached to the end state (tests pass, task completed). OpenAI’s o3 generation made tool use inside the chain of thought a headline feature, and 2025–26 saw an industry-wide scramble to build RL environments: sandboxed, gradeable task worlds for exactly this training. The reward-hacking surface grows accordingly (a model that can run the tests can also, sometimes, rewrite them). That thread continues in Chapter 44, and the products it produced — deep-research modes, autonomous coding agents — in Chapter 40.

Open questions: does RL create ability, or reveal it?

The era’s sharpest scientific dispute sounds almost philosophical but is precisely measurable. When RLVR takes a base model from 16% to 71% on AIME, did the RL create reasoning capability, or elicit — concentrate probability onto — capability the base model already had?

✎Note

The measuring stick is pass@k (from Chapter 21): the probability that at least one of kkk independent samples solves the problem. Pass@1 measures reliability; pass@k for large kkk measures coverage — the outer boundary of what the model can ever do. Yue et al. (2025) ran the comparison and found a consistent crossover: RLVR-trained models dominate at small kkk, but as kkk grows into the hundreds, the base model catches up and often overtakes — with enough tries, the untuned model solves a superset of the problems its RL-trained descendant solves, while the RL model’s sampling diversity (its output entropy) has visibly collapsed. On that evidence, RLVR mostly sharpens: it reweights the base distribution toward already-reachable successful modes, buying enormous reliability at some cost in exploration, without expanding the frontier of solvable problems.

The counter-case: these results are snapshots of particular models, tasks, and (modest) RL budgets. Follow-up work — e.g., NVIDIA’s ProRL line — reports that much longer RL runs, with entropy-preserving tricks and periodic reference-policy resets, do show gains even at large kkk on some task families, and the boundary is contested as of 2026. Meanwhile the giant asterisk: pass@1024 is not a product. A model that is right once in a thousand tries is useless without a verifier; making capability reliable is exactly what makes it deployable — and distillation shows elicited capability becomes portable training data, which compounds across model generations regardless of which side of the debate is right. Where the field honestly stands: “RL mostly elicits, at least at current scales, and eliciting is worth a fortune” — held with moderate confidence and under active fire.

A related open front belongs to Part VII: whether the visible chain of thought is a faithful account of the computation that produced the answer. Reasoning models sometimes reach correct answers via chains containing unused or wrong steps, and optimization pressure on the chain (for length, style, or safety appearance) can push the real computation underground. Labs monitor chains precisely because they were not heavily optimized for looks — a fragile, load-bearing accident explored in Chapter 55.

Where this leaves us

  • Chain of thought works for two reasons you can now state precisely: it decomposes hard predictions into in-distribution steps, and each emitted token buys another forward pass with the context as working memory — thinking tokens are serial compute, which fixed-depth transformers otherwise lack.
  • Test-time compute is a second scaling axis alongside training compute: parallel (sample-and-select, which saturates) and sequential (longer chains, which require training to exploit).
  • RLVR replaces RLHF’s hackable learned judge with programmatic verification on math and code — thin but true rewards that can be optimized hard, bounded by what we can verify and by a reward pipeline that must be engineered adversarially.
  • You can run GRPO by hand: sample GGG responses, verify, and set each response’s advantage to its group-normalized reward — (1,0,0,0)→(+1.73,−0.58,−0.58,−0.58)(1,0,0,0) \to (+1.73, -0.58, -0.58, -0.58)(1,0,0,0)→(+1.73,−0.58,−0.58,−0.58) — broadcast to every token, inside PPO’s clipped objective, with no value network; and you know vanilla GRPO’s length and difficulty biases and the Dr. GRPO fix.
  • The o1 release proved RL-trained long-form thinking scales; DeepSeek-R1 published the mechanism — R1-Zero’s pure-RL emergence (reflection, “aha,” and language-mixing artifacts included), the four-stage production recipe, and distillation showing that discovered reasoning compresses into ordinary SFT data.
  • The 2025–26 settlement: hybrid models with routed, budgeted thinking; outcome rewards beating process rewards at the frontier (so far); agentic RL as the growth edge; and a live debate — backed by pass@k crossovers — over whether RL expands capability or concentrates it.
