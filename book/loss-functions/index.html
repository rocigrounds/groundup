Loss Functions

Why the loss is the only thing a model "wants", where MSE and cross-entropy come from, and the softmax gradient — predicted minus target — derived in full.

A neural network has no goals, no preferences, no idea what you built it for. It is a pile of numbers arranged into layers, and left alone it will happily map inputs to garbage forever. Everything the finished model appears to “want” — to predict the next token, to classify the image, to be helpful — was installed by exactly one mechanism: a loss function, a single number that measures how wrong the model currently is, which training then relentlessly pushes down.

This is worth saying bluntly, because it is the most load-bearing fact in deep learning: the loss is the only thing the model optimizes. Not accuracy. Not truthfulness. Not what you meant. If your loss rewards confident nonsense, you will get confident nonsense, delivered with machine efficiency. Every behavior of every frontier model — the good, the bad, and the weird — traces back to some loss (pretraining cross-entropy, a reward model’s score, a preference objective) that made that behavior score well. When people say a model was “trained to predict the next token,” they are naming its loss function.

This chapter builds the two losses that carry most of deep learning: mean squared error for predicting numbers, and cross-entropy for predicting categories — which includes predicting the next token, so cross-entropy is the loss that trained every large language model (LLM) you have ever used. Neither is arbitrary. Both fall out of a single principle (maximum likelihood), and we will derive both from it. Along the way we will reach a key result — the gradient of softmax plus cross-entropy is predicted minus target — and we will look honestly at what the loss surface over a billion parameters actually looks like, as preparation for descending it in the next chapter.

What makes a loss function good

Formally, a loss function takes the model’s prediction y^\hat{y}y^​ (read “y-hat”) and the true answer yyy, and returns a non-negative number ℓ(y^,y)\ell(\hat{y}, y)ℓ(y^​,y): zero-ish when the prediction is right, large when it is wrong. The training loss is the average of this per-example loss over the dataset, viewed as a function of the model’s parameters θ\thetaθ (theta — the collective name for every weight and bias):

L(θ)  =  1N∑n=1Nℓ(fθ(xn),  yn),L(\theta) \;=\; \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \ell\big(f_\theta(x_n),\; y_n\big),L(θ)=N1​n=1∑N​ℓ(fθ​(xn​),yn​),

where fθf_\thetafθ​ is the network, xnx_nxn​ is the nnn-th input, and NNN is the number of training examples. Training means finding θ\thetaθ that makes L(θ)L(\theta)L(θ) small. That’s the whole game.

💡Intuition

The loss is the model’s entire value system, compressed into one number. During training, the model has no access to your intentions, your documentation, or the task description — only to the gradient of this number. A useful habit for the rest of the book: whenever a model does something surprising, ask “what loss was it trained on, and did that behavior lower it?” The answer is almost always yes, and the surprise usually dissolves.

Not every function of (y^,y)(\hat{y}, y)(y^​,y) makes a workable loss. Three requirements matter in practice, and they explain most of the design choices in this chapter:

It must be differentiable — or at least differentiable almost everywhere. The entire training procedure of the next chapter works by computing ∇θL\nabla_\theta L∇θ​L, the gradient of the loss with respect to every parameter, and stepping downhill. This is why we cannot train directly on accuracy: accuracy is a count of right answers, a staircase function whose derivative is zero everywhere it exists. Nudge a weight by a tiny amount and the accuracy almost never changes, so the gradient carries no information about which direction is better. A good loss is a smooth proxy for the thing you actually care about — squared distance instead of “close enough,” cross-entropy instead of “got it right.”

It must be aligned with the task. The proxy has to point the same direction as the goal. Lower loss should reliably mean better performance on the thing you wanted, at least in the regime you care about. Misalignment between the loss and the intent is not an exotic failure — it is the default, and much of Parts V and VII of this book (reward hacking in RLHF, models optimizing the letter of the objective) is about what happens when a powerful optimizer meets a slightly-wrong loss.

Its gradients must be well-scaled. The loss should produce a big gradient when the model is very wrong and a small one when it is nearly right — so that learning is fast where it matters and gentle near the answer. A loss whose gradient goes to zero while the model is still badly wrong is said to saturate, and a saturating loss can freeze learning entirely. We will see a famous example shortly: squared error applied to classification. This requirement also has an upper side — losses whose gradients explode cause the stability problems of Chapter 12.

One more property is quietly essential at scale: the loss must be cheap, because it is evaluated on every example of every batch of every one of the millions of steps in a training run.

Mean squared error: a Gaussian in disguise

Suppose the model predicts a single number — tomorrow’s temperature, a house price, a bounding-box coordinate. The classic loss is mean squared error (MSE): the average of the squared difference between truth and prediction,

LMSE  =  1N∑n=1N(yn−y^n)2.L_{\text{MSE}} \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \big(y_n - \hat{y}_n\big)^2 .LMSE​=N1​n=1∑N​(yn​−y^​n​)2.

Squaring does three jobs at once: it makes every error positive (an error of −3-3−3 is as bad as +3+3+3), it is smooth everywhere (unlike the absolute value, which has a kink at zero), and it punishes large errors disproportionately — an error of 4 costs sixteen times an error of 1, not four times.

∑The math

The gradient with respect to one prediction, by the chain rule from Chapter 5: let u=y−y^u = y - \hat{y}u=y−y^​, so the per-example loss is u2u^2u2. Then

∂ℓ∂y^=d(u2)du⋅∂u∂y^=2u⋅(−1)=−2 (y−y^).\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \hat{y}} = \frac{d(u^2)}{du}\cdot\frac{\partial u}{\partial \hat{y}} = 2u \cdot (-1) = -2\,(y - \hat{y}).∂y^​∂ℓ​=dud(u2)​⋅∂y^​∂u​=2u⋅(−1)=−2(y−y^​).

Read it as an instruction: the gradient is proportional to the error itself. If the prediction is 5 units too low, the loss tells the network to raise it — with a signal 5 times stronger than if it were 1 unit too low. No saturation: the more wrong you are, the harder you are pushed. (The factor of 2 is a harmless constant; many texts define the loss as 12(y−y^)2\tfrac{1}{2}(y-\hat{y})^221​(y−y^​)2 purely so the gradient comes out as the tidy y^−y\hat{y}-yy^​−y.)

✏️Worked example

The model predicts y^=3.0\hat{y} = 3.0y^​=3.0; the truth is y=5.0y = 5.0y=5.0. The loss is (5.0−3.0)2=4.0(5.0 - 3.0)^2 = 4.0(5.0−3.0)2=4.0, and the gradient is −2(5.0−3.0)=−4.0-2(5.0 - 3.0) = -4.0−2(5.0−3.0)=−4.0. A negative gradient means “increasing y^\hat{y}y^​ decreases the loss,” so gradient descent will push the prediction up — toward 5. If instead y^=4.9\hat{y} = 4.9y^​=4.9, the loss is 0.010.010.01 and the gradient is −0.2-0.2−0.2: same direction, twenty times gentler. The correction is always proportional to the miss.

So far MSE looks like a sensible arbitrary choice. It is better than that: it is the mathematically forced choice under one specific assumption about the world. This is the pattern that will repeat for cross-entropy, so it is worth walking slowly.

∑The math

MSE is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise. Assume the data was generated as “true function plus bell-curve noise”: y=fθ(x)+εy = f_\theta(x) + \varepsilony=fθ​(x)+ε, with ε∼N(0,σ2)\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)ε∼N(0,σ2) — Gaussian noise with mean 0 and variance σ2\sigma^2σ2, as in Chapter 6. Then the probability density of observing yyy given xxx is a Gaussian centered on the model’s prediction y^=fθ(x)\hat{y} = f_\theta(x)y^​=fθ​(x):

p(y∣x,θ)  =  12πσ2exp⁡ ⁣(−(y−y^)22σ2).p(y \mid x, \theta) \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(y - \hat{y})^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).p(y∣x,θ)=2πσ2​1​exp(−2σ2(y−y^​)2​).

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) says: choose θ\thetaθ to maximize the probability of the data you actually saw. Products of many small probabilities are numerically miserable, so we maximize the log-likelihood instead (the log is monotonic, so the maximizer is unchanged). Take the log of the density — the log of a product is a sum of logs, and log⁡exp⁡(a)=a\log \exp(a) = alogexp(a)=a:

log⁡p(y∣x,θ)  =  −(y−y^)22σ2  −  12log⁡(2πσ2).\log p(y \mid x, \theta) \;=\; -\frac{(y - \hat{y})^2}{2\sigma^2} \;-\; \tfrac{1}{2}\log\big(2\pi\sigma^2\big).logp(y∣x,θ)=−2σ2(y−y^​)2​−21​log(2πσ2).

Sum over all NNN independent examples, flip the sign to turn “maximize likelihood” into “minimize negative log-likelihood”:

−log⁡p(data∣θ)  =  12σ2∑n=1N(yn−y^n)2  +  const.-\log p(\text{data} \mid \theta) \;=\; \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \big(y_n - \hat{y}_n\big)^2 \;+\; \text{const}.−logp(data∣θ)=2σ21​n=1∑N​(yn​−y^​n​)2+const.

The constant does not depend on θ\thetaθ, and 12σ2\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}2σ21​ is a positive scale factor that does not change where the minimum is. What remains is exactly the sum of squared errors. Minimizing MSE is maximizing the likelihood of your data under the assumption that errors are Gaussian.

This derivation is a two-way street. It tells you when to trust MSE — when the “true value plus roughly symmetric, thin-tailed noise” story is plausible — and when to distrust it. If your targets have heavy-tailed outliers, the squared penalty lets a few wild points dominate the entire loss (practitioners reach for the absolute-error loss or its smooth blend, the Huber loss, precisely to soften this). And if your target is not a number at all but a category, the Gaussian story collapses completely. Which brings us to the main event.

Why squared error fails for classification

Suppose the task is now a yes/no question — “is the next token the?”, “is this email spam?” — and the model outputs a probability p^\hat{p}p^​ via a sigmoid squashing function, p^=σ(z)=1/(1+e−z)\hat{p} = \sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})p^​=σ(z)=1/(1+e−z), applied to a raw score zzz (a logit). Nothing stops you from bolting MSE on top: loss =(y−p^)2= (y - \hat{p})^2=(y−p^​)2 with y∈{0,1}y \in \{0, 1\}y∈{0,1}. It is differentiable. It is zero at the right answer. It is also a trap, for two reasons — one about scale, one fatal.

The scale problem: MSE’s penalty is capped. If the true label is 1 and the model says p^=0.001\hat{p} = 0.001p^​=0.001 — as wrong as it is possible to be — the loss is (1−0.001)2≈0.998(1 - 0.001)^2 \approx 0.998(1−0.001)2≈0.998. Barely worse than predicting 0.10.10.1 (loss 0.810.810.81). A loss that treats “quite wrong” and “catastrophically, confidently wrong” as nearly the same is not exerting the pressure we want on exactly the predictions that most need fixing.

The fatal problem is saturation of the gradient. Follow the chain rule through the sigmoid:

∂ℓ∂z=∂ℓ∂p^⋅∂p^∂z=−2 (y−p^)  ⋅  σ(z)(1−σ(z)),\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \hat p} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat p}{\partial z} = -2\,(y - \hat p)\;\cdot\;\sigma(z)\big(1 - \sigma(z)\big),∂z∂ℓ​=∂p^​∂ℓ​⋅∂z∂p^​​=−2(y−p^​)⋅σ(z)(1−σ(z)),

using the sigmoid’s derivative σ′(z)=σ(z)(1−σ(z))\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))σ′(z)=σ(z)(1−σ(z)). Look at that second factor. When the model is confidently wrong — say z=−5z = -5z=−5 when the true label is 1 — we get p^=σ(−5)≈0.0067\hat p = \sigma(-5) \approx 0.0067p^​=σ(−5)≈0.0067, and σ′(−5)≈0.0067×0.9933≈0.0066\sigma'(-5) \approx 0.0067 \times 0.9933 \approx 0.0066σ′(−5)≈0.0067×0.9933≈0.0066. The full gradient is about −2×0.993×0.0066≈−0.013-2 \times 0.993 \times 0.0066 \approx -0.013−2×0.993×0.0066≈−0.013. At z=−10z = -10z=−10 it is around −0.00009-0.00009−0.00009. The error factor (y−p^)(y - \hat p)(y−p^​) is at its maximum, screaming that the prediction is maximally wrong — and the sigmoid’s derivative multiplies that scream by nearly zero. The more confidently wrong the model is, the flatter the loss becomes and the less it learns. A badly initialized network can sit in this frozen state indefinitely.

⚠️Careful

This is the general shape of a recurring villain. Any time a loss’s gradient must pass through a squashing function’s derivative, check what happens in the tails. “Gradient →\to→ 0 while error is large” is the signature of a saturated loss, and variants of this bug — vanishing gradients through deep networks, dead units in activation functions — will reappear throughout Part II. The fix here is not to tweak MSE but to replace it with a loss whose shape cancels the saturation, which is exactly what cross-entropy does.

What shape do we want? When the model assigns probability p^\hat pp^​ to the correct class, the penalty should grow without bound as p^→0\hat p \to 0p^​→0: confident wrongness should be unboundedly expensive. There is a function from Chapter 7 with exactly this profile — surprise, −log⁡p^-\log \hat p−logp^​.

Cross-entropy and the cleanest gradient in deep learning

For classification over KKK classes, the network’s last layer produces a score ziz_izi​ (a logit) for each class iii, and softmax — met in Chapter 0 and studied in Chapter 6 — converts them into a probability distribution:

pi  =  ezi∑j=1Kezj.p_i \;=\; \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{z_j}}.pi​=∑j=1K​ezj​ezi​​.

The cross-entropy (CE) loss charges the model the surprise of the correct answer under its own predicted distribution. If the true class is ccc:

ℓCE  =  −log⁡pc.\ell_{\text{CE}} \;=\; -\log p_c .ℓCE​=−logpc​.

That is the whole loss: the negative log of the probability the model gave to what actually happened. Assign the truth probability 1 and pay nothing; assign it 0.5 and pay log⁡2≈0.693\log 2 \approx 0.693log2≈0.693 nats; assign it 0.01 and pay 4.64.64.6 nats. (A nat is the unit of surprise when using the natural log; one nat ≈1.44\approx 1.44≈1.44 bits.)

This is the same maximum-likelihood move as before, wearing different clothes. The model outputs a distribution ppp; the probability of the observed label is pcp_cpc​; maximizing log-likelihood over the dataset is minimizing −1N∑nlog⁡pcn-\frac{1}{N}\sum_n \log p_{c_n}−N1​∑n​logpcn​​. Where Gaussian likelihood gave us MSE, categorical likelihood gives us cross-entropy. Equivalently, in the language of Chapter 7: writing the true label as a one-hot vector yyy (a 1 at the correct class, 0 elsewhere), the loss −∑iyilog⁡pi-\sum_i y_i \log p_i−∑i​yi​logpi​ is the cross-entropy H(y,p)H(y, p)H(y,p) between the target distribution and the model’s, and minimizing it is the same as minimizing the Kullback–Leibler divergence DKL(y ∥ p)\KL(y \,\|\, p)DKL​(y∥p), since the entropy of a one-hot target is zero. Three framings — surprise, likelihood, distribution-matching — one loss.

Now for the derivation this chapter exists to deliver. We want the gradient of the loss with respect to the logits — the numbers the network actually produces — because that is the signal backpropagation will carry into the rest of the network.

∑The math

The two-case structure falls out in one line. Same answer, less sweat, once you trust it.

All that machinery — exponentials, a shared normalizer, a quotient rule, a logarithm — collapses to predicted minus target. The gradient on each logit is literally “how much probability you gave this class, minus how much you should have.” No exponentials survive into the gradient. No saturating σ′\sigma'σ′ factor multiplies the error into oblivion: when the model is confidently wrong (pcp_cpc​ near 0, so the gradient on the correct logit is near −1-1−1), the signal is at its strongest, and it fades gracefully to zero only as the prediction actually becomes correct. The log in the loss exactly cancels the exp in the softmax; the unbounded penalty of Figure 1 and the saturating squash annihilate each other, leaving a gradient that is linear in the error. This cancellation is not luck — MSE’s gradient y^−y\hat{y} - yy^​−y from its Gaussian likelihood, and softmax-CE’s p−yp - yp−y from its categorical likelihood, are two instances of one theorem about matching an output layer to its natural likelihood. Pair softmax with MSE instead and the cancellation is destroyed: the softmax Jacobian factors (pip_ipi​‘s and 1−pi1-p_i1−pi​‘s) survive in the gradient, and saturation returns.

⚙️In practice

Softmax + cross-entropy on logits is so universal that deep learning frameworks fuse them into a single numerically stable operation — PyTorch’s nn.CrossEntropyLoss (and F.cross_entropy) takes raw logits, never probabilities. Computing softmax yourself and then taking a log is both slower and numerically dangerous: for a large logit, eze^{z}ez overflows, and for ppp rounded to zero, log⁡p\log plogp is −∞-\infty−∞. The fused version works with log⁡S\log SlogS directly via the “log-sum-exp trick” (subtract the max logit before exponentiating) and never materializes the probabilities. If you ever see NaN losses in a model that computes its own softmax-then-log, this is the first suspect.

Label smoothing: don’t demand certainty

Cross-entropy with one-hot targets has a quietly odd property: it is never satisfied. The loss −log⁡pc-\log p_c−logpc​ hits zero only at pc=1p_c = 1pc​=1 exactly, which the softmax can approach but never reach with finite logits — driving pc→1p_c \to 1pc​→1 requires driving the correct-class logit toward infinity relative to the rest. So the optimizer keeps pushing logits apart long after the model’s ranking of the classes has stopped changing, manufacturing extreme confidence that the training labels may not deserve (labels are sometimes wrong; many examples are ambiguous).

Label smoothing, introduced in Szegedy et al. (2015) — the “Inception v3” paper, where it appears almost as an afterthought — softens the target. With smoothing parameter ε\varepsilonε (typically 0.1), replace the one-hot target with a mixture of the one-hot vector and the uniform distribution:

yiLS  =  (1−ε) yi  +  εK.y_i^{\text{LS}} \;=\; (1 - \varepsilon)\, y_i \;+\; \frac{\varepsilon}{K}.yiLS​=(1−ε)yi​+Kε​.

The correct class now gets target 1−ε+ε/K1 - \varepsilon + \varepsilon/K1−ε+ε/K and every other class gets ε/K\varepsilon/Kε/K — small but nonzero. The loss becomes the cross-entropy against this smoothed distribution, and — because our gradient derivation never used the fact that yyy was one-hot — the gradient is still exactly pi−yiLSp_i - y_i^{\text{LS}}pi​−yiLS​. The optimum is no longer at infinity: the model is asked to be confident, not certain.

✏️Worked example

Three classes, ε=0.1\varepsilon = 0.1ε=0.1: the smoothed target for a true cat is [ 0.9+0.1/3,  0.1/3,  0.1/3 ]≈[0.933,  0.033,  0.033][\,0.9 + 0.1/3,\; 0.1/3,\; 0.1/3\,] \approx [0.933,\; 0.033,\; 0.033][0.9+0.1/3,0.1/3,0.1/3]≈[0.933,0.033,0.033]. If the model predicts our earlier p=[0.70,0.20,0.10]p = [0.70, 0.20, 0.10]p=[0.70,0.20,0.10], the gradient is p−yLS≈[−0.233,  0.167,  0.067]p - y^{\text{LS}} \approx [-0.233,\; 0.167,\; 0.067]p−yLS≈[−0.233,0.167,0.067] — same directions, slightly gentler. That push-back is the entire mechanism.

When should you use it? Label smoothing (usually ε=0.1\varepsilon = 0.1ε=0.1) has been a standard ingredient in image classification since Inception, and the original transformer of Vaswani et al. (2017) used it for machine translation — it slightly hurt the training perplexity while improving translation quality, a nice reminder that the loss is a proxy. The careful study by Müller, Kornblith & Hinton (2019) found that it improves calibration (the model’s confidences better match its accuracy) but also erases fine-grained structure in the logits — smoothed teachers make measurably worse teachers for knowledge distillation. And one modern boundary matters most for this book: LLM pretraining does not use label smoothing. The next token is inherently uncertain, and we want the model’s output to converge to the true conditional distribution of language — the smeared targets would bias it toward uniformity and distort the very probabilities that perplexity and downstream sampling depend on. Standard practice in 2026: smoothing for classification-style fine-tuning tasks where you only need the argmax, none for language-model pretraining.

The loss landscape: what L(θ) actually looks like

Everything so far treats the loss one prediction at a time. Training sees it differently: L(θ)L(\theta)L(θ) is a single surface over parameter space — one axis per weight, plus one for the loss. For the toy networks of Chapter 8 that might be a surface over a few dozen dimensions; for a frontier model it is a surface over hundreds of billions. Next chapter’s algorithm, gradient descent, is nothing more than walking downhill on this surface, so it is worth asking what kind of terrain we are sending it into.

The honest answer starts with a confession: nobody can see this object. Every picture you have ever seen of a “loss landscape” — including the one below — is a one- or two-dimensional slice through a space with a billion axes, chosen by a human to be legible. With that disclaimer, here is what the slices, the theory, and a lot of hard-won experience suggest.

It is not convex, and that’s fine. A convex function (a single bowl) has one minimum, and optimization on it comes with guarantees. L(θ)L(\theta)L(θ) for any real network is emphatically not convex — but the classical fear that gradient descent would get stuck in bad local minima turned out to be mostly misplaced in high dimension. For a critical point (gradient zero) to be a local minimum, the surface must curve upward along every axis; with a billion directions available, points where some directions curve up and others down — saddle points — vastly outnumber true minima, and the minima that do exist tend to have similar, good loss values. High-dimensional geometry strikes again: what looks like a trap in 2D is, in a billion dimensions, almost always a mountain pass with an exit.

It is redundant. Swap two hidden neurons in a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) — along with their weights — and the function computed is identical, so the landscape contains factorially many copies of every solution. Scale symmetries (in networks with rectified linear unit (ReLU) activations, multiplying one layer by 10 and dividing the next by 10 changes nothing) add continuous flat directions. “The” minimum is really a vast connected family of equivalent parameter settings.

Flat beats sharp. Minima come in wide, flat basins and narrow, sharp ravines, and there is long-standing evidence — and a long-standing debate, flagged honestly in Chapter 13 — that flat minima generalize better: a solution that stays good when the parameters wobble is a solution that did not memorize fine detail. Li et al. (2018) visualized these slices and showed that architecture choices — notably residual connections — visibly smooth the terrain. And in a result that still surprises people, Goodfellow, Vinyals & Saxe (2014) found that along the straight line from a random initialization to a trained solution, the loss often descends nearly monotonically — no wall between the start and the goal. The landscape is rugged, but the paths that matter are gentler than the cartoons suggest.

⚠️Careful

Do not over-trust landscape pictures — including this book’s. A 2D slice can make the same network look smooth or hopelessly spiky depending on the slice direction and the scaling of the axes; Li et al. needed a normalization trick (scaling directions filter-by-filter) before comparisons across networks meant anything at all. When a blog post shows you a dramatic loss surface, the correct first question is “which two directions, and normalized how?”

The loss of a language model

Now connect all of this to the systems this book is really about. A language model is a classifier run in a loop: at each position in the text, it must produce a probability distribution over its entire vocabulary — every token, typically 50,000 to 250,000 of them — for what comes next. The pretraining loss is nothing more exotic than the cross-entropy of this chapter, applied at every position and averaged:

L(θ)  =  − 1T∑t=1Tlog⁡pθ(xt ∣ x1,…,xt−1),L(\theta) \;=\; -\,\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta\big(x_t \,\big|\, x_1, \dots, x_{t-1}\big),L(θ)=−T1​t=1∑T​logpθ​(xt​​x1​,…,xt−1​),

where x1,…,xTx_1, \dots, x_Tx1​,…,xT​ are the tokens of the training text and pθ(xt∣⋅)p_\theta(x_t \mid \cdot)pθ​(xt​∣⋅) is the probability the model assigned to the token that actually came next. Every insight from this chapter transfers directly: the loss is the model’s average surprise per token; minimizing it is maximum likelihood on the training corpus; the gradient flowing into the logits at every position is exactly p−yp - yp−y over the whole vocabulary — push up the actual next token, push down the hundred-thousand-odd pretenders in proportion to the probability each one stole.

⚙️In practice

What do the numbers look like? Pretraining losses are quoted in nats per token (natural log), and a healthy modern run typically lands somewhere around 2 nats per token, give or take a wide margin — the number depends heavily on the tokenizer (bigger vocabularies mean harder individual predictions), the data mixture, and model scale, so treat any specific figure as approximate and non-comparable across setups. Two nats means the model’s average surprise corresponds to a perplexity of e2≈7.4e^{2} \approx 7.4e2≈7.4: roughly, “as uncertain as a fair choice among 7 or so tokens” on average, even though the vocabulary offers six figures of options. The loss never approaches zero, and never should: language is inherently uncertain, and Chapter 7 tells us the floor is the entropy of language itself. The gap above that floor is what training can remove; scaling laws — Kaplan et al.’s and then Chinchilla’s — describe with eerie precision how that gap shrinks as a power law in model size, data, and compute.

During a training run, this number is plotted against tokens seen, and the resulting loss curve is the primary vital sign of a multi-month, multi-million-dollar process — the first chart checked every morning at every lab. Its healthy shape is a steep early drop (the model learning token frequencies and basic patterns is worth a lot of nats) flattening into a long, slow, grinding descent where each increment of loss buys disproportionate capability. Sudden upward loss spikes signal instability; plateaus signal learning-rate problems; a training loss that keeps falling while held-out loss rises signals memorization. The full art of reading these curves belongs to the training run.

One last practical wrinkle completes the picture: the loss that frontier models actually minimize is usually the cross-entropy plus small auxiliary terms. The most common is the z-loss popularized by the PaLM team: a tiny penalty (≈10−4⋅(log⁡Z)2\approx 10^{-4} \cdot (\log Z)^2≈10−4⋅(logZ)2, where ZZZ is the softmax normalizer SSS from our derivation) that keeps the logits from drifting to numerically dangerous magnitudes — cheap insurance against the saturation and overflow failure modes we met above, at scales where a single spike can cost days. Mixture-of-experts models add load-balancing losses that pay experts for sharing the work. The pattern to remember: the main loss defines what the model learns; auxiliary losses are small taxes that keep the optimization stable and efficient, and they are covered where they arise.

✎Note

A vocabulary note that prevents real confusion: “loss,” “objective,” “cost function,” and “criterion” are used near-interchangeably in papers and code. “Error” sometimes means loss and sometimes means (1 − accuracy). And a reward — the quantity in reinforcement learning from human feedback — is a loss with its sign flipped: something to maximize rather than minimize. When Part V discusses reward models, it is discussing learned loss functions by another name, with all of this chapter’s caveats about proxies attached.

Where this leaves us

  • A loss function is the only channel through which intent reaches a model: differentiable, aligned with the task, with gradients that stay informative — large when wrong, gentle when nearly right.
  • Mean squared error is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise; its gradient −2(y−y^)-2(y - \hat{y})−2(y−y^​) is proportional to the error, which is exactly the behavior a regression loss should have.
  • MSE fails for classification because its penalty is capped and, through a sigmoid or softmax, its gradient saturates — a confidently wrong model receives almost no learning signal.
  • Cross-entropy charges the model its own surprise, −log⁡pc-\log p_c−logpc​; it is simultaneously maximum likelihood for categories and KL minimization against the target, and its penalty grows without bound for confident mistakes.
  • The gradient of softmax + cross-entropy is pi−yip_i - y_ipi​−yi​ — predicted minus target — derived from the two-case softmax Jacobian, with the log exactly cancelling the exp. Worked check: pc=0.7p_c = 0.7pc​=0.7 on the true class gives a gradient of −0.3-0.3−0.3 on its logit.
  • Label smoothing mixes ε\varepsilonε of uniform into the one-hot target to cap confidence; standard for classification, deliberately absent from LLM pretraining, where calibrated next-token probabilities are the product.
  • The loss surface over parameters is non-convex, saddle-dominated, and visualizable only through slices that must be read skeptically; wide flat basins, not isolated points, are where trained networks live.
  • LLM pretraining loss is mean cross-entropy per token (roughly 2-ish nats for modern runs, setup-dependent), floored by the entropy of language, tracked as the loss curve, and lightly seasoned with auxiliary terms like z-loss.
