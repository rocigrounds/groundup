Multimodal Models

How transformers absorbed images, audio, and video: ViT patches, CLIP's shared embedding space, the three ways to bolt vision onto an LLM, Whisper and native voice, and the brutal token economics of video.

In this chapter

  3. Bolting eyes onto a language model
  4. Audio: from spectrograms to native voice
  5. Video: the token explosion
  6. The omni-model scorecard, 2026
  7. Where this leaves us

If you learned deep learning around 2017, you learned a field of separate kingdoms. Computer vision belonged to convolutional networks, speech recognition to its own stack of acoustic models and decoders, and language to recurrent networks. Each kingdom had its own architectures, its own conferences, its own folk wisdom. The idea that one architecture would swallow all of them — and that you would talk to a single model that looks at your screenshot, listens to your voice, and answers out loud — would have sounded like marketing.

That is what happened, and the mechanism is almost embarrassingly uniform: everything becomes tokens. The transformer you built piece by piece in Chapter 25 never cared that its input tokens were words. It cares that the input is a sequence of vectors. So the entire multimodal revolution reduces to one question, asked once per modality: how do we chop this signal into a sequence of vectors worth attending over? For images the answer is patches. For audio it is spectrogram frames or codec tokens. For video it is patches over frames — which, as we will see, is where the arithmetic gets scary.

This chapter builds the modern multimodal stack in the order history did: the Vision Transformer, which proved images could be tokens; CLIP, which taught images and text to share one embedding space; the three architectural patterns for giving a language model eyes; then audio, video, and an honest scorecard of where “any-to-any” models actually stand in 2026. Generating images and video with diffusion is its own machinery and gets its own chapter.

Images become tokens: the Vision Transformer

Start with the problem. A modest image — 224×224224 \times 224224×224 pixels, 3 color channels — is 150,528 numbers. Attention over individual pixels would mean a sequence of 50,176 positions (one per pixel location), and you know from Chapter 23 that attention cost grows with the square of sequence length: about 2.5 billion query–key pairs per layer per head. Dead on arrival. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) (Chapter 17) solved this with a built-in assumption: nearby pixels matter to each other, distant ones mostly don’t, and the same detector should slide across the whole image. Those assumptions — locality and translation equivariance — are called inductive biases: knowledge about the problem baked into the architecture rather than learned from data.

The Vision Transformer (ViT) (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021) made a bet that looks reckless: throw the biases away, and make the sequence manageable by chopping the image into patches.

💡Intuition

A patch is a visual word. Cut the image into a grid of 16×1616 \times 1616×16-pixel squares, flatten each square into one long vector, and treat each vector as a token — exactly the way a token embedding turns a word ID into a vector. After that single step, the “image model” is the transformer encoder you already know, unchanged: every patch attends to every other patch from layer one. Where a CNN must pass information through many layers to relate the top-left corner to the bottom-right, a ViT can relate them in a single attention operation.

∑The math

Take an image x∈RH×W×Cx \in \R^{H \times W \times C}x∈RH×W×C (height, width, channels) and a patch size PPP. Cut it into N=HWP2N = \frac{HW}{P^2}N=P2HW​ non-overlapping patches, each flattened into a vector of dimension P2CP^2 CP2C. A single learned matrix WE∈R(P2C)×dW_E \in \R^{(P^2 C) \times d}WE​∈R(P2C)×d projects each flat patch into the model dimension ddd:

zi=WE⊤ flatten(patchi)+b,i=1,…,N.z_i = W_E\T\, \mathrm{flatten}(\text{patch}_i) + b, \qquad i = 1, \dots, N.zi​=WE⊤​flatten(patchi​)+b,i=1,…,N.

Because the patches arrive as an unordered set, we add a learned position embedding pi∈Rdp_i \in \R^dpi​∈Rd to each token — a plain 1D lookup table, one entry per grid position, learned like any other parameter (compare Chapter 26). Following BERT (Chapter 27), the original ViT also prepends a learned [CLS] token: an extra input vector, tied to no patch, whose final hidden state is the summary of the whole image and feeds the classification head. The sequence [zCLS,z1+p1,…,zN+pN][z_{\text{CLS}}, z_1 + p_1, \dots, z_N + p_N][zCLS​,z1​+p1​,…,zN​+pN​] then enters a standard transformer encoder.

✏️Worked example

The canonical numbers, worth doing once by hand. Image 224×224×3224 \times 224 \times 3224×224×3, patch size P=16P = 16P=16:

  • Patches per side: 224/16=14224 / 16 = 14224/16=14, so N=14×14=196N = 14 \times 14 = \mathbf{196}N=14×14=196 tokens.
  • Flattened patch dimension: 16×16×3=76816 \times 16 \times 3 = \mathbf{768}16×16×3=768 numbers per patch.
  • ViT-Base uses model dimension d=768d = 768d=768, so WEW_EWE​ is a 768×768768 \times 768768×768 matrix — about 590k parameters turn raw pixels into token vectors.
  • With the [CLS] token, the sequence length is 197197197. Attention now touches 1972≈38,800197^2 \approx 38{,}8001972≈38,800 pairs per head per layer instead of 2.5 billion — a 64,000-fold saving bought by the P2=256P^2 = 256P2=256-pixel patch granularity.

One image ≈ 200 tokens. Hold onto that number; the video section multiplies it by thousands.

Why ViT needed scale to win

Here is the part that makes ViT a deep lesson rather than a clever trick. Trained on ImageNet’s 1.3 million images alone, ViT loses to a comparable ResNet. Trained on Google’s internal JFT-300M (300 million images) and then transferred, it wins — and keeps winning by more as models and data grow, while CNNs plateau earlier.

The explanation is the inductive-bias trade we set up above. A CNN’s architecture asserts locality; it cannot learn otherwise, and when data is scarce that assertion is a gift — it is regularization you don’t pay for (Chapter 13). A ViT has to learn that nearby patches usually matter most, spending data to rediscover what the CNN got for free. But an assertion is also a ceiling. Real images contain long-range structure a strictly local prior handles awkwardly — symmetry, repeated texture across the frame, a caption relating to a diagram — and given enough data, the model that assumed nothing ends up ahead of the model that assumed something almost right. Interpretability work later confirmed that early ViT attention heads learn both local, convolution-like attention and global attention in the same layer: it learns the CNN prior where useful and ignores it where not.

✎Note

There is a pleasing symmetry between this story and the rest of the book: scaling laws reward general architectures with weak priors, and the transformer keeps being the weakest-prior architecture we can train stably. ViT is the cleanest controlled experiment we have on that thesis — same data, same budget, prior versus no prior, and the crossover point is measurable in dataset size.

⚙️In practice

The standard sizes, still the reference points in 2026: ViT-Base/16 (12 layers, d=768d = 768d=768, ~86M parameters), ViT-Large/16 (24 layers, d=1024d = 1024d=1024, ~307M), ViT-Huge/14 (32 layers, d=1280d = 1280d=1280, ~632M) — the number after the slash is the patch size, and smaller patches mean more tokens and more compute at the same image size. Two field notes worth knowing. First, the patchify step is mathematically a convolution with kernel size PPP and stride PPP — the lone surviving convolution in the architecture, and how every framework implements it. Second, large ViTs develop an odd pathology: they hijack a few low-information background patches as internal scratchpads, giving those tokens huge norms and garbage attention maps. Vision Transformers Need Registers (Darcet et al., 2023) showed that appending a handful of extra throwaway “register” tokens — attended to but never read out — gives the model a legitimate scratchpad and cleans up the artifacts. Registers are now a standard ingredient — the DINOv2 lineage ships register variants — though not a universal one: SigLIP 2, notably, does without them and pools its output with attention instead of a [CLS] token.

Teaching images and text to share a space: CLIP

A ViT trained on ImageNet knows 1,000 nouns. It has no idea that a photo of a golden retriever has anything to do with the string “golden retriever,” let alone “a good boy at the beach.” For images to enter a language model’s world, vision needs to land in a space where text already has coordinates — the shared-embedding-space idea from Chapter 20, stretched across modalities.

CLIP — Contrastive Language–Image Pre-training (Radford et al., 2021) — got there with a training signal of almost brutal simplicity, applied to 400 million image–caption pairs scraped from the web. Train two encoders: an image encoder (a ViT) producing a vector per image, and a text encoder (a small transformer) producing a vector per caption. Ask one thing: given a batch of images and captions, match them up.

💡Intuition

Think of it as a massive game of concentration. Show the model NNN images and the same NNN captions, shuffled. Every image embedding is compared to every caption embedding — an N×NN \times NN×N grid of similarity scores. The diagonal cells are the true pairs; everything off-diagonal is a decoy. Training pulls each true pair together in embedding space and pushes the N−1N-1N−1 decoys away. No labels, no class list — the caption is the label, and the web wrote millions of them for free.

∑The math

Let uiu_iui​ be the image embedding and vjv_jvj​ the text embedding, both normalized to unit length so that the dot product ui⋅vju_i \cdot v_jui​⋅vj​ is a cosine similarity in [−1,1][-1, 1][−1,1]. Form the logit matrix

Lij=ui⋅vjτ,L_{ij} = \frac{u_i \cdot v_j}{\tau},Lij​=τui​⋅vj​​,

where τ\tauτ is a temperature — the same role it played in softmax sampling: small τ\tauτ sharpens the distinction between the best match and the rest. CLIP learns τ\tauτ as a parameter (as ete^{t}et for a learnable scalar ttt, so it stays positive), initialized at τ=0.07\tau = 0.07τ=0.07 and capped so training can’t collapse it to zero.

Limg→txt=−1N∑i=1Nlog⁡eLii∑j=1NeLij.\mathcal{L}_{\text{img}\to\text{txt}} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \frac{e^{L_{ii}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} e^{L_{ij}}}.Limg→txt​=−N1​i=1∑N​log∑j=1N​eLij​eLii​​.

The total is the average of the two. This is the InfoNCE objective, and Chapter 7 gives the deeper reading: minimizing it maximizes a lower bound on the mutual information between image and caption embeddings — the encoders are forced to keep exactly the information the two modalities share (content) and free to discard what they don’t (JPEG noise, word order quirks).

What CLIP bought

Three things, each of which shaped the next five years.

Zero-shot classification. To classify an image into arbitrary categories, embed the image, embed the strings “a photo of a dog”, “a photo of a spreadsheet”, …, and pick the nearest. CLIP matched a supervised ResNet-50 on ImageNet without seeing a single ImageNet label — and, more importantly, it degrades gracefully on out-of-distribution images where trained-on-the-test-set classifiers fall off a cliff.

The vision backbone for vision-language models (VLMs). Because CLIP’s image features were optimized to align with language, they turned out to be exactly the right currency to hand a language model. Nearly every open vision-language model of 2023–2026 starts from a CLIP-family encoder. This is the section after next.

Embedding search. Image search, deduplication, content moderation, and the data-curation pipelines of Chapter 31 — including, recursively, filtering the training data for image generators — run on CLIP similarity. When Chapter 42 mentions “CLIP score,” this is the object computing it.

⚙️In practice

CLIP’s softmax has an engineering sore spot: the denominator couples every example in the batch, and CLIP needs huge batches (32,768 in the original) so that decoys are plentiful and hard. At that scale the N×NN \times NN×N logit matrix must be assembled across all devices every step — an all-gather of everyone’s embeddings (Chapter 33). SigLIP (Zhai et al., 2023) replaces the softmax with independent sigmoids: every cell (i,j)(i, j)(i,j) becomes its own binary question — “is this a real pair?” — with label +1+1+1 on the diagonal and −1-1−1 off it, scored by log⁡σ\log \sigmalogσ of the signed logit (plus a learned bias to offset the wild class imbalance: NNN positives versus N2−NN^2 - NN2−N negatives). No shared denominator means the loss decomposes into chunks that devices can compute pairwise without a global gather, and — the surprising empirical finding — it works better at moderate batch sizes, with returns saturating around 32k anyway. SigLIP and its 2025 successor SigLIP 2 are the default open vision encoders in 2026 (PaliGemma, many LLaVA descendants); the pattern of “replace a coupled softmax with decoupled sigmoids when scaling hurts” echoes the router story in Chapter 29.

Bolting eyes onto a language model

CLIP can say this image is about a golden retriever. It cannot answer “what is the dog about to knock off the table, and why is that funny?” Reasoning about images needs the machinery we spent Part V building: a large language model (LLM) with its world knowledge and instruction-following. The engineering question of 2022–2026 has been how to marry the two, and every answer in production falls into one of three patterns.

Pattern 1 — the projector (LLaVA and nearly every open model)

The cheapest idea turned out to be the durable one. Take a pretrained CLIP-style ViT. Take a pretrained LLM. Between them, train a small projector — in LLaVA (Liu et al., 2023), literally a linear layer; in LLaVA-1.5, a two-layer multi-layer perceptron (MLP) — that maps each ViT output vector into the LLM’s embedding dimension. The projected vectors are then spliced into the prompt as if they were token embeddings: the LLM sees [image tokens][“What is unusual about this picture?”] and continues in text, its self-attention freely mixing visual and textual positions.

Nothing about the LLM changes architecturally. The visual tokens skip the embedding table — they arrive as vectors, not vocabulary IDs — but from the first transformer block onward they are citizens like any other. Training typically runs in two stages: first freeze both big models and train only the projector on image–caption pairs (aligning the spaces), then unfreeze the LLM (and often the ViT) and fine-tune on visual instruction data — conversations about images, which LLaVA famously bootstrapped by having GPT-4 (text-only) write Q&A dialogues about the captions and box annotations of existing images. The original LLaVA trained its alignment stage on a single node of eight graphics processing units (GPUs) in about a day; that accessibility, more than any benchmark, is why the projector pattern took over open research. The lineage runs straight through BLIP-2’s learned-query variant to Qwen-VL, InternVL, and most of the open VLM leaderboard in 2026.

Pattern 2 — cross-attention (Flamingo)

DeepMind’s Flamingo (Alayrac et al., 2022) predates LLaVA and answers a different constraint: do not disturb the language model at all. The language model (LM) — a 70B Chinchilla — stays completely frozen. Vision enters through new gated cross-attention layers inserted between the frozen transformer blocks: the text stream provides queries, the image features provide keys and values — the same cross-attention you met in seq2seq, pointed at pixels. A Perceiver Resampler first compresses the ViT’s variable number of patch vectors down to a fixed 64 latent tokens, so cross-attention cost doesn’t depend on image count or resolution.

The detail that makes it trainable is the gate: each new layer’s output is multiplied by tanh⁡(α)\tanh(\alpha)tanh(α) with α\alphaα initialized to zero. At step zero the new layers contribute nothing — the model is exactly the frozen LM, fluent from the start — and vision fades in only as α\alphaα grows. This is the residual-stream logic of Chapter 16 used as a safety valve: never destroy a working function; add to it gently. Flamingo’s other legacy is data: it trained on interleaved web pages — text with images scattered mid-document — which taught it few-shot visual learning (show it two example image–answer pairs in context, it does the third), and interleaved pretraining data has been standard for serious VLMs ever since.

The trade-offs across patterns 1 and 2 are real: projector models spend LLM context on every image (hundreds of tokens deep in the key–value (KV) cache), while cross-attention keeps images out of the context but adds parameters and a second attention path that complicates serving. Industry drifted toward the projector for its uniformity — one sequence, one cache, one serving path — and that is what “VLM” means by default in 2026. Meta’s Llama 3.2-Vision was the notable cross-attention holdout.

Pattern 3 — early fusion (Chameleon, GPT-4o, Gemini)

Both patterns above are marriages of two pretrained models, and it shows: the vision encoder was trained to describe, not to read the time off an analog clock, and whatever it didn’t preserve, the LLM never sees. The third pattern refuses the marriage: choose token vocabularies for every modality up front and pretrain one transformer from scratch on mixed streams — “early fusion,” because modalities meet at the input rather than in the middle.

For images this usually means going discrete: a vector-quantized (VQ) tokenizer — an autoencoder whose bottleneck snaps each spatial cell to the nearest entry in a learned codebook (compare tokenization, but learned from pixels) — turns an image into, say, 1,024 IDs from a codebook of 8,192. Now an image is literally a sequence of vocabulary items, the model’s softmax ranges over text and image tokens, and the same next-token training from Chapter 18 covers reading and generating images in one objective. Meta’s Chameleon (2024) is the open documentation of this recipe — including its cost: mixed-modal training destabilized badly at scale until they re-plumbed normalization (query-key norm, reordered LayerNorms — the Chapter 14 toolkit earning its keep).

The frontier labs (Chapter 40) bet on this pattern early. GPT-4o (“omni”) and the Gemini line were pretrained natively multimodal, and the payoff became visible in 2025 when native image generation shipped inside the chat models themselves — the model that reasons about your prompt is the model laying down image tokens, which is why these systems handle text rendering inside images and iterative “now make the sign say OPEN” edits far better than the separate diffusion pipelines of Chapter 42. The honest caveats: continuous-input patterns still tend to win at fine-grained understanding per FLOP, discrete tokenizers throw away detail that captioning tasks miss but optical character recognition (OCR) tasks need, and no lab has published a clean controlled comparison at frontier scale. Early fusion is the direction of travel, not yet a solved argument — see the scorecard at the end of this chapter.

Resolution: the tiling wars

One ViT at 336×336336 \times 336336×336 gives 576 tokens and cannot read a receipt. The field’s fixes are unglamorous but consequential. Tiling / AnyRes (LLaVA-NeXT, InternVL): keep the fixed-size encoder, cut a high-resolution image into a grid of 3362336^23362 tiles, encode each tile separately, concatenate all the tokens plus a downscaled thumbnail for global context — a 2×32 \times 32×3 grid is already 7×576=4,0327 \times 576 = 4{,}0327×576=4,032 tokens. Native dynamic resolution (Qwen2-VL and Qwen2.5-VL): drop the fixed input size entirely, patchify at the image’s own resolution into however many tokens it needs, use windowed attention inside the ViT to keep cost linear-ish, and extend rotary position embeddings (RoPE, Chapter 26) to two spatial axes (M-RoPE) so positions mean where, not which index. Either way, the lesson generalizes: in multimodal models, resolution is a token budget, and “how many tokens is this image worth?” is a live engineering dial — modern model providers literally bill images in tokens.

⚠️Careful

VLMs are worse at seeing than their prose suggests. Fluent description coexists, in 2026, with failures that would embarrass a careful child: counting eight objects and reporting six; confusing left/right and above/below in cluttered scenes; misreading analog clocks and chess positions; inventing values off a dense chart while sounding certain (a favorite demonstration paper is titled, accurately, “Vision language models are blind”). The causes are structural, not cosmetic: a few hundred tokens per image is a lossy summary tilted toward what things are rather than exactly where and how many; contrastive pretraining rewarded gist, not measurement; and captions on the web rarely say “the third bolt from the left is missing.” Treat a VLM’s reading of any chart, meter, or diagram the way you would treat an eyewitness: plausible, useful, and to be verified before money moves.

Audio: from spectrograms to native voice

Sound enters the token world by a different door than images, because raw audio is absurdly oversampled for meaning: 16,000 amplitude values per second, almost none of them individually informative. Every practical system first folds audio into a spectrogram — sliding a short window across the waveform and measuring the energy in each frequency band — producing an image-like array of time × frequency. From there, the transformer recipe applies unchanged.

Whisper (Radford et al., 2022) is the clean case study, and a deliberate throwback: a plain encoder–decoder transformer, the Chapter 22 shape, applied to speech-to-text. Thirty seconds of audio become an 80-band log-mel spectrogram — 3,000 frames at 10 ms per frame — which two small convolutions downsample to 1,500 positions for the encoder. The decoder is a text language model that cross-attends to those audio positions, and its output format is itself a lesson in the token religion: special tokens tell the decoder which language to expect, whether to transcribe or translate, and where timestamps fall, so one model does four jobs with no architectural change. Whisper’s real innovation was the data bet: 680,000 hours of weakly supervised audio–transcript pairs scraped from the web, noise and all, instead of the few thousand pristine academic hours that were standard — scaling-law reasoning (Chapter 32) applied to speech, and it produced recognition robust to accents and background noise in a way clean data never had. Its encoder now leads a second life as the “audio ViT” bolted onto LLMs by projector, exactly pattern 1 (Qwen2-Audio and most open audio-LLMs do this).

Cascades versus native voice

Until 2024, every “voice mode” you used was a cascade: speech recognition transcribes you, an LLM answers in text, text-to-speech (TTS) reads the answer. Cascades work, and much of production still runs on them, but the figure above shows the two structural flaws. Latency stacks — each stage waits on the last, and ChatGPT’s original voice mode averaged several seconds per turn (OpenAI reported ~2.8 s with GPT-3.5, ~5.4 s with GPT-4) — far above the ~200–500 ms gap at which human conversation feels alive. And the text bottleneck is literal: everything a transcript cannot carry — your sarcasm, your hesitation, which word you stressed, the fact that two people are talking — is destroyed before the model thinks, and the reply comes back in a voice that cannot choose to whisper.

Native voice models remove the bottleneck by making audio a first-class token stream, usually via a neural audio codec (SoundStream and EnCodec are the canonical designs): an encoder–quantizer–decoder that compresses audio to discrete IDs — typically a few dozen frames per second, with residual quantization stacking a few parallel codebooks per frame — plus a decoder that turns IDs back into a waveform. The omni model then consumes and emits codec tokens interleaved with text, streaming its reply as sound directly. GPT-4o was the public turning point: voice-to-voice latency around 320 ms on average (OpenAI’s reported figure — treat it, like all latency numbers here, as approximate and fast-moving), audible tone control, and the ability to be interrupted mid-sentence because the model is still listening while it speaks. Gemini’s Live mode and a wave of open models (Moshi from Kyutai is a notable fully-duplex open example) followed. The same codec-token recipe powers the generation-only branches of audio: text-to-speech systems in the VALL-E lineage, and music generators from AudioLM and MusicLM through the commercial wave of Suno, Udio, and ElevenLabs — all, at heart, language models over codec vocabularies (Chapter 42 covers the competing diffusion route).

Video: the token explosion

Video is where the “everything is tokens” story collides with arithmetic, and arithmetic wins. A video is images times time, and the token bill multiplies accordingly.

✏️Worked example

Take the LLaVA-1.5-era rate of 576 tokens per frame and a 30-minute screen recording. At a modest 1 frame per second: 30×60=1,80030 \times 60 = 1{,}80030×60=1,800 frames, and 1,800×576=1,036,8001{,}800 \times 576 = \mathbf{1{,}036{,}800}1,800×576=1,036,800 tokens — a million-token context (Chapter 43) consumed by half an hour at one frame per second, before you have asked a single question. At 24 fps it would be ~25 million tokens. And the KV cache for a million tokens runs to hundreds of gigabytes at 7B-model dimensions — the memory bill arrives before the compute bill does.

So every video model is, first and foremost, a token-budget policy. The standard levers, usually combined: sample sparsely in time (1 fps is the common default; the model literally does not see what happens between samples, which is why VLMs miss the moment the ball leaves the hand); compress per frame (aggressive pooling down to tens of tokens per frame — Gemini has used ~258 tokens per frame at 1 fps, so an hour of video fits in roughly 3,600×258≈930k3{,}600 \times 258 \approx 930\text{k}3,600×258≈930k tokens; approximate, per Google’s docs); and compress across time — consecutive frames are nearly identical, so architectures increasingly patchify space–time tubes or merge near-duplicate tokens rather than re-paying for a static background every frame, and Qwen2.5-VL aligns its position encoding to absolute timestamps so the model knows two sampled frames are 4 seconds apart, not “adjacent.”

Long-video understanding is, as of 2026, a Gemini signature: the Gemini 1.5 report demonstrated needle-in-a-haystack retrieval from hours of footage inside a million-token-class context, and “find the scene where…” over an hour of video is now a working product surface, if an expensive one. The competing lightweight approach — sample a few frames, caption or embed them, and retrieve relevant moments on demand — trades fidelity for cost and remains what most applications ship. Generating video is a different animal entirely — diffusion over space–time latents, Sora and Veo — and belongs to Chapter 42.

The omni-model scorecard, 2026

The marketing word is “any-to-any”: one model, all modalities in, all modalities out. Honest status, modality by modality, as of mid-2026:

  • Text and images in: solved as a default. Every frontier model and most serious open models take interleaved text + images. Quality varies exactly where the warning box above says it does.
  • Audio in/out: native at the frontier, cascaded in the long tail. True speech-to-speech (GPT-4o-class, Gemini Live) is real and shipping; plenty of production “voice” is still Whisper + LLM + TTS because cascades are cheap, controllable, and easier to moderate.
  • Image out: crossed over in 2025. Native autoregressive image generation inside chat models went from demo to default, displacing bolt-on diffusion for instruction-heavy editing; dedicated diffusion systems still lead on raw visual fidelity.
  • Video in: real but rationed by the token economics above. Video out: not unified — generation lives in separate diffusion systems, and no frontier chat model emits video natively as of this writing.
  • One set of weights for everything: partial. Even “omni” systems route some outputs through specialist decoders and voices, and labs publish few details. The gap between the any-to-any diagram and the shipping plumbing is real — narrowing yearly, but real.

The deeper takeaway is architectural, and it is the thesis of this chapter: modality turned out to be a tokenizer problem, not an architecture problem. Nobody redesigned the transformer for vision or speech; they redesigned the first five minutes of the pipeline — patches, contrastive alignment, codecs — and the machine from Parts IV and V did the rest. That is also why interpretability work on multimodal models (Part VII) reuses the language-model toolkit nearly unchanged: same residual stream, same attention heads, same features — some of which fire for “golden retriever” whether it arrives as pixels or as letters.

Where this leaves us

  • You can walk the ViT pipeline from raw pixels to transformer input — 224² image, 16×16 patches, 196 tokens of dimension 768 plus [CLS] and learned positions — and explain why it loses to CNNs on small data and wins at scale (inductive bias as a gift that becomes a ceiling).
  • You can write CLIP’s symmetric InfoNCE loss from memory, compute it by hand on a 3×3 logit matrix, say what the learned temperature does, and explain why SigLIP’s per-pair sigmoids sidestep the giant-batch softmax gather.
  • You can classify any vision-language model you meet into projector (LLaVA-style, the open-model default), gated cross-attention (Flamingo), or early fusion over discrete tokens (Chameleon, GPT-4o, Gemini) — and argue the trade-offs, including why resolution is really a token budget.
  • You know what VLMs still reliably get wrong in 2026 — counting, precise spatial relations, dense charts — and the structural reasons why.
  • You can trace both audio routes: Whisper’s encoder–decoder over spectrograms, and native voice over neural-codec tokens, with the latency and lost-paralinguistics argument for why cascades are being replaced.
  • You can do the video token arithmetic (a million tokens for 30 minutes at 1 fps × 576 tokens/frame) and name the three levers — temporal sampling, per-frame compression, temporal merging — every video model pulls.
