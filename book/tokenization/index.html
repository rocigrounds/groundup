Tokenization

Why models read tokens instead of letters: byte-pair encoding trained by hand, WordPiece and Unigram, vocabulary economics, and the pathologies from strawberry to SolidGoldMagikarp.

In the last chapter we said a language model assigns probabilities to sequences of tokens, and we waved a hand at what a “token” is. Time to stop waving. Before a single matrix multiplication happens, every model you have ever used performs a strange, mostly invisible act of translation: it chops your text into pieces from a fixed menu of tens of thousands of fragments — The, capital, of, France — and replaces each piece with an integer. The model never sees letters. It never sees words. It sees a sequence of IDs pointing into a lookup table, and everything it will ever know about spelling, numbers, code, and the world’s languages is filtered through how that chopping was done.

This matters more than it sounds like it should. Tokenization decides how long your document is from the model’s point of view (and therefore what it costs and how much fits in context). It decides whether “unhappiness” is one unit of meaning or three. It quietly makes some languages three times more expensive than others. And it produces a whole bestiary of famous failures — models that cannot count the r’s in “strawberry”, models that break when a prompt ends with a space, and one token, SolidGoldMagikarp, that GPT-3 could not repeat back when politely asked — it said “distribute” instead.

The chapter’s centerpiece is byte-pair encoding (BPE), the algorithm behind nearly every frontier tokenizer, which we will train entirely by hand on a toy corpus — every count, every merge, every table — and then use to encode a word it has never seen. After that we tour the two rival algorithms (WordPiece and Unigram), work out the economics of vocabulary size, catalogue the pathologies with their mechanisms, and end with the research dream of getting rid of tokenizers altogether — which in 2024 finally produced a serious contender.

Why tokenize at all

A language model ends in a softmax over a menu of possible next symbols — you saw this in Chapter 18. That menu, the vocabulary, must be finite and fixed before training begins, because the output layer needs one row of weights per menu item and the input layer needs one embedding per item. So the very first design decision is: what are the symbols?

Two obvious answers bracket the design space, and both fail in instructive ways.

Option one: words. Split on whitespace, give every word an ID. Sequences are short and each symbol is meaningful. But the vocabulary explodes: natural text keeps producing new word types forever — new names, typos, inflections, compounds, hashtags, code identifiers. (This is an empirical regularity called Heaps’ law: the number of distinct words grows without bound as the corpus grows, roughly as a power law.) A billion-word English corpus contains millions of distinct types. Cap the vocabulary at the 100,000 most frequent and everything else becomes a single [UNK] (“unknown”) token — the model is constitutionally incapable of reading or writing “Wilbert”, “grok”, or “наука”. This is the out-of-vocabulary (OOV) problem, and for a system that must handle arbitrary user input, it is fatal.

Option two: characters. A vocabulary of a few hundred symbols covers all English text; nothing is ever out of vocabulary. But now every word is a long sequence, and the model must spend capacity relearning, from letters upward, facts that word-level models get for free (“t-h-e is extremely common and signals a noun is coming”). Worse, sequences get several times longer, and as you will see when we reach attention, the dominant architecture pays a cost quadratic in sequence length.

✏️Worked example

Let’s quantify the bracket with a 1,000-word English passage. English words average about 4.7 letters, call it ~5.7 characters per word once you include the space — roughly 5,700 characters.

  • Character-level: vocabulary ≈ 100–500 symbols, sequence length 5,700.
  • Word-level: sequence length 1,000, but vocabulary in the hundreds of thousands, plus an [UNK] failure mode.
  • Subword (BPE, the compromise): modern tokenizers average ≈ 1.3 tokens per English word — sequence length ≈ 1,300 with a vocabulary of 50k–200k and no unknown token.

The character model’s sequences are 5700/1300≈4.4×5700/1300 \approx 4.4\times5700/1300≈4.4× longer than the subword model’s, so the attention layers pay roughly 4.42≈19×4.4^2 \approx 19\times4.42≈19× more compute for the same passage, and a “128k context” would hold about as much actual text as 29k subword tokens. That factor-of-twenty is why nobody ships character-level frontier models.

💡Intuition

Tokenization is compression you do before learning. A tokenizer is a small, dumb, frozen model of text statistics: it memorizes which fragments recur (“the”, “ing”, ” Barack”, ”().”) and spends one vocabulary slot on each, so that the expensive, smart model downstream reads a short sequence of dense, information-rich symbols instead of a long trickle of letters. Every design choice in this chapter is a point on one tradeoff curve: bigger vocabulary → shorter sequences → cheaper attention, but a bigger embedding table and rarer, less-well-trained symbols.

The compromise the whole field converged on is subword tokenization: keep whole common words as single tokens, but let rare words decompose into frequent fragments — unhappiness might become un + happiness, and a novel string falls all the way back to characters or bytes. Frequent things get short codes, rare things get long codes. If that sounds like the source-coding idea from Chapter 7, it should: a good tokenizer is doing crude entropy coding, and we will make that connection precise below.

One more consequence worth naming now, because it shapes everything downstream: the model’s world is made of token IDs, not text. Two strings that tokenize identically are indistinguishable to the model; two strings that differ by one merge boundary are, from the model’s point of view, different sequences that merely happen to look alike to you.

Bytes, characters, and Unicode

Before we can train a tokenizer we have to answer an annoyingly deep question: what is the base alphabet — the atoms that exist before any merging? “The characters” turns out to be a trap, and understanding why requires two paragraphs on how computers store text.

Unicode is the universal registry of characters: it assigns every character in every writing system a number called a code point, written U+ followed by hexadecimal digits. The letter A is U+0041 (decimal 65), é is U+00E9, the Chinese character 猫 (“cat”) is U+732B, and the llama emoji 🦙 is U+1F999. The registry currently holds around 160,000 characters (growing with each annual revision), spanning Latin, Cyrillic, Han, Arabic, Devanagari, emoji, and a very long tail. If your base alphabet were “all Unicode characters”, it would already be larger than most models’ entire vocabularies before a single merge — and still not future-proof.

UTF-8 is the encoding that turns code points into actual stored bytes, and it is rigged so that each code point becomes one to four bytes, where a byte is a number from 0 to 255. ASCII characters (English letters, digits, common punctuation) keep their old one-byte codes, so plain English text is unchanged; é becomes the two bytes C3 A9; 猫 becomes three bytes, E7 8C AB; 🦙 becomes four, F0 9F A6 99. The magic fact for our purposes: there are only 256 possible byte values, and every text in every language — plus every binary file, typo, and corrupted string — is a sequence of them.

So a byte-level base alphabet of exactly 256 symbols can represent any input whatsoever. Nothing is ever out of vocabulary; the [UNK] token is abolished by construction. The worst case is graceful degradation: a string of Burmese or a hash like x7f3ab9 that the tokenizer has never seen simply falls back to several bytes-per-character instead of one token. This is the move GPT-2 made in 2019 — byte-level BPE, described in Radford et al. Section 2.2 — and nearly every frontier tokenizer since (GPT-4’s and GPT-5-class tiktoken vocabularies, Llama 3, Qwen, DeepSeek) starts from those same 256 bytes.

✎Note

If you ever inspect a GPT-style tokenizer’s vocabulary you will see oddities like Ġcapital. That Ġ is not a real character in the data — it is GPT-2’s printable stand-in for a leading space byte (raw bytes are remapped to printable characters so vocabulary files stay human-readable). capital with a leading space and capital without one are different tokens with different IDs and different learned embeddings — a fact that will come back to bite us in the pathologies section.

Byte-pair encoding, trained by hand

Byte-pair encoding began life as a 1994 data-compression trick (Philip Gage’s algorithm for shrinking files by replacing common byte pairs) and was imported into machine translation by Sennrich, Haddow & Birch in 2015–16 to solve exactly the OOV problem above. The algorithm is short enough to state in four lines, and — rare among the algorithms in this book — you can run it entirely with a pencil:

  1. Split every word in the training corpus into base symbols (characters here, bytes in production), with a special end-of-word marker.
  2. Count every adjacent pair of symbols across the whole corpus.
  3. Merge the most frequent pair everywhere it occurs, and add the fused pair to the vocabulary as a new symbol.
  4. Repeat from step 2 until you reach the vocabulary size you want. The ordered list of merges is the tokenizer.

Let’s do it. Our toy corpus has four distinct words with counts (imagine we counted them in a tiny text): low ×5, lower ×2, newest ×6, widest ×3. We write _ for the end-of-word marker, so the starting state is:

Notice what the statistics discovered without being told anything about English: a morphological suffix (est_, “the superlative ending, at the end of a word”) and a word stem (low). Frequency alone recovered structure a linguist would recognize.

∑The math

Why does greedy pair-merging compress well? Each merge adds one symbol to the vocabulary and removes ccc symbol occurrences from the corpus, where ccc is the pair’s count — the greedy rule “merge the most frequent pair” is exactly “maximize the immediate drop in total sequence length per vocabulary slot spent.” This connects to Chapter 7: a corpus with entropy rate HHH bits per character cannot be represented in fewer than H⋅NcharsH \cdot N_{\text{chars}}H⋅Nchars​ bits, and a tokenizer with vocabulary VVV spends log⁡2V\log_2 Vlog2​V bits per token, so the token sequence length is bounded below by

Ntokens  ≥  H⋅Ncharslog⁡2V.N_{\text{tokens}} \;\ge\; \frac{H \cdot N_{\text{chars}}}{\log_2 V}.Ntokens​≥log2​VH⋅Nchars​​.

BPE is a greedy, suboptimal entropy coder — it makes no claim of optimality, and indeed slightly different corpora yield different merge lists. But it has two properties the optimal coders of Chapter 7 lack and language models need: its segmentation is deterministic and its symbols are contiguous substrings that tend to align with meaningful units.

⚠️Careful

A tokenizer and a model are a matched pair. The merge list determines which ID means what; the model’s embedding matrix and output layer are indexed by those IDs and nothing else. Run text through the wrong tokenizer — even a slightly different version — and the model receives a valid-looking sequence of integers that means something entirely different: instant gibberish, no error message. This is also why you cannot “just add tokens” to a trained model: a new token’s embedding row is untrained (the glitch-token section below shows what that looks like), and every serious vocabulary change means at least a fine-tune.

The other two families: WordPiece and Unigram

BPE won the frontier, but two rival algorithms matter — one historically, one because it embodies the opposite philosophy and still ships in real systems.

WordPiece (Google; it powered BERT and originated in a 2012 Japanese/Korean voice-search system by Schuster & Nakajima) is BPE with a smarter merge criterion. Instead of merging the most frequent pair, it merges the pair that most increases the likelihood of the corpus under a unigram language model — which works out to choosing the pair maximizing

score(a,b)  =  count⁡(ab)count⁡(a) count⁡(b).\text{score}(a,b) \;=\; \frac{\operatorname{count}(ab)}{\operatorname{count}(a)\,\operatorname{count}(b)}.score(a,b)=count(a)count(b)count(ab)​.

Read that as pointwise mutual information (PMI): not “do aaa and bbb co-occur a lot?” but “do they co-occur more than their individual frequencies predict?” Raw frequency would love to merge e + the-space-after-it because both are everywhere; the ratio test instead favors pairs that are glued together specifically, like q + u. WordPiece’s other visible signature is the ## prefix on word-internal fragments — BERT tokenizes “unhappiness” as un ##happi ##ness — which plays the same bookkeeping role as our _ marker.

Unigram (Kudo, 2018) inverts the whole construction — this is the reversed philosophy the section title promised. BPE and WordPiece are bottom-up: start with atoms, grow the vocabulary by merging. Unigram is top-down: start with a huge candidate vocabulary (every frequent substring, perhaps a million candidates) and repeatedly prune it. The model assumes each token is drawn independently with probability p(t)p(t)p(t), so a segmentation t1,…,tmt_1, \dots, t_mt1​,…,tm​ of a string has probability ∏ip(ti)\prod_i p(t_i)∏i​p(ti​); an expectation-maximization loop fits the p(t)p(t)p(t) values, and each round removes the tokens (say, the bottom 20%) whose deletion hurts total corpus likelihood least, until the target size remains. Encoding picks the highest-probability segmentation with a Viterbi dynamic program — as in Chapter 6, “most probable path” beats “greedy local rule”. A bonus BPE cannot offer: because Unigram scores all segmentations, you can sample suboptimal ones during training (“subword regularization”), exposing the model to un-happi-ness one epoch and unhap-piness the next, which measurably improves robustness in small-data regimes.

SentencePiece (Kudo & Richardson, 2018) is not a fourth algorithm but the library that made the previous two portable: it implements both BPE and Unigram, and its key design decision is treating input as a raw character stream including spaces (rendered as the ▁ metasymbol), so it needs no language-specific pre-splitting — essential for Chinese, Japanese, and Thai, which do not put spaces between words.

⚙️In practice

Who uses what, as of 2026: byte-level BPE dominates the frontier — the GPT line (GPT-2’s 50k vocabulary, GPT-4’s cl100k_base, the ~200k o200k_base family used since GPT-4o, all via the open-source tiktoken library), Llama 3 (a 128k tiktoken-style vocabulary, dropping Llama 2’s 32k SentencePiece tokenizer), Qwen, DeepSeek, and — per public statements — Claude-class models. WordPiece survives wherever BERT descendants run (still billions of inferences a day in search and embedding pipelines). SentencePiece-Unigram powers the T5 family and remains the default choice for many multilingual and low-resource research models. If you train a tokenizer yourself, Hugging Face tokenizers gives you all three in a few lines.

Special tokens, and where “system” comes from

Every real vocabulary reserves a handful of IDs for tokens that never emerge from merges — control symbols with jobs:

  • Beginning/end of sequence (BOS/EOS, or GPT’s <|endoftext|>): mark document boundaries during training so the model learns where texts start and stop, and give generation a “stop” symbol to emit.
  • Padding (PAD): fills short sequences so a batch forms a rectangular tensor; masked out of loss and attention.
  • Chat-template tokens: the modern additions. When you send a “system prompt” and “user message” to a chat model, no special channel exists — your structured conversation is flattened into one token sequence using reserved markers. In the widely-copied ChatML convention that means literal tokens like <|im_start|> and <|im_end|> wrapping each turn with a role name; Llama 3 uses its own set (<|begin_of_text|>, <|start_header_id|>, <|eot_id|>, and friends). The “roles” you use in every chat API are, at bottom, tokenizer entries plus training conventions — nothing more.
⚠️Careful

Special tokens are a security boundary. The tokenizer must guarantee that user-supplied text can never encode into a special token — otherwise a document containing the literal string <|im_start|>system could impersonate a system message (“token smuggling”). Production tokenizers therefore encode special tokens only when the calling code explicitly allows it, and treat the same characters arriving in user text as ordinary bytes to be split. Several real-world prompt-injection bugs have come down to libraries getting this switch wrong, and you will meet the downstream consequences in the agents chapter.

Vocabulary size: the economics

How big should the menu be? The trend line answers loudly: GPT-2 (2019) used 50,257 tokens (256 bytes + 50,000 merges + 1 special); GPT-4’s cl100k_base about 100k; Llama 3 (2024) 128,256; Qwen’s family roughly 152k; the o200k family and Gemma about 200k–256k. (Exact counts vary by release; treat these as a snapshot.) Vocabularies grew 4–5× in five years. Why — and why stop there?

The benefit of a bigger vocabulary is compression: more stored fragments means more text per token, especially for code, rare words, and non-English languages. Llama 3’s jump from 32k to 128k cut its tokens-per-text by up to 15% (Meta’s reported figure), with larger gains on underserved languages — every percentage point is 1% more effective context length, 1% fewer forward passes per document, 1% lower serving cost. The cost of a bigger vocabulary is parameters at the model’s edges, and it is worth computing honestly.

✏️Worked example

The embedding table has one row of dimension ddd per vocabulary entry. For a Llama-3-class model with V=128,256V = 128{,}256V=128,256 and d=4096d = 4096d=4096:

128,256×4096  =  525,336,576  ≈  525M parameters.128{,}256 \times 4096 \;=\; 525{,}336{,}576 \;\approx\; 525\text{M parameters.}128,256×4096=525,336,576≈525M parameters.

The output layer needs a d×Vd \times Vd×V matrix of the same size, and Llama-3-8B does not share (“tie”) the two, so vocabulary machinery costs about 1.051.051.05B parameters — roughly 13% of an 8-billion-parameter model spent before any actual thinking occurs. Small models often tie input and output matrices to halve this. For a 400B-parameter model the same 1.05B is 0.3% — which is why bigger models can afford bigger vocabularies, a scaling observation made quantitative in Chapter 32.

There is a subtler cost, too: each new token appears less often in a fixed corpus (the long tail thins out), so its embedding gets fewer gradient updates and is learned less well. Push vocabulary size far enough and you are minting tokens whose training signal approaches zero — which is the nursery where glitch tokens are born, as we will see shortly. The optimum, therefore, moves with model and data scale, and 100k–256k is simply where frontier labs’ curves currently cross (128k-class remains the most common choice in open models as of 2026).

∑The math

Tokenizer choice also breaks naive model comparisons, which is why Chapter 21 will insist on tokenizer-independent units. If model A’s tokenizer yields NAN_ANA​ tokens for a document and model B’s yields NB≠NAN_B \ne N_ANB​=NA​, their per-token cross-entropies are incomparable — model A can “improve” its per-token loss by tokenizing more coarsely. The fix is to normalize by something both share: the bytes of the underlying text. With cross-entropy ℓ\ellℓ in nats per token, tokens NNN, and bytes BBB,

bits-per-byte  =  NB⋅ℓln⁡2,\text{bits-per-byte} \;=\; \frac{N}{B} \cdot \frac{\ell}{\ln 2},bits-per-byte=BN​⋅ln2ℓ​,

where N/BN/BN/B is tokens-per-byte (the inverse of compression) and ln⁡2\ln 2ln2 converts nats to bits. Two models with different tokenizers can be compared on bits-per-byte because both are ultimately predicting the same bytes.

When tokenization bites: a field guide to pathologies

Everything strange in this section follows from one sentence: the model sees token IDs, not characters. Hold onto that and each pathology becomes a mechanism instead of a mystery.

Counting the r’s in strawberry. Ask a 2023–24-era model how many r’s are in “strawberry” and it would confidently answer two. The mechanism: the model receives strawberry as one or two token IDs — opaque atoms. It has no perceptual access to the letters inside a token, any more than you can count the strokes in a Chinese character you cannot read. Everything a model knows about spelling is memorized side information absorbed from training text (spelling bees, dictionaries, rhymes, typo corrections) and imperfectly attached to each token’s embedding. The information usually survives — models can often spell words letter-by-letter when asked — but operating on that recalled spelling (counting, reversing) chains two fragile steps, and errors compound. Post-2024 frontier models mostly pass, partly through reasoning training that teaches them to spell the word out into separate letter tokens first — converting a perception problem into a sequence problem they can do.

Numbers and arithmetic. GPT-2’s frequency-driven merges carved numbers arbitrarily: 1000 one token, 1001 two (100 + 1), forcing the model to learn separate arithmetic facts for overlapping chunkings. Modern tokenizers impose sanity by rule rather than statistics: the GPT-4-family and Llama 3 tokenizers cap number runs at three digits per token, and DeepSeek goes further, splitting every number into single digits — trading sequence length for the clean place-value structure that makes digit-by-digit algorithms learnable. Grouping direction matters too: three-digit tokens grouped right-to-left align with how positional arithmetic carries, and researchers have measured real accuracy differences from this choice alone. Tokenization is one honest reason “how much is 7,377 × 8,591” was, for years, a harder question for a language model than “explain the Krebs cycle”.

Multilingual inequity. A tokenizer’s merges are elected by its training corpus. Train on mostly-English web text and English gets luxuriously short encodings while other scripts — especially non-Latin scripts underrepresented in the data — fall back toward bytes. Recall that UTF-8 already charges Thai, Hindi, or Burmese three bytes per character before the tokenizer even votes. The result, documented across commercial tokenizers (Petrov et al., 2023), is that the same content can cost a few times more tokens in Hindi or Burmese than in English — with older English-centric tokenizers, an order of magnitude more. This is not cosmetic: tokens are the unit of API billing, of context capacity, and of the compute budget per thought. A Burmese speaker pays more per sentence, fits less history in context, and gets fewer effective “steps of computation” per idea. Larger multilingual vocabularies (Llama 3’s 128k, the ~200k+ vocabularies of GPT-4o-era and Gemma tokenizers) have narrowed but not closed the gap.

Glitch tokens: the SolidGoldMagikarp story. In early 2023, Jessica Rumbelow and Matthew Watkins went looking for tokens near the centroid of GPT’s embedding space and found a cluster of bizarre entries — SolidGoldMagikarp, petertodd, cloneembedreportprint — that made GPT-3 models behave deranged: asked to repeat ” SolidGoldMagikarp”, models would evade or hallucinate a different word (“distribute”), and sibling glitch tokens drew outright insults — StreamerBot reliably got back “You’re a jerk.” The mechanism is a supply chain bug between two training runs. The tokenizer was trained on one corpus — which happened to include masses of Reddit r/counting logs, where user SolidGoldMagikarp was a prolific counter — so the string earned its own token. The model was then trained on a differently-filtered corpus where those logs were largely absent. Result: a vocabulary entry whose embedding row received almost no gradient updates, left near its random initialization. Feeding that ID to the model injects an out-of-distribution vector into layer one — formally valid, semantically nonsense, like a word from a language that does not exist. Downstream layers do something, and that something is undefined behavior. Later tokenizers and better tokenizer/corpus alignment have mostly (not entirely) eliminated the species; the deeper lesson — vocabulary entries are only as meaningful as their training signal — is permanent, and glitch tokens remain a favorite probe in interpretability work.

Prompt-boundary sensitivity. Because Paris (with leading space) and Paris are different tokens, the end of your prompt interacts with tokenization in ways users never suspect. Complete “The capital of France is” and the model happily continues with the common token Paris. Add a trailing space — “The capital of France is ” — and now producing Paris would double the space; the model must instead emit the space-less Paris, a token that almost never begins a continuation in training data, and quality measurably degrades. The same boundary effect means a word split mid-token at the prompt’s edge (“The capital of France is Par”) asks the model to complete from a token boundary it has rarely seen. Serving stacks patch this with token healing: back up over the prompt’s final partial token and constrain generation to continuations consistent with the characters typed. It works, and the fact that it must exist is the indictment: the interface between “text you typed” and “sequence the model sees” leaks.

💡Intuition

Every pathology above is the same pathology. Tokenization is a lossy, frozen, statistics-driven lens bolted onto the model’s eye before training ever starts. Where the lens is aligned with the task (common English prose), you forget it exists. Where it is misaligned — characters inside tokens, digits across token boundaries, languages the lens barely saw, IDs the model never trained on, boundaries mid-word — the model is not being stupid; it is being asked about a world it literally cannot see.

The tokenizer-free dream

Given that catalogue, the obvious question: why not delete the tokenizer and feed the model raw bytes? The vocabulary shrinks to 256, every pathology above dissolves by construction — no hidden letters, no digit chunking, no language lens, no glitch IDs, no boundaries. The blocker is the arithmetic from the start of this chapter: byte sequences are ~4–5× longer, attention is quadratic, and every byte gets the same full-model forward pass whether it is the predictable second half of “the” or the uncertain first letter of a new sentence. Early attempts (ByT5, CANINE in 2021; MegaByte’s fixed-size byte patches in 2023) confirmed both the appeal and the cost: byte models were more robust to typos and spelling tasks but paid too much compute to match subword models at equal budgets.

The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) (Meta, December 2024) is the first result that makes the dream look practical, and its central idea is lovely: spend compute where the bytes are hard. A small byte-level model estimates the entropy of each next byte — exactly the “surprise” of Chapter 7 — and patch boundaries are drawn where entropy spikes (word starts, odd names, code identifiers), grouping predictable runs into long patches. A large latent transformer then operates over these variable-length patches — dynamic, learned tokens — with lightweight byte-level encoder/decoder models on either side. Where BPE allocates its budget by frequency, frozen forever at tokenizer-training time, BLT allocates compute by information content, per input, at runtime.

The honest scorecard, per the paper: at 8B parameters trained on 4T bytes, BLT matches Llama 3’s benchmark performance at equal training compute (flop-controlled), with better robustness to character noise, stronger spelling/character-level manipulation, and gains on low-resource translation; the authors also report the first flop-controlled scaling study of byte models to that scale, with trends favoring patches as models grow. The caveats are equally real: 8B is a generation behind frontier scale, the entropy-model machinery complicates training and serving pipelines that a decade of tooling has optimized around fixed tokenizers, and inference-time batching of variable patch boundaries is unsolved engineering. As of 2026 no frontier production model has shipped tokenizer-free, and every model you can rent today still speaks BPE — but for the first time the field’s answer to “could we delete the tokenizer?” is “at 8B scale, verifiably yes,” and several labs’ research groups are pulling the thread. Watch the frontier chapter for updates.

🕰History

The arc in one paragraph: Gage (1994) invents BPE to compress files; Schuster & Nakajima (2012) build WordPiece for Japanese/Korean voice search; Sennrich, Haddow & Birch (2015–16) import BPE to neural machine translation and end the unknown-word era; Kudo (2018) inverts it with Unigram and ships SentencePiece; GPT-2 (2019) goes byte-level and sets the frontier default; 2019–2024 is quiet consolidation — vocabularies grow 50k → 100k → 128k → 200k+ and digit rules get sane — and then BLT (2024) makes tokenizer-free credible again. Thirty years, one loop: compression trick → linguistic tool → infrastructure → suspected bottleneck.

Where this leaves us

  • You can state why tokenization exists: a finite output menu is structurally required, and subwords are the compromise between word-level OOV failure and character-level sequence explosion (~19× attention compute in our worked example).
  • You can explain Unicode vs UTF-8 in two sentences, and why a 256-byte base alphabet makes the unknown token impossible by construction.
  • You can train a BPE tokenizer by hand — count pairs, merge the max, repeat — and encode unseen words by replaying merges in order (lowest → low + est_).
  • You can contrast the three families: BPE merges by frequency, WordPiece merges by likelihood gain (the PMI-style ratio), Unigram starts huge and prunes by likelihood — bottom-up versus top-down.
  • You know what special tokens do, where chat “roles” physically live, and why user text must never encode into a special token.
  • You can compute what a vocabulary costs (128k×4096≈525M128\text{k} \times 4096 \approx 525\text{M}128k×4096≈525M embedding parameters, ~13% of an 8B model with untied output) and what it buys (compression, context, cost), and why the optimum grows with model scale.
  • You can give the mechanism, not just the meme, for strawberry-counting, digit chunking, multilingual token inequity, glitch tokens, and trailing-space degradation — and you know bits-per-byte is the tokenizer-independent yardstick, formalized in Chapter 21.
  • You know where tokenizer-free stands: BLT’s entropy-based patches match BPE at 8B scale, and the frontier hasn’t switched — yet.

Each token ID now needs to become something a neural network can compute with: a vector, positioned in a space where geometry is meaning. That lookup table we kept mentioning is the subject of the next chapter: Next: Embeddings.
