The Transformer, Piece by Piece

Assemble attention, MLPs, norms, and residuals into the full transformer — then count every one of GPT-2's 124 million parameters by hand and scale the same arithmetic to Llama-3-8B.

You have now met every component of a transformer separately: attention that lets tokens exchange information, run in parallel heads; the humble multi-layer perceptron; normalization layers that keep activations tame; residual connections that keep gradients flowing. What you have not yet seen is the whole machine — how these parts snap together, in what order, and why that particular arrangement, repeated a few dozen times, is the architecture behind nearly every frontier model of the 2020s.

This is the assembly chapter. There is almost no new math in it, and that is the point: a transformer is not a new idea so much as a composition of ideas you already own. What is new is the discipline of tracking a batch of tokens through the full pipeline — knowing the shape of the data at every arrow, and knowing where every parameter lives. By the end you should be able to do two things from memory: draw the architecture on a napkin, and — more unusual — count its parameters on the same napkin. We will do that counting twice: once for GPT-2 small, reproducing its famous 124M total exactly, and once for a Llama-3-8B-shaped model, landing within rounding distance of 8.03 billion.

One warning about maps and territories. The “transformer” in the 2017 paper “Attention Is All You Need” and the “transformer” inside a 2026 frontier model differ in nearly every local detail — where the norms go, what the norms are, what the activation is, how position is encoded — while keeping the same skeleton. We will build the modern decoder-only block first, because that is what runs the world, then walk through the original encoder–decoder design, both for historical honesty and because pieces of it survive in translation systems, speech models, and embedding models. A closing table collects everything that changed between 2017 and 2026, with pointers to the chapters that explain each change.

Two operations, alternating

Strip away every detail and a decoder-only transformer is startlingly small:

  1. Turn each token id into a vector (embedding).
  2. Alternate two operations, over and over: attention, then a feed-forward network (FFN).
  3. Turn the final vectors back into a probability distribution over the vocabulary (unembedding).

The alternation in step 2 is the heart of it, and the two operations have complementary jobs. Attention is the only place where information moves between positions: token 7 can read from token 3. But attention, on its own, mostly moves and mixes vectors — its per-position processing power is limited. The FFN is the opposite: it applies the same multi-layer perceptron (MLP) to each position independently, with no communication between positions at all — but with a lot of nonlinear processing capacity per position.

💡Intuition

Think of it as communicate, then compute. In the attention step, every token looks around the sequence and gathers whatever context it needs — “I’m the word ‘bank’, and three tokens ago someone said ‘river’.” In the FFN step, each token retreats to its own office and thinks about what it gathered — transforming “bank + river-context” into something like “riverbank, a geographical feature”. Then the cycle repeats, a few dozen times. Neither operation alone would work: all communication with no computation is a rumor mill; all computation with no communication is a room of people who have never read the sentence they are in.

Everything rides on the residual stream, the concept from the residual connections chapter: each operation does not replace the token’s vector but adds an update to it, so the vector at position iii accumulates contributions from every attention and FFN layer it passes through. That additive structure is what lets gradients flow through dozens of layers (Chapter 12 explains why), and it is also the load-bearing metaphor of the entire interpretability part of this book: layers read from and write to a shared communication channel.

Throughout this chapter we track a sequence of nnn tokens, each represented by a vector of dimension ddd (the model dimension, also called dmodeld_{\text{model}}dmodel​ or “hidden size” or “width”). The data flowing through the model is therefore an n×dn \times dn×d matrix — one row per token — and the single most useful habit you can build is asking, at every arrow of every diagram, “what shape is the tensor here?” For almost every arrow in this chapter the answer is n×dn \times dn×d, and the exceptions are exactly the interesting places.

One block, arrow by arrow

Here is the modern decoder-only block — the unit that Llama, Qwen, DeepSeek, Gemma, and (as far as public evidence suggests) the closed frontier models stack a few dozen to over a hundred times:

Let us walk one forward pass through this block slowly, with real shapes. Take a 6-token prompt (“The cat sat on the mat”, roughly) flowing through a Llama-3-8B-sized model, so n=6n = 6n=6 and d=4096d = 4096d=4096. The input to the block is a 6×40966 \times 40966×4096 matrix xxx — six rows, one per token, each row that token’s current residual-stream vector.

Arrow 1: into the first norm. We take a copy of xxx and apply root-mean-square normalization (RMSNorm) to each row independently: rescale the row to a standard length, then multiply elementwise by a learned gain vector. Shape: 6×40966 \times 40966×4096 in, 6×40966 \times 40966×4096 out. The residual stream itself is not overwritten — the original xxx continues along the bottom of the figure, untouched. This is pre-norm: normalize on the way into the sublayer, not after the addition. The 2017 original did it the other way (post-norm), and the difference matters enormously for trainability — we return to this below.

Arrow 2: through attention. The normalized copy enters masked multi-head attention. Inside, each of 32 heads projects the rows into queries, keys, and values, computes softmax⁡(QK⊤/dk)V\softmax(QK\T/\sqrt{d_k})Vsoftmax(QK⊤/dk​​)V with a causal mask so each token attends only to itself and earlier positions, and the concatenated head outputs are mapped back to width 4096 by the output projection. (Position information enters here too, via rotary position embeddings applied to the queries and keys — next chapter’s topic.) Shape: 6×40966 \times 40966×4096 in, 6×40966 \times 40966×4096 out. From outside the box, attention is a shape-preserving update whose content for token iii depends on tokens 1..i1..i1..i.

Arrow 3: add it back. The attention output is added, elementwise, to the unmodified xxx: h=x+attention outputh = x + \text{attention output}h=x+attention output. Still 6×40966 \times 40966×4096. Token 6’s row now contains its original content plus whatever it gathered from tokens 1–5.

Arrows 4–6: norm, FFN, add. The second half mirrors the first: a fresh RMSNorm of hhh (its own learned gain — the two norms in a block share nothing), then the feed-forward network, which processes each of the 6 rows independently through the same MLP — up to a hidden width of 14,336, gated nonlinearity, back down to 4096 — and the result is added to hhh. Output: yyy, shape 6×40966 \times 40966×4096, handed to the next block, which does exactly the same thing with its own weights.

The whole block is two lines. With N1,N2\mathrm{N}_1, \mathrm{N}_2N1​,N2​ the two RMSNorms (each with its own gain vector), Attn\mathrm{Attn}Attn masked multi-head attention, and FFN\mathrm{FFN}FFN the feed-forward network:

Unrolled over NNN blocks, the final vector for a token is its embedding plus 2N2N2N additive updates — which is why Chapter 16 described the network as a shallow ensemble of many paths. For the norm itself, recall from Chapter 14 that for a row vector u∈Rdu \in \R^du∈Rd, RMSNorm(u)=u1d∑i=1dui2+ϵ⊙g\mathrm{RMSNorm}(u) = \frac{u}{\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d} u_i^2 + \epsilon}} \odot gRMSNorm(u)=d1​∑i=1d​ui2​+ϵ​u​⊙g, where g∈Rdg \in \R^dg∈Rd is the learned gain and ϵ\epsilonϵ (typically 10−510^{-5}10−5 or 10−610^{-6}10−6) avoids division by zero. ⚠️Careful

The most common drawing mistake is putting the norm in the residual path — normalizing x+Attn(…)x + \mathrm{Attn}(\ldots)x+Attn(…) before it continues onward. That is post-norm, the 2017 design, and it is not what modern models do. In pre-norm, nothing ever stands between a block’s input and its output except two additions; the norms sit on the branches. This is why gradients reach layer 1 of a 100-layer model intact, and why post-norm models needed delicate learning-rate warmup to avoid diverging in the first steps — Xiong et al. (2020) traced exactly how post-norm scales gradients badly at initialization. If your hand-drawn transformer has a norm on the horizontal line, redraw it.

✎Note

A small refinement you will meet in recent models: some (Gemma 2 and 3, several others) add a second pair of norms after each sublayer’s output, before the addition — “sandwich” norm — and several 2025-era models (Qwen3, Gemma 3, OLMo 2) apply an extra “QK-norm” — an RMSNorm of the queries and keys — inside attention. These are stability patches on the same skeleton, not architectural changes; the two-line equation above still describes the data flow.

The feed-forward network: where most of the model lives

Attention gets the fame, but by parameter count the transformer is mostly feed-forward network. It deserves a section of its own.

The FFN is exactly the two-layer MLP of Chapter 8, applied to each position’s vector independently and identically — the same weights for every position, the same weights at every step of generation. The classic version, used from 2017 through roughly GPT-3, expands the vector by a factor of 4, applies a nonlinearity, and contracts back:

FFN(u)=W2 ϕ(W1u),W1∈R4d×d,  W2∈Rd×4d,\mathrm{FFN}(u) = W_2\, \phi(W_1 u), \qquad W_1 \in \R^{4d \times d},\; W_2 \in \R^{d \times 4d},FFN(u)=W2​ϕ(W1​u),W1​∈R4d×d,W2​∈Rd×4d,

where ϕ\phiϕ was the rectified linear unit (ReLU) in the original paper and the Gaussian error linear unit (GELU) in the GPT series (Chapter 15 covers both). The modern version — in Llama, Qwen, DeepSeek, Mistral, and nearly everyone else (Gemma uses the sibling GeGLU: same gating, with GELU as the activation) — is SwiGLU, a gated variant proposed by Shazeer (2020):

∑The math

SwiGLU uses three weight matrices instead of two. For hidden width mmm:

FFN(u)  =  Wdown(SiLU(Wgate u)⏟gate  ⊙  Wup u⏟candidate),\mathrm{FFN}(u) \;=\; W_{\text{down}}\Big( \underbrace{\mathrm{SiLU}(W_{\text{gate}}\, u)}_{\text{gate}} \;\odot\; \underbrace{W_{\text{up}}\, u}_{\text{candidate}} \Big),FFN(u)=Wdown​(gateSiLU(Wgate​u)​​⊙candidateWup​u​​),

where Wgate,Wup∈Rm×dW_{\text{gate}}, W_{\text{up}} \in \R^{m \times d}Wgate​,Wup​∈Rm×d, Wdown∈Rd×mW_{\text{down}} \in \R^{d \times m}Wdown​∈Rd×m, ⊙\odot⊙ is elementwise multiplication, and SiLU(z)=z σ(z)\mathrm{SiLU}(z) = z\,\sigma(z)SiLU(z)=zσ(z) is the sigmoid-weighted linear unit (“Swish”). Two parallel projections produce a candidate signal and a gate; the gate is squashed through SiLU and multiplies the candidate elementwise — each hidden unit can be smoothly turned up or shut off depending on the input. Because there are now three matrices instead of two, implementations that want to keep the parameter count of the old 4d4d4d design shrink the hidden width to m=23⋅4d≈2.67dm = \tfrac{2}{3} \cdot 4d \approx 2.67dm=32​⋅4d≈2.67d, which keeps the total at 8d28d^28d2. Why does the FFN hold most of the parameters, and what does it do with them? A line of interpretability work beginning with Geva et al. (2021) offers a compelling reading: the FFN is a key–value memory. Each row of WgateW_{\text{gate}}Wgate​ / WupW_{\text{up}}Wup​ is a key — a pattern detector that fires when the incoming residual vector points in a particular direction (“this looks like a sentence about a capital city”, “this is the middle of a common idiom”). Each corresponding column of WdownW_{\text{down}}Wdown​ is a value — a vector that gets written into the residual stream, scaled by how strongly its key fired. A hidden width of 14,336 means 14,336 pattern→update rules per layer, and the model has hundreds of thousands of them across its depth. Concretely, Geva et al. found lower-layer keys firing on shallow surface patterns and upper-layer keys on semantic ones, with values that often directly push probability toward specific next tokens. This picture — directions as detectors, FFNs as banks of stored associations — is a preview of Part VII, where it becomes the working model for how facts live inside large language models (LLMs) (and why there are more “rules” than neurons to store them in).

💡Intuition

So the alternation gets a sharper reading: attention routes, the FFN looks things up. Attention’s matrices are all d×dd \times dd×d-ish — they decide where information should move. The FFN’s fat d×md \times md×m matrices are where the knowledge is — a huge associative memory consulted independently by every token, every layer. When we count parameters below and find roughly two-thirds of the non-embedding weights sitting in FFNs, you can read that as: two-thirds of a transformer’s capacity is storage. This is also exactly why mixture-of-experts models sparsify the FFN rather than attention — it is where the bulk is.

From token ids to a probability distribution

Blocks are the middle of the story. The full pipeline — what happens when a prompt enters and a next-token prediction leaves — bookends them with the embedding machinery of Chapter 20:

In symbols, with vocabulary size ∣V∣|V|∣V∣: tokenization turns text into nnn integer ids; the embedding matrix WE∈R∣V∣×dW_E \in \R^{|V| \times d}WE​∈R∣V∣×d turns each id into a row vector (a lookup — id 7,312 fetches row 7,312); the NNN blocks transform the resulting n×dn \times dn×d matrix; a final RMSNorm cleans up the residual stream; and the unembedding matrix WU∈Rd×∣V∣W_U \in \R^{d \times |V|}WU​∈Rd×∣V∣ (also called the “LM head”) maps each position’s vector to ∣V∣|V|∣V∣ logits — one score per vocabulary token. Softmax, which you have known since Chapter 6, turns scores into probabilities; during training the cross-entropy loss compares each position’s distribution against the actual next token, and backpropagation with AdamW or a successor does the rest.

Two details are worth pinning down. First, where does position information enter? In GPT-2 it entered right at the start: a learned position-embedding matrix (context length × ddd) added to the token embeddings before block 1. In modern models using rotary position embeddings (RoPE) there is no position vector added at the input at all — the token embedding is all you get, and position is injected inside every attention layer by rotating queries and keys. That is the next chapter; for now, know that the pipeline figure above is complete for a Llama-class model.

Second, weight tying. Notice that WEW_EWE​ maps tokens→vectors and WUW_UWU​ maps vectors→tokens; they have transposed shapes. GPT-2 tied them — WU=WE⊤W_U = W_E\TWU​=WE⊤​, one matrix doing both jobs — saving 38.6M parameters. Most large modern models untie them (Llama-3-8B and 70B do), because at scale the saving is proportionally small and the two jobs benefit from different geometry; small models (Gemma, Llama-3.2-1B) often still tie. Remember this for the parameter count: tied unembedding costs zero extra parameters.

The original: encoder, decoder, and the bridge between them

Now that you can draw the 2026 block, the 2017 original will read like a period photograph — same family, different styling. The transformer was born as a machine translation model, and translation shaped its anatomy: an encoder reads the entire source sentence, a decoder generates the target sentence one token at a time, and a bridge called cross-attention lets the decoder consult the encoder’s output at every step. This is the architecture of the seq2seq chapter, rebuilt with attention everywhere and recurrence nowhere.

Three differences from the modern block deserve explicit narration.

The encoder attends bidirectionally. The encoder’s job is to understand a complete input, not to generate, so there is no causal mask: every source token attends to every other, in both directions. Its block is otherwise the familiar pair — self-attention sublayer, FFN sublayer, each wrapped in a residual connection.

The decoder has three sublayers, not two. First masked self-attention over what it has generated so far (this is the part that survives as the modern decoder-only block). Then cross-attention: queries computed from the decoder’s own state, but keys and values computed from the encoder’s output. Mechanically it is the identical softmax⁡(QK⊤/dk)V\softmax(QK\T/\sqrt{d_k})Vsoftmax(QK⊤/dk​​)V machinery from Chapter 23 — the only change is whose vectors feed the K and V projections. This is the bridge: at every generation step, every decoder layer gets to ask questions of the fully-processed source sentence. Then the FFN, as usual.

Norms come after, not before. Each sublayer is wrapped as LayerNorm(x+Sublayer(x))\mathrm{LayerNorm}(x + \mathrm{Sublayer}(x))LayerNorm(x+Sublayer(x)) — “add & norm”, post-norm, with the original LayerNorm (mean subtraction, bias and gain) rather than RMSNorm. And position enters as sinusoidal vectors added to the embeddings at the bottom of each tower.

🕰History

“Attention Is All You Need” (Vaswani et al., 2017) was a machine-translation paper from eight authors at Google — the title a deliberate provocation aimed at the recurrent networks (Chapter 17) that then owned sequence modeling. The base model was about 65M parameters, the big one about 213M; they set new state-of-the-art scores on the BLEU translation-quality metric for English→German and English→French while training in days rather than weeks, because — with recurrence gone — every position could be processed in parallel on graphics processing units (GPUs). The paper’s ablations already contained the seeds of much that followed: multi-head vs single-head, the dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ scaling, dropout rates. What nobody quite foresaw was that the decoder half alone, scaled a thousandfold on next-token prediction, would become the general-purpose learner of the following decade. All eight authors have since left Google, most to found AI companies — a fact people enjoy citing as a lesson about something, though there is no agreement about what.

Where does each variant live in 2026? The decoder-only stack dominates: every major LLM. Encoder-only stacks (BERT’s lineage) survive wherever you want one vector representing a text rather than a continuation of it — embedding models for retrieval, rerankers, classifiers. Encoder–decoder survives where the input is a different modality or cleanly separate from the output: Whisper encodes audio and decodes text; translation systems and T5-descended models still use it; and cross-attention specifically reappears inside some multimodal models to let a language model consult image features. The full taxonomy, and why decoder-only won, is Chapter 27.

Counting every parameter

Now the promised centerpiece. Being able to count a model’s parameters from its config is the cheapest superpower in this book: it turns “8B” from a marketing number into an arithmetic fact you can verify, it tells you where capacity sits, and it is the foundation for the scaling-law and training-cost reasoning of Part V.

Attention is 22.8% of the total, the FFNs 45.5%. And the norms — all 25 of them — contribute 38,400 parameters, 0.03%: whenever you are estimating, drop them.

Scaling the same arithmetic to Llama-3-8B

Where the parameters live is the takeaway worth memorizing. In Llama-3-8B: FFNs 32×176.2M=5.6432 \times 176.2\text{M} = 5.6432×176.2M=5.64B — 70% of the entire model (81% of the non-embedding weights). Attention: 1.34B, about 17%. Embedding plus unembedding: 1.05B, 13%. The rule of thumb “roughly two-thirds of a transformer is feed-forward network” is conservative for modern configs; GQA and wide SwiGLU push it higher. This is the quantitative backing for the key–value-memory picture above — most of what you download when you download a model is the FFNs’ stored associations — and it is why mixture-of-experts models get such an edge from making only the FFN sparse.

⚠️Careful

Two counting traps. First, “active” vs total parameters: for dense models like the two above every parameter participates in every token, but mixture-of-experts (MoE) models advertise both numbers (DeepSeek-V3: 671B total, ≈37B active), and comparing a dense model’s total to an MoE’s total misleads. Second, embedding params do little compute: the embedding lookup is free (indexing, not multiplication) and the unembedding is one matmul, so scaling-law papers often report non-embedding parameters — Kaplan et al.’s cleaner curves used exactly that. When two sources disagree about a model’s size, check which convention each is using.

Depth, width, and what changed 2017 → 2026

Given a parameter budget, the architect’s remaining dials are depth NNN (more blocks: more sequential steps of read-think-write, more opportunity for composed circuits) and width ddd (a roomier residual stream: more features carried simultaneously, and — since parameters grow as d2d^2d2 per block but only linearly with NNN — the faster way to add capacity). Wider also runs more efficiently on GPUs, which love big matrix multiplications; deeper runs slower per token, since blocks execute in sequence, and historically trained less stably (pre-norm and careful initialization were what tamed depth).

The empirical surprise, reported in Kaplan et al.’s scaling-law study, is how little the shape matters once the total count is fixed: over a wide range, loss depends overwhelmingly on total parameters, weakly on the depth-to-width split. So the field settled into a comfortable corridor — aspect ratio d/Nd/Nd/N of roughly 64–130:

⚙️In practice

If you want the 2026 defaults as one sentence for drawing from memory: pre-norm RMSNorm → attention with RoPE and GQA → add → pre-norm RMSNorm → SwiGLU FFN → add, no biases, no dropout, repeated N≈d/128N \approx d/128N≈d/128 times between an untied embedding and unembedding. That describes Llama 3, Qwen, and Mistral; Gemma tweaks it (GeGLU, tied embeddings, sandwich norms), and DeepSeek-V3 swaps in MLA and MoE — all to within the small per-model refinements each technical report lists in a paragraph. The skeleton has been stable since about 2023; the churn moved on to data, post-training, and inference.

Where this leaves us

  • You can draw the modern decoder-only block from memory: residual stream untouched along the bottom, two branches — RMSNorm → masked attention → add, RMSNorm → SwiGLU FFN → add — with every arrow carrying n×dn \times dn×d.
  • You can name the division of labor: attention is the only cross-position communication; the FFN is per-position computation — and, on the key–value memory reading, the model’s main knowledge store.
  • You can trace the full pipeline: token ids → embedding rows → NNN blocks → final norm → unembedding → softmax, with a next-token distribution at every position.
  • You can reconstruct the 2017 encoder–decoder, explain cross-attention (decoder queries, encoder keys and values), and say where each variant still earns its keep.
  • You can count parameters from a config sheet: 4d24d^24d2 classic attention, 8d28d^28d2 classic FFN, ≈12d2\approx 12d^2≈12d2 per block; and you have verified GPT-2 small’s 124,439,808 and Llama-3-8B’s 8.03B by hand.
  • You know where the weights live (two-thirds or more in FFNs at modern scale) and the standard depth–width corridor (d/Nd/Nd/N roughly 64–130).
  • You can recite what changed 2017 → 2026 — pre-norm, RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE, GQA/MLA, MoE, decoder-only — and point at why.

One box in our diagrams is still half-open: we waved at “RoPE rotates the queries and keys” without explaining how a rotation can possibly encode position. That story — from the sinusoids of 2017 to the rotary embeddings in every modern model, and what they mean for long context — is next: Next: Positional Information and RoPE.
