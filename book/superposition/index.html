Superposition and Polysemanticity

Why single neurons fire for many unrelated things, the superposition hypothesis that explains it, and the toy model whose phase transitions and pentagons made the whole story precise.

  5. Privileged bases, or: why neurons were a reasonable guess

The previous chapter made a bet: concepts live in activation space as directions, and we can find them. There is an obvious first strategy that the bet seems to suggest. A network’s activations arrive organized into coordinates — neuron 1, neuron 2, neuron 3 — so why not read those? Pick a neuron, find the inputs that make it fire, and write down what it means. Do that for every neuron and you have a dictionary of the model’s concepts. Interpretability becomes a large but straightforward cataloguing project.

People tried. The strategy fails, and it fails in a specific, maddening way: most neurons almost mean something. The typical finding looks like this: a neuron fires strongly for text about music theory — and also for base64-encoded strings. Another activates on descriptions of national parks — and also on golf commentary and code syntax. The failure is not noise or randomness; each neuron’s list of triggers is stable and reproducible. It is not one thing. This phenomenon is called polysemanticity, and it is the single biggest obstacle between “models represent features as directions” and “we can enumerate those features.”

This chapter is about the leading explanation for polysemanticity: the superposition hypothesis. The claim is that a model with nnn dimensions represents many more than nnn features by storing them along almost-orthogonal directions, deliberately tolerating a little interference between them — and that this is not a bug but an optimal compression strategy when features are sparse. We will build the geometric argument from high-dimensional geometry, then walk through the paper that turned the hypothesis into something you can watch happen in a two-dimensional model: Anthropic’s Toy Models of Superposition. By the end you will understand the slogan that organizes all of Part VII: features are not neurons — and you will see exactly why the field had to invent the machinery of the next chapter to get the features back out.

Neurons that mean too many things

First, the evidence. Polysemanticity was documented carefully in vision models before language models. In Zoom In: An Introduction to Circuits, Olah and collaborators catalogued neurons in InceptionV1, an image classifier. Many neurons were clean and specific — curve detectors, dog-head detectors, wheel detectors. But scattered among them were neurons like one in layer mixed4e that responds to cat faces, fronts of cars, and cat legs. Not “things that are furry,” not “vehicles” — three visually and semantically unrelated categories, reliably, in one unit.

Language models are worse. The Softmax Linear Units paper (SoLU) ran the same kind of survey on small transformers and found that typical neurons in later layers respond to grab-bags of unrelated triggers. The example that became the field’s standard illustration: a single neuron responding to academic citations, English dialogue, HTTP requests, and Korean text. Whatever this neuron is “for,” it is not for any one human concept.

Let us pin down terms, because the field uses them precisely:

  • A feature, as in the previous chapter, is a property of the input that the network represents as a direction in some activation space — “this text is French,” “this token is inside a string literal,” “the current clause is negated.”
  • A neuron is monosemantic if it activates for exactly one feature. Its activation is directly interpretable: read the value, know the feature.
  • A neuron is polysemantic if it activates for several unrelated features. Its activation is ambiguous: a high value could mean any of them.
⚠️Careful

Keep the observation and the explanation separate. Polysemanticity is an empirical fact: we look at neurons and find they fire for unrelated things. Superposition is a hypothesis about why: the model is representing more features than it has dimensions, so features cannot each get their own neuron. The hypothesis has strong theoretical and toy-model support, and the success of sparse autoencoders is evidence for it in real models — but other contributors to polysemanticity exist. A model with enough neurons could still end up with polysemantic ones by accident of training dynamics (sometimes called incidental polysemanticity), or because nothing in the loss ever asked it to align features with neurons. Superposition is the leading explanation, not a theorem about real models.

Why does polysemanticity matter so much? Because it breaks the unit of analysis. If neurons were monosemantic, understanding a model would be a scaling problem: label every neuron, then trace how labeled neurons connect into circuits. Tedious for millions of neurons, but parallelizable — the kind of tedious that automation handles. Polysemanticity means the labels do not exist. The question “what does neuron 4,731 do?” has no answer, in the same way “what does the letter e mean?” has no answer. The meaningful units are elsewhere, and we have to go find them.

The geometry of cramming

Here is the puzzle from the model’s point of view. A transformer’s residual stream might have d=4,096d = 4{,}096d=4,096 dimensions. How many features might a model want to represent? Count crudely: thousands of named entities, tens of thousands of word senses across dozens of languages, syntactic states, code constructs, facts, styles, topics, formats. Any reasonable count lands somewhere between hundreds of thousands and hundreds of millions — vastly more than 4,0964{,}0964,096.

Linear algebra says you can fit at most ddd mutually orthogonal directions in ddd dimensions. If features needed perfect orthogonality — zero crosstalk between them — the model would face a brutal choice: pick its 4,0964{,}0964,096 favorite features and discard everything else it knows.

But Chapter 3 showed the escape hatch, and it is worth restating because this is where it pays off. Orthogonality is a knife-edge condition; almost-orthogonality is abundant. If you only require pairwise dot products smaller than some tolerance ϵ\epsilonϵ (say, every pair of unit vectors within 85°85°85°–95°95°95° of each other rather than exactly 90°90°90°), then the number of directions you can fit grows exponentially in the dimension. This is the Johnson–Lindenstrauss phenomenon: the count scales roughly like ec ϵ2de^{c\,\epsilon^2 d}ecϵ2d for a modest constant ccc. For ddd in the thousands and ϵ≈0.1\epsilon \approx 0.1ϵ≈0.1, the exponent is in the tens or hundreds — room for astronomically more almost-orthogonal directions than orthogonal ones. (The constant matters for exact counts; the exponential shape is the point.)

So a model could assign each of a million features its own direction in a 4,0964{,}0964,096-dimensional space, with every pair of directions nearly but not exactly perpendicular. The price is interference: reading one feature picks up faint echoes of the others.

That second observation is the heart of the superposition hypothesis. Interference is not paid per feature stored; it is paid per feature simultaneously active. And real-world features are overwhelmingly sparse. Almost all text is not about golf. Almost all tokens are not Korean. Almost no sentence involves DNA sequences, and the rare one that does involves almost nothing else from the long tail. At any given token, out of the million features a model might track, perhaps a few dozen are meaningfully active.

💡Intuition

Superposition is an overbooking strategy, and it works for the same reason overbooking works. An airline sells more tickets than seats because passengers rarely all show up; a model stores more features than dimensions because features rarely all fire. Interference — two features needing the same representational “seat” — happens only when features co-occur, and if each feature is rare, co-occurrence is rare squared. The model accepts occasional small collisions in exchange for representing vastly more of the world. Compression by betting on sparsity.

We can push the intuition one step toward a derivation. Suppose each feature is active with probability ppp (the same for all, to keep it simple), and ask: should the model represent one more feature than it has dimensions, at the cost of some interference?

  • The benefit of representing an extra feature: when that feature occurs, the model no longer makes an error on it. Expected gain per input scales like ppp — linear in how often the feature appears.
  • The cost: interference between the new feature and an existing one corrupts the readout only when both are active, which happens with probability p2p^2p2 — quadratic.

As features get sparser (p→0p \to 0p→0), the linear benefit shrinks slower than the quadratic cost. Below some threshold sparsity, cramming wins: the model gains more from representing rare features at all than it loses from their rare collisions. Above the threshold — dense features that co-occur constantly — interference is ruinous, and the model should fall back to giving its few most important features clean, orthogonal storage. This predicts a switch in behavior as sparsity varies. As we will see, the switch is real, and it is sharp.

The toy model: superposition in a bottle

Everything so far is plausibility argument. The 2022 Anthropic paper Toy Models of Superposition (Elhage, Hume, Olsson, Schiefer et al.) turned it into an experiment small enough to solve almost exactly, and the rest of this chapter is a guided tour of it. The design philosophy: if superposition is a general strategy for storing sparse features in too few dimensions, it should emerge in the smallest possible model with those ingredients — no transformer required.

∑The math

The setup. Synthetic data x∈Rnx \in \R^nx∈Rn has one coordinate per feature. Each feature is independently inactive (exactly 000) with probability SSS — the sparsity — and otherwise uniform on [0,1][0,1][0,1]. Each feature also has an importance weight Ii>0I_i > 0Ii​>0, expressing how much the loss cares about it.

The model compresses xxx into a bottleneck h∈Rmh \in \R^mh∈Rm with m<nm < nm<n (the paper’s headline case: n=5n = 5n=5 features, m=2m = 2m=2 dimensions) and tries to reconstruct:

h=Wx,x^=ReLU(W⊤h+b),h = W x, \qquad \hat{x} = \mathrm{ReLU}(W\T h + b),h=Wx,x^=ReLU(W⊤h+b),

where W∈Rm×nW \in \R^{m \times n}W∈Rm×n is the trained weight matrix (note the same WWW compresses and reconstructs — a tied autoencoder) and b∈Rnb \in \R^nb∈Rn is a trained bias. The training loss is importance-weighted squared error:

L  =  Ex∑i=1nIi (xi−x^i)2.L \;=\; \E_x \sum_{i=1}^{n} I_i \,\bigl(x_i - \hat{x}_i\bigr)^2 .L=Ex​i=1∑n​Ii​(xi​−x^i​)2.

The matrix W⊤WW\T WW⊤W of all pairwise dot products Wi⊤WjW_i\T W_jWi⊤​Wj​ is the object to watch: its diagonal says how strongly each feature is represented (∥Wi∥2\lVert W_i \rVert^2∥Wi​∥2, with 000 meaning “not stored at all”), and its off-diagonal entries are exactly the interference terms from the previous section.

The experiment: train this model to convergence at many values of the sparsity SSS, and look at what WWW learned. Two regimes appear, with a sharp boundary between them.

Dense regime (SSS near 0: features usually all active). The model behaves like principal component analysis. It picks the mmm most important features, gives each its own orthogonal direction — a dedicated dimension — and stores nothing else. The other n−mn - mn−m features get ∥Wi∥=0\lVert W_i \rVert = 0∥Wi​∥=0: dropped entirely, taking the loss hit on every input. With everything active at once, interference would be constant and crippling, so the model refuses to share dimensions. This regime is monosemantic by construction: each bottleneck dimension carries one feature.

Sparse regime (SSS near 1: features rarely active). The model stores all the features — more features than dimensions — by arranging their directions in the almost-orthogonal configurations we anticipated: our pentagon, among others. Negative biases and the ReLU gate away the (now rare) interference. This regime is polysemantic: any basis dimension of the bottleneck has projections from many features, so “what does dimension 1 respond to?” has a long, unrelated-looking answer. Exactly the phenomenology of real neurons.

In between, the transition is not gradual. Individual features hop from “dropped” to “superposed” to “dedicated” at specific sparsity thresholds, and the paper shows that in a minimal two-feature version these hops are phase transitions — discontinuous changes in the optimal weights as the data statistics vary smoothly, directly analogous to ice-to-water.

Pentagons, digons, and unreasonable geometry

Inside the sparse regime, the toy model does something nobody asked it to do: it organizes features into crystalline geometric structures. With uniform importance, features do not smear into arbitrary near-orthogonal directions — they lock into regular polytopes. Two features share a dimension as an antipodal pair (a digon: directions www and −w-w−w). Three features in two dimensions form an equilateral triangle. Five form our pentagon; four features in three dimensions form a tetrahedron. As you vary sparsity, the model jumps between these configurations discretely, like a molecule snapping between conformations.

The paper quantifies this with a feature dimensionality: roughly, what fraction of a full dimension each feature gets, once you account for how much its direction is shared. In the antipodal pair each feature gets 1/21/21/2 a dimension; in the triangle, 2/32/32/3; the tetrahedron, 3/43/43/4; the pentagon, 2/52/52/5. When you plot dimensionality across a sparsity sweep, features cluster at exactly these fractions with sharp jumps between — the model is “rationing” dimensions in discrete units, and the polytopes are how the rations get delivered. (These are solutions to a classic packing problem — spreading points on a sphere to maximize minimum angle, known as the Thomson problem in another guise — that the optimizer stumbles into via gradient descent.)

Why should you care about pentagons in a toy? Two reasons. First, they are a fingerprint. If real models use superposition, feature directions recovered from them should show near-orthogonal, geometry-flavored packing rather than neuron alignment — and (previewing the sparse autoencoder chapter) that is what dictionary learning finds. Second, the discreteness matters: it means superposition is not a smooth blur but a structured code, which is precisely what gives dictionary-learning methods something crisp to recover.

✎Note

The toy model also contains stranger things we will not tour in full: non-uniform importance breaks the symmetric polytopes into lopsided configurations; correlated features prefer orthogonal storage (anti-correlated ones happily share as antipodal pairs, since they never co-occur); and a follow-up, Superposition, Memorization, and Double Descent, connects the same geometry to memorization of training examples. The paper rewards a full read — it is one of the field’s best-written artifacts.

Privileged bases, or: why neurons were a reasonable guess

There is a question we have been stepping around. Polysemanticity means features are not aligned with neurons. But why would features align with neurons even in the best case? What makes the coordinate axes of an activation space special at all?

The answer differs by location in the network, and the difference is one of the cleanest arguments in interpretability. Call a basis privileged if something in the architecture treats individual coordinates differently from arbitrary rotations of them — giving features a reason to align with axes.

The residual stream has no privileged basis. Recall from Chapter 16 that the residual stream is nothing more than a running sum that layers read from and write to with linear maps (and the near-linear RMSNorm). Suppose you pick any rotation matrix RRR (orthogonal, R⊤R=IR\T R = IR⊤R=I) and rewire the model: every matrix WinW_{\text{in}}Win​ that reads from the stream becomes WinR⊤W_{\text{in}} R\TWin​R⊤, every matrix WoutW_{\text{out}}Wout​ that writes to it becomes R WoutR\, W_{\text{out}}RWout​. Every read now sees WinR⊤(R h)=WinhW_{\text{in}} R\T (R\,h) = W_{\text{in}} hWin​R⊤(Rh)=Win​h — identical to before. The rewired model computes exactly the same function, but every vector in the residual stream has been rotated by RRR. Since nothing observable distinguishes the original from the rotated model, the individual coordinates of the residual stream can carry no intrinsic meaning. Asking “what does residual dimension 803 represent?” is like asking which direction is really north in deep space. Only directions-relative-to-learned-weights mean anything — which is why Chapter 49 probed for directions rather than reading coordinates.

Multi-layer perceptron (MLP) neurons have a partially privileged basis. Inside an MLP block, the hidden activation passes through an elementwise nonlinearity — ReLU, GELU, SiLU (Chapter 15) — applied to each coordinate separately. Try the rotation trick here and it fails: ReLU(R z)\mathrm{ReLU}(R\,z)ReLU(Rz) is generally not R ReLU(z)R\,\mathrm{ReLU}(z)RReLU(z) (rotate the vector (1,−1)(1, -1)(1,−1) by 45° and check: ReLU-then-rotate gives a different vector than rotate-then-ReLU). The nonlinearity singles out the coordinate axes: each neuron’s activation is individually gated at zero. That gives the model an incentive to align features with neurons — a feature aligned with an axis can be cleanly turned on and off by that neuron’s nonlinearity, while a feature diagonal to the axes gets awkwardly, jointly gated.

So the old hope was not silly: MLP neurons do live in a privileged basis, and some of them are monosemantic — the curve detectors of Zoom In are real. Privilege is an incentive, though, not a guarantee. When the model wants to track far more features than it has neurons, the pressure of superposition overwhelms the pressure of axis alignment, and features end up smeared across neurons anyway. The observed mix — some clean neurons, mostly polysemantic ones — is exactly what “privileged basis + superposition” predicts.

🕰History

This diagnosis suggested a seductive fix: strengthen the privilege. The 2022 SoLU paper replaced the MLP activation with a softmax-based unit designed to force axis-aligned, winner-take-most activations. It half-worked — many more neurons became human-interpretable — but the interpretability was partly an illusion: the LayerNorm needed to keep the model trainable let it hide features in small, smeared activations that evaded the winner-take-most pressure (“hidden” superposition). The lesson the field drew was sobering: you cannot easily architecture your way out of superposition, because the underlying pressure — more useful features than dimensions — does not go away. If the model benefits from cramming, it will find a place to cram.

⚠️Careful

A distinction that trips up careful readers: superposition is not the same thing as a distributed representation. Distributed representations — an idea from Hinton and the 1980s connectionists (Hinton, 1986) — mean each concept is encoded as a pattern across many units, rather than one unit per concept. You can have a fully distributed code with no superposition: nnn features stored along nnn perfectly orthogonal but axis-misaligned directions in Rn\R^nRn is distributed (every feature touches every neuron) yet interference-free, and a simple rotation would make it one-neuron-per-feature. Superposition is the strictly stronger claim that there are more features than dimensions — no rotation can ever disentangle them, because nnn directions in m<nm < nm<n dimensions cannot all be orthogonal. Distributed says features spread across neurons; superposition says features outnumber them. The second is what makes interpretability hard.

What superposition dooms, and what it demands

Take stock of the consequences, because they set the agenda for the rest of Part VII.

Neuron-level analysis is doomed as a general strategy. Not useless — privileged bases mean some neurons are clean, and finding them is cheap — but any plan of the form “understand the model by understanding each neuron” founders on the fact that the model’s own units of meaning do not respect the neuron grid. This is the slogan to carry forward: features are not neurons. Features are directions; neurons are coordinates; superposition is precisely the regime where the two cannot be reconciled.

The features are still there — recover them. Superposition does not destroy information; it encodes it. The activation vector hhh is a linear combination ∑ixiwi\sum_i x_i w_i∑i​xi​wi​ of feature directions with sparse coefficients. Recovering a sparse vector from an under-determined linear mixture is a well-studied mathematical problem: compressed sensing. Its central theorem is encouraging: if xxx is kkk-sparse and the directions wiw_iwi​ are incoherent (small mutual dot products — exactly our near-orthogonality), then xxx can be recovered from only m≳klog⁡(n/k)m \gtrsim k \log(n/k)m≳klog(n/k) measurements — but only by nonlinear algorithms (iterative thresholding, ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ minimization). The same moral as our pentagon example: linear readouts see mush; a nonlinearity that exploits sparsity sees features. This is the mathematical justification for dictionary learning — fit an overcomplete set of directions plus a sparse nonlinear encoder to real activations — which is exactly what the sparse autoencoders of the next chapter do. Analyses in this vein, like Polysemanticity and Capacity in Neural Networks (Scherlis et al., 2022), sharpen the picture by treating features as competing for fractions of a fixed representational “capacity” budget — recovering the toy model’s dropped/superposed/dedicated regimes from an allocation argument.

Computation in superposition is a deeper, open puzzle. Everything in this chapter treated superposition as storage: pack features in, read features out. But storage is not the whole story: models also compute new features from old ones, layer after layer, apparently without ever decompressing. How does an MLP apply “if feature A and feature B then feature C” when A, B, and C are all smeared across shared dimensions along with thousands of other features? Toy constructions show it is possible — you can hand-build circuits that compute on superposed variables, paying interference costs that sparsity keeps manageable — but nobody has a general theory of how much computation fits in how few dimensions, or what algorithms trained models use. As of 2026 this is one of interpretability’s most important open problems: sparse autoencoders (next chapter) largely sidestep it by decomposing activations, and the harder question of decomposing the computation between activations drives the transcoder and attribution-graph work of Chapter 52.

💡Intuition

A model in superposition is like a compressed file that is executed without being decompressed. The weights operate directly on the packed encoding, and the packing was chosen (by gradient descent) so that the operations still approximately work. Interpretability’s job is to write the decompressor after the fact: find the codebook — the feature directions — that turns opaque packed vectors back into a sparse list of human-legible properties. Chapter 51 is about building exactly that decompressor.

⚙️In practice

Where this landed by 2026: frontier interpretability teams treat superposition as settled-enough-to-build-on. Anthropic’s dictionary-learning line (Towards Monosemanticity, 2023; Scaling Monosemanticity, 2024) pulled millions of interpretable features out of production-scale models — Claude 3 Sonnet’s public showcase used a sparse autoencoder (SAE) with ~34 million features against a residual stream of a few thousand dimensions, a features-to-dimensions ratio in the thousands — and OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and the open-source ecosystem (SAELens, Neuronpedia, Gemma Scope) run the same playbook. Nobody at the frontier tries to read raw neurons anymore except as a cheap first pass. The numbers are fast-moving and the “did we find the true features?” debate is live (Chapter 51 covers it), but the core lesson of this chapter — expect far more features than dimensions, so decompose before you interpret — is the working assumption of the entire field.

One caveat deserves its own paragraph, because this book promised honesty about provisional knowledge. The toy model is a model of the hypothesis, not of a transformer: its “features” are given by construction, its data is synthetic, and its architecture is a two-layer autoencoder. The inference “toy models superpose under sparsity + real neurons look polysemantic + SAEs recover sparse near-orthogonal overcomplete dictionaries ⟹ real models use superposition” is abductive — the best explanation of the evidence, not a proof. Alternatives get airtime in the literature: maybe some polysemanticity is incidental rather than optimal; maybe some “features” are artifacts of the recovery method; maybe parts of a model’s knowledge are not direction-shaped at all (Chapter 49’s multi-dimensional and nonlinear exceptions). The field proceeds on superposition because it keeps paying rent — every year it predicts findings that then materialize — which is the most a young science can ask of a hypothesis.

Where this leaves us

  • Polysemanticity is the observation: individual neurons in real models reliably fire for unrelated collections of things (cat faces + car fronts; citations + HTTP + Korean), so neuron-by-neuron interpretation fails.
  • Superposition is the explanation: a model can store far more features than dimensions along almost-orthogonal directions — exponentially many fit, by the high-dimensional geometry of near-orthogonality — as long as features are sparse, because interference costs scale with co-activation (p2p^2p2) while benefits scale with activation (ppp).
  • The toy model makes it visible: an importance-weighted autoencoder with fewer dimensions than features reproduces the whole story — orthogonal storage of top features when dense, superposition when sparse, sharp phase transitions between regimes, and crystalline geometry (antipodal pairs, triangles, pentagons, tetrahedra) rationing fractions of dimensions.
  • Basis privilege explains the pattern of failure: the residual stream’s basis is meaningless by rotation symmetry; MLP bases are privileged by the elementwise nonlinearity, which is why some neurons are clean — but privilege is an incentive superposition can outvote, as the SoLU episode showed.
  • Superposition ≠ distributed representations: distributed means features spread across units; superposition means features outnumber dimensions, so no rotation can untangle them.
  • The consequences set the agenda: features are not neurons; recovering them is a compressed-sensing-flavored problem demanding nonlinear, overcomplete dictionary learning; and computation-in-superposition remains an open problem.

The model is keeping more concepts than it has room for, filed along a million subtle diagonals. Next we build the tool that reads the filing system: Next: Sparse Autoencoders.
