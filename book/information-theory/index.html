Information Theory: Surprise, Entropy, and KL

Derive surprise, entropy, cross-entropy, and KL divergence from scratch — with a 20-questions game, hand-computed bits, and the exact reason LM training loss is what it is.

Four words haunt every explanation of language models: surprise, entropy, cross-entropy, and Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence. They appear in every loss function, every training curve, every alignment paper — and they are routinely introduced in a single breathless paragraph, as if the reader were born knowing them. If you have ever nodded along to “the model minimizes cross-entropy” while privately unsure what a cross-entropy is, this chapter exists for you. We are going to build all four ideas from nothing, slowly, with actual numbers.

Here is the punchline in advance, so you know where we are going. Surprise measures how unexpected a single event is. Entropy is average surprise — how unpredictable a source of events is overall. Cross-entropy is the average surprise you experience when reality follows one distribution but you were betting on a different one — and this is precisely the loss that every large language model (LLM) minimizes during training. KL divergence is the gap between those two: the extra surprise you pay for believing the wrong thing. One idea, measured four ways.

The payoff is enormous. By the end of this chapter, the training loss of GPT-5 and Claude will not be a mysterious squiggle on a chart — it will be a number with physical meaning (“the model is, on average, this many bits surprised by each token of real text”), and perplexity will be its honest translation into “the model is as confused as if it were choosing among kkk equally likely options.” The same machinery will return, with derivations, in loss functions, in reinforcement learning from human feedback, in direct preference optimization (DPO), and in model evaluation. Everything here builds on the previous chapter’s probability — distributions, expectations, independence — and nothing else.

Surprise: a number for “I didn’t see that coming”

Start with a single event. A friend tells you “the sun rose this morning.” You learn nothing; the statement carries no information because you already assigned it probability close to 1. The same friend tells you “it snowed in the Sahara this morning.” That carries a lot of information, precisely because it was improbable. Information, in Shannon’s sense, is not about meaning or importance — it is about how much an event reduces your uncertainty, and improbable events reduce it more.

So we want a function s(p)s(p)s(p) — read “the surprise of an event that had probability ppp” — and we can write down, before doing any math, three properties any reasonable candidate must have:

  1. Certain events carry no surprise: s(1)=0s(1) = 0s(1)=0.
  2. Rarer is more surprising: s(p)s(p)s(p) should decrease as ppp increases, and it should do so smoothly (no sudden jumps as probability varies).
  3. Independent surprises add. If two unrelated events happen — your coin lands heads and it rains — your total surprise should be the sum of the individual surprises. From the probability chapter, the probability of both independent events is the product p1p2p_1 p_2p1​p2​. So we need s(p1p2)=s(p1)+s(p2)s(p_1 p_2) = s(p_1) + s(p_2)s(p1​p2​)=s(p1​)+s(p2​): a function that turns multiplication into addition.

That third property is the whole ballgame. “Turns products into sums” is the defining behavior of exactly one family of functions.

Language model training lives on this curve — every training token the model considered near-impossible delivers a huge loss spike.

Twenty questions, and what a “bit” really is

“Bits” so far is a unit we picked by choosing base 2. It deserves a concrete meaning, and the meaning is delightful: one bit is one optimally chosen yes/no question.

Imagine a simplified weather station in a town with four kinds of day, with long-run probabilities:

symbolprobability
sun1/21/21/2
cloud1/41/41/4
rain1/81/81/8
snow1/81/81/8

Each morning I know today’s weather and you must discover it by asking yes/no questions. What questioning strategy minimizes the average number of questions over many mornings?

A naive strategy asks two balanced questions regardless (“is it sun or cloud?”, then one more) — always exactly 2 questions. But the probabilities are lopsided, and you can exploit that. Ask about the likely outcome first:

  • Q1: “Is it sun?” Half of all mornings, the answer is yes and you are done in 1 question.
  • Q2 (if no): “Is it cloud?” Among the remaining mornings, this ends half of them at 2 questions.
  • Q3 (if no again): “Is it rain?” Yes means rain, no means snow — 3 questions either way.

The average number of questions is an expectation, exactly as defined in the probability chapter:

12(1)+14(2)+18(3)+18(3)  =  0.5+0.5+0.375+0.375  =  1.75 questions.\tfrac{1}{2}(1) + \tfrac{1}{4}(2) + \tfrac{1}{8}(3) + \tfrac{1}{8}(3) \;=\; 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.375 + 0.375 \;=\; 1.75 \text{ questions.}21​(1)+41​(2)+81​(3)+81​(3)=0.5+0.5+0.375+0.375=1.75 questions.

Better than 2. And no strategy beats it — we will see why in a moment.

Now the twist that turns a party game into engineering. Write down the answers along each path, recording “yes” as a bit and “no” as the other. Sun becomes the answer sequence 0; cloud becomes 10; rain 110; snow 111. Your question tree is a binary code: to transmit a morning’s weather, I send the bit-string of answers you would have received, and you can decode it unambiguously (no codeword is a prefix of another, because each codeword ends at a leaf of the tree). The average number of questions equals the average number of bits per transmitted symbol: 1.75 bits. Questions and bits are the same currency.

Look at the depths in that tree. Sun, with probability 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​, sits at depth 1=−log⁡2121 = -\log_2 \tfrac121=−log2​21​. Cloud sits at depth 2=−log⁡2142 = -\log_2 \tfrac142=−log2​41​. Rain and snow sit at depth 3=−log⁡2183 = -\log_2 \tfrac183=−log2​81​. Each symbol’s optimal codeword length is exactly its surprise. The average number of questions, 1.751.751.75, is therefore the average surprise of the distribution. That quantity has a name.

Entropy: average surprise

💡Intuition

Entropy asks one question of a probability distribution: on average, how surprised will I be by draws from you? Equivalently: how many optimally-chosen yes/no questions do I need, on average, to pin down one draw? A distribution that always does the same thing has entropy zero — no questions needed. A distribution spread evenly over many options has high entropy — many questions needed, because nothing about it can be anticipated. Entropy measures the distribution’s inherent unpredictability, before anyone’s beliefs enter the picture.

∑The math

For a discrete random variable XXX with distribution ppp over outcomes xxx, the entropy is the expected surprise:

H(p)  =  Ex∼p ⁣[−log⁡2p(x)]  =  −∑xp(x)log⁡2p(x),H(p) \;=\; \E_{x \sim p}\!\left[-\log_2 p(x)\right] \;=\; -\sum_{x} p(x) \log_2 p(x),H(p)=Ex∼p​[−log2​p(x)]=−x∑​p(x)log2​p(x),

where the sum runs over every outcome with p(x)>0p(x) > 0p(x)>0, and — by convention, justified by the limit plog⁡p→0p \log p \to 0plogp→0 as p→0p \to 0p→0 — outcomes with zero probability contribute nothing. Entropy is always ≥0\geq 0≥0 (each term is), is 000 exactly when one outcome has all the probability, and for kkk outcomes is maximized by the uniform distribution, where it equals log⁡2k\log_2 klog2​k.

✏️Worked example

Fair coin, p=(12,12)p = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)p=(21​,21​): H=−12log⁡212−12log⁡212=12+12=1H = -\tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12 - \tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1H=−21​log2​21​−21​log2​21​=21​+21​=1 bit. One flip, one question, one bit — everything agrees.

Biased coin, p=(0.9,0.1)p = (0.9, 0.1)p=(0.9,0.1): using −log⁡20.9≈0.152-\log_2 0.9 \approx 0.152−log2​0.9≈0.152 and −log⁡20.1≈3.322-\log_2 0.1 \approx 3.322−log2​0.1≈3.322, H=0.9×0.152+0.1×3.322=0.137+0.332≈0.469H = 0.9 \times 0.152 + 0.1 \times 3.322 = 0.137 + 0.332 \approx 0.469H=0.9×0.152+0.1×3.322=0.137+0.332≈0.469 bits. Less than half a fair coin’s entropy: the coin still has two faces, but it is far more predictable, and entropy notices. (The occasional 3.32-bit shock of a tails is outweighed by the drumbeat of unsurprising heads.)

Uniform over 8 outcomes, p(x)=18p(x) = \tfrac18p(x)=81​ each: H=8×18×log⁡28=3H = 8 \times \tfrac18 \times \log_2 8 = 3H=8×81​×log2​8=3 bits — and indeed 3 balanced yes/no questions (“is it in the top half?”, repeated) identify one of 8 options. That last coincidence is a theorem, and it is the reason entropy matters outside of puzzles. Shannon’s source coding theorem (Shannon, 1948), stated informally: no lossless code — no question strategy, no compression scheme, however clever — can use fewer than H(p)H(p)H(p) bits per symbol on average; and codes exist that get as close to H(p)H(p)H(p) as you like (within one bit per symbol immediately, and arbitrarily close if you encode long blocks of symbols together). Entropy is not one measure of compressibility among many. It is the floor: the number of bits a source requires. When the probabilities are all powers of 12\tfrac1221​, as in our weather example, a tree can hit the floor exactly; in general, assigning each symbol a codeword of roughly −log⁡2p(x)-\log_2 p(x)−log2​p(x) bits gets within one bit of it.

🕰History

Claude Shannon developed all of this in “A Mathematical Theory of Communication” (1948), motivated by telephone and telegraph engineering at Bell Labs, and the paper is startlingly readable today — it also introduced, almost in passing, the idea of modeling English by the probability of each letter given preceding letters, and generated text from such models by hand. In a real sense the first language model appears in the same paper as entropy. The name “entropy” was reportedly suggested by John von Neumann on the grounds that the same formula appears in statistical mechanics — and that “no one really knows what entropy is, so in a debate you will always have the advantage.”

⚠️Careful

Entropy is a property of a distribution, not of an outcome. A single event has surprise; only a distribution has entropy. Sloppy writing says “this token has high entropy” when it means either “this token was surprising” (high −log⁡p-\log p−logp) or “the model’s predicted distribution at this position has high entropy” (the model is uncertain about what comes next). Those are different claims — the first is about what happened, the second about the spread of predictions — and conflating them causes real confusion when reading interpretability and sampling papers.

Cross-entropy: paying for the wrong code

Everything so far assumed you know the true distribution: you built the question tree from the real probabilities. Now the move that connects this chapter to machine learning. Suppose reality follows ppp, but you believe qqq, and you build your code (your question tree, your bets) around qqq. Symbols you consider likely get short codewords; symbols you consider rare get long ones. When reality’s symbols actually arrive — drawn from ppp, not qqq — how many bits do you pay on average?

Each symbol xxx costs you −log⁡2q(x)-\log_2 q(x)−log2​q(x) bits (the codeword length your model assigned), and symbols arrive with frequency p(x)p(x)p(x) (reality’s rates). The average is:

H(p,q)  =  −∑xp(x)log⁡2q(x).H(p, q) \;=\; -\sum_{x} p(x) \log_2 q(x).H(p,q)=−x∑​p(x)log2​q(x).

This is the cross-entropy of qqq relative to ppp: the average surprise of a qqq-believer living in a ppp-world. Compare it with entropy — same formula, except the distribution inside the logarithm (your model, setting the code lengths) differs from the distribution outside it (reality, setting the frequencies). When q=pq = pq=p the two coincide: H(p,p)=H(p)H(p, p) = H(p)H(p,p)=H(p).

Reality’s own distribution is the uniquely cheapest code for reality.

Why language model training loss is cross-entropy

Here is where four chapters of setup pay off. A language model, as we will develop properly in Part III, looks at a context and outputs a probability distribution q(⋅∣context)q(\cdot \mid \text{context})q(⋅∣context) over its vocabulary — its beliefs about the next token. Training data supplies what token actually came next. The training objective is: make the model’s distribution cheap-to-encode-with, on average, over real text. That is a cross-entropy — and it collapses into something concrete.

∑The math

Fix one training position with correct next token yyy. The “data distribution” for this position is one-hot: p(x)=1p(x) = 1p(x)=1 if x=yx = yx=y and p(x)=0p(x) = 0p(x)=0 otherwise — the training example asserts that yyy is what came next, full stop. Substitute into the cross-entropy (using the natural log now, as deep learning frameworks do):

H(p,q)  =  −∑xp(x) ln⁡q(x)  =  −p(y)⏟= 1ln⁡q(y)  −  ∑x≠yp(x)⏟= 0ln⁡q(x)  =  −ln⁡q(y).H(p, q) \;=\; -\sum_{x} p(x)\, \ln q(x) \;=\; -\underbrace{p(y)}_{=\,1} \ln q(y) \;-\; \sum_{x \neq y} \underbrace{p(x)}_{=\,0} \ln q(x) \;=\; -\ln q(y).H(p,q)=−x∑​p(x)lnq(x)=−=1p(y)​​lnq(y)−x=y∑​=0p(x)​​lnq(x)=−lnq(y).

Every term with p(x)=0p(x) = 0p(x)=0 vanishes, whatever qqq assigned there, and the entire sum collapses to a single term: the negative log-probability the model gave to the token that actually occurred. The training loss over a corpus is the average of this over positions,

L  =  −1N∑t=1Nln⁡q ⁣(yt∣y1,…,yt−1),\mathcal{L} \;=\; -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N} \ln q\!\left(y_t \mid y_{1}, \ldots, y_{t-1}\right),L=−N1​t=1∑N​lnq(yt​∣y1​,…,yt−1​),

where NNN is the number of tokens and yty_tyt​ the ttt-th token of the data. This is exactly the average surprise, in nats, that the model experiences reading real text. Minimizing it means: be less surprised by reality.

Read that result again, because it dissolves a great deal of mystique. The loss that trains GPT-5-class and Claude-class models is: look at the probability you gave the true next token; take its negative log; average. If the model assigned the true token probability 0.250.250.25, that position contributes −ln⁡0.25≈1.386-\ln 0.25 \approx 1.386−ln0.25≈1.386 nats. If it assigned 0.0010.0010.001, the position contributes 6.96.96.9 nats — the unbounded left wall of the surprise curve is what punishes confident wrongness. A pretraining loss of, say, 2.02.02.0 nats per token means the model’s average experience of real text is the surprise of an event it had rated at e−2.0≈13.5%e^{-2.0} \approx 13.5\%e−2.0≈13.5%.

⚙️In practice

In PyTorch, F.cross_entropy takes the model’s raw scores (logits), applies softmax internally, and returns exactly −ln⁡q(y)-\ln q(y)−lnq(y) averaged over the batch — in nats, because frameworks use natural log. So the loss curves you see in every LLM paper and every training dashboard are nats per token; multiply by 1/ln⁡2≈1.4431/\ln 2 \approx 1.4431/ln2≈1.443 to convert to bits. The scaling laws literature plots this same quantity against compute. One refinement you will meet in loss functions: label smoothing replaces the one-hot ppp with a slightly softened target (e.g., 0.90.90.9 on the true token, crumbs elsewhere), which turns the collapsed −ln⁡q(y)-\ln q(y)−lnq(y) back into a genuine sum — common in machine translation historically, and generally not used in modern LLM pretraining.

KL divergence: the extra bits

We computed an overhead above — 1.918−1.75=0.1681.918 - 1.75 = 0.1681.918−1.75=0.168 bits per symbol for believing qqq in a ppp-world. That overhead is the fourth and final quantity, and in some ways the most important one in modern machine learning.

∑The math

The Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence from qqq to ppp is the excess cross-entropy:

DKL(p ∥ q)  =  H(p,q)−H(p)  =  ∑xp(x)log⁡2p(x)q(x),\KL(p \,\|\, q) \;=\; H(p, q) - H(p) \;=\; \sum_{x} p(x) \log_2 \frac{p(x)}{q(x)},DKL​(p∥q)=H(p,q)−H(p)=x∑​p(x)log2​q(x)p(x)​,

where the second form follows by combining the two sums: −∑xplog⁡2q+∑xplog⁡2p=∑xp(log⁡2p−log⁡2q)-\sum_x p \log_2 q + \sum_x p \log_2 p = \sum_x p (\log_2 p - \log_2 q)−∑x​plog2​q+∑x​plog2​p=∑x​p(log2​p−log2​q). Read it as: the average number of extra bits per symbol you pay for using code qqq when reality is ppp — or, without the coding story, how surprised a qqq-believer is by ppp-reality, beyond the irreducible surprise reality itself carries.

Three properties to internalize:

  1. Nonnegativity (Gibbs’ inequality): DKL(p ∥ q)≥0\KL(p \,\|\, q) \geq 0DKL​(p∥q)≥0 always.   2. Identity: DKL(p ∥ q)=0\KL(p \,\|\, q) = 0DKL​(p∥q)=0 if and only if p=qp = qp=q (strict concavity of log⁡\loglog makes Jensen’s inequality strict unless q/pq/pq/p is constant, and two normalized distributions with constant ratio are equal). This is what licenses using KL — or equivalently cross-entropy — as a training signal: the minimum is achieved exactly at the truth.
  3. Asymmetry: DKL(p ∥ q)≠DKL(q ∥ p)\KL(p \,\|\, q) \neq \KL(q \,\|\, p)DKL​(p∥q)=DKL​(q∥p) in general. KL is not a distance; it does not satisfy symmetry or the triangle inequality, which is why it is called a divergence. The asymmetry is not a defect — the two directions ask different questions, as the next example shows.
✏️Worked example

Asymmetry with real numbers. Let p=(12,12)p = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)p=(21​,21​) (a fair coin) and q=(0.99,0.01)q = (0.99, 0.01)q=(0.99,0.01) (a near-certain coin).

Direction 1 — reality is fair, model is overconfident:

DKL(p ∥ q)=12log⁡2 ⁣0.50.99+12log⁡2 ⁣0.50.01=12(−0.986)+12(5.644)≈2.33 bits.\KL(p \,\|\, q) = \tfrac12 \log_2\!\frac{0.5}{0.99} + \tfrac12 \log_2\!\frac{0.5}{0.01} = \tfrac12(-0.986) + \tfrac12(5.644) \approx 2.33 \text{ bits.}DKL​(p∥q)=21​log2​0.990.5​+21​log2​0.010.5​=21​(−0.986)+21​(5.644)≈2.33 bits.

Direction 2 — reality is near-certain, model hedges 50/50:

DKL(q ∥ p)=0.99log⁡2 ⁣0.990.5+0.01log⁡2 ⁣0.010.5=0.99(0.986)+0.01(−5.644)≈0.92 bits.\KL(q \,\|\, p) = 0.99 \log_2\!\frac{0.99}{0.5} + 0.01 \log_2\!\frac{0.01}{0.5} = 0.99(0.986) + 0.01(-5.644) \approx 0.92 \text{ bits.}DKL​(q∥p)=0.99log2​0.50.99​+0.01log2​0.50.01​=0.99(0.986)+0.01(−5.644)≈0.92 bits.

2.332.332.33 versus 0.920.920.92 — a factor of 2.5, from the same pair of distributions. The lopsidedness has a clean reading. In direction 1, tails happen half the time and the model rated them at 1%1\%1%: half your symbols cost 5.645.645.64 bits extra, a disaster. In direction 2 the model’s hedge wastes about one bit on nearly every flip, but nothing it witnesses ever shocks it. KL punishes assigning low qqq where ppp has real mass — the expectation is taken under the first argument, so only events that actually happen (under the first argument’s distribution) can hurt you.

💡Intuition

A useful mnemonic for reading DKL(a ∥ b)\KL(a \,\|\, b)DKL​(a∥b) anywhere you meet it: the first slot is the distribution doing the experiencing; the second slot is the belief being judged. Expectations are taken under the first argument, so the first argument decides which events matter, and the second argument gets punished wherever it assigned them low probability. “Extra surprise felt by a bbb-believer in an aaa-world.” Every application in the next section becomes readable with this one sentence.

And the decomposition H(p,q)=H(p)+DKL(p ∥ q)H(p,q) = H(p) + \KL(p\,\|\,q)H(p,q)=H(p)+DKL​(p∥q) deserves its own picture, because it explains a fact about training curves that puzzles many newcomers: loss does not go to zero.

⚠️Careful

Minimizing cross-entropy and minimizing KL are the same optimization — but the number on your dashboard is cross-entropy. Since H(p,q)=H(p)+DKL(p ∥ q)H(p,q) = H(p) + \KL(p\,\|\,q)H(p,q)=H(p)+DKL​(p∥q) and H(p)H(p)H(p) does not depend on the model qqq, adjusting qqq to shrink one shrinks the other identically. But a reported loss of 1.9 bits does not mean the model is 1.9 bits wrong; it is H(p)H(p)H(p) bits of irreducible reality plus the model’s actual overhead. Nobody knows the true entropy of natural language precisely — Shannon’s own experiments with human guessers put printed English around 0.6–1.3 bits per character, and the number for modern web-scale token streams is an estimate, not a measurement — so we can never say exactly how much of a frontier model’s loss is “its fault.”

Where KL runs the modern stack

If entropy is the foundational quantity, KL divergence is the load-bearing one in 2026 practice. Three places you will meet it later in this book, each using the asymmetry deliberately.

The RLHF penalty. In reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) — chapter 35 — a policy π\piπ (the model being tuned) is optimized to maximize a learned reward, and left unconstrained it will happily wander into degenerate text that games the reward model. The fix, introduced by Ziegler et al. (2019) and canonical since InstructGPT, is to subtract a penalty β DKL(π ∥ πref)\beta \, \KL(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})βDKL​(π∥πref​), where πref\pi_{\text{ref}}πref​ is the frozen pre-RLHF model and β\betaβ is a coefficient trading reward against drift. Note the direction: the policy is in the first slot, so the expectation runs over text the policy actually generates, and the penalty explodes wherever the policy puts mass the reference model considered improbable. This is the mode-seeking direction: the tuned model may concentrate on a subset of the reference’s behaviors, but it is strongly discouraged from inventing behavior the reference found bizarre. In bits: “you may become a narrower version of your former self, but not a stranger.”

The DPO derivation. Direct Preference Optimization (Rafailov et al., 2023) — chapter 36 — begins from exactly the objective above, maximize reward minus β DKL(π ∥ πref)\beta\,\KL(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}})βDKL​(π∥πref​), and observes that this objective has a closed-form optimal policy: π∗(y∣x)∝πref(y∣x) er(x,y)/β\pi^*(y \mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\, e^{r(x,y)/\beta}π∗(y∣x)∝πref​(y∣x)er(x,y)/β. Solving that expression for the reward rrr and substituting into the preference-model likelihood eliminates the reward model entirely. The entire DPO family is, at bottom, algebra on a KL-constrained objective — if the KL term in this chapter is comfortable, the DPO paper’s Section 4 is four lines of familiar moves.

Distillation. To compress a large teacher model into a small student (Hinton, Vinyals & Dean, 2015), you train the student on the teacher’s full output distribution rather than on one-hot data labels: the loss is DKL(qteacher ∥ qstudent)\KL(q_{\text{teacher}} \,\|\, q_{\text{student}})DKL​(qteacher​∥qstudent​) per position, usually with both distributions softened by a temperature. Because the teacher’s distribution is not one-hot, the sum no longer collapses to a single term — the student learns that “cat” was likely and “kitten” was nearly as good and “carburetor” was absurd, which is far more signal per token than a bare correct answer. This is a large part of how compact frontier models (the Gemini Flash / Claude Haiku / GPT-mini class) inherit ability from larger siblings — vendors rarely publish details, but distillation-style training on teacher outputs is widely understood to be standard practice.

⚙️In practice

Numbers you will see, as of 2025–2026 (approximate and fast-moving): RLHF-era KL coefficients β\betaβ are typically in the 0.010.010.01–0.20.20.2 range, and practitioners monitor “KL from reference” in nats as a drift gauge during post-training — runaway KL is the classic symptom of reward hacking. DPO’s β\betaβ commonly sits around 0.10.10.1–0.50.50.5. In reasoning-model training (the o1/R1 era), several groups weakened or dropped the KL penalty to let the policy explore further from the reference — DeepSeek-R1’s group relative policy optimization (GRPO) objective still carries a KL term, while several successor recipes remove it outright — accepting drift in exchange for capability: an active, unsettled design debate rather than a solved default.

Perplexity: entropy you can picture

One last translation, because raw bits and nats are hard to feel. Exponentiate average surprise and you get perplexity:

PPL  =  2H(H in bits),PPL  =  eH(H in nats),\text{PPL} \;=\; 2^{H} \quad (H \text{ in bits}), \qquad \text{PPL} \;=\; e^{H} \quad (H \text{ in nats}),PPL=2H(H in bits),PPL=eH(H in nats),

and the two agree — same number, reached through whichever logarithm you used. Perplexity undoes the logarithm in entropy, taking the quantity back to the scale of counts. Its meaning: a source (or a model evaluated on data) with perplexity kkk is exactly as unpredictable as a uniform choice among kkk options. It is the effective branching factor — “how many ways could the next symbol go, effectively?”

The uniform case anchors the intuition: uniform over 8 outcomes has entropy 333 bits and perplexity 23=82^3 = 823=8. Perfectly — eight equally live options. For non-uniform distributions, perplexity compresses the lopsidedness into an honest effective count.

✏️Worked example

Our weather distribution has H=1.75H = 1.75H=1.75 bits, so PPL=21.75=2×20.75≈3.36\text{PPL} = 2^{1.75} = 2 \times 2^{0.75} \approx 3.36PPL=21.75=2×20.75≈3.36: four possible weathers, but the skew toward sun makes the source only as unpredictable as about 3.43.43.4 equally likely options. The 0.9/0.10.9/0.10.9/0.1 biased coin: PPL=20.469≈1.38\text{PPL} = 2^{0.469} \approx 1.38PPL=20.469≈1.38 — barely more than one effective option, matching the feeling that this coin has almost no suspense in it.

For a language model: suppose evaluation on held-out text gives an average cross-entropy loss of 3.23.23.2 nats per token. Then PPL=e3.2≈24.5\text{PPL} = e^{3.2} \approx 24.5PPL=e3.2≈24.5 — reading this text, the model is as uncertain, on average, as if each next token were a fair draw among about 25 candidates. Out of a vocabulary of 100,000+ tokens, narrowing to an effective 25 is the entire achievement of language modeling in one number.

Perplexity was the language-modeling scoreboard for decades — GPT-2’s headline results were perplexity numbers — and it remains the cleanest intrinsic measure of a model’s grip on text. It also comes with sharp caveats: it is only comparable between models that share a tokenizer (a model with bigger tokens faces a harder prediction per token — the fix is bits-per-byte normalization), and a lower perplexity does not guarantee better downstream behavior. Both caveats, and perplexity’s modern role alongside benchmark suites, get a full treatment in evaluating language models.

✎Note

You now hold the complete chain that most explanations skip: probability →\to→ surprise −log⁡p-\log p−logp →\to→ entropy (average surprise) →\to→ cross-entropy (average surprise under the wrong beliefs) →\to→ KL (the overhead) →\to→ perplexity (the overhead-inclusive cost, exponentiated back into counts). Each link is one small move. Whenever a later chapter’s loss function looks opaque, walk the chain from the left.

Where this leaves us

  • Surprise is −log⁡2p-\log_2 p−log2​p, and the log is forced, not chosen: it is the only continuous function making independent surprises add. Bits are its units; one bit is one optimally chosen yes/no question.
  • Entropy H(p)=−∑plog⁡2pH(p) = -\sum p \log_2 pH(p)=−∑plog2​p is average surprise — computed by hand: fair coin 111 bit, a 0.90.90.9 coin ≈0.47\approx 0.47≈0.47 bits, uniform-of-8 exactly 333 bits — and, by Shannon’s source coding theorem, the unbeatable floor on average code length for a source.
  • Cross-entropy H(p,q)H(p,q)H(p,q) is the cost of using code qqq in a ppp-world, and it always satisfies H(p,q)≥H(p)H(p,q) \geq H(p)H(p,q)≥H(p). With a one-hot data distribution it collapses to −ln⁡q(correct token)-\ln q(\text{correct token})−lnq(correct token) — which is, term for term, the training loss of every modern language model.
  • KL divergence is the overhead, DKL(p ∥ q)=H(p,q)−H(p)≥0\KL(p\,\|\,q) = H(p,q) - H(p) \geq 0DKL​(p∥q)=H(p,q)−H(p)≥0, zero only at p=qp = qp=q, and asymmetric (2.332.332.33 vs 0.920.920.92 bits in our coin example): the first argument chooses which events matter, the second pays for underrating them.
  • KL is the workhorse of post-training: the RLHF drift penalty, the starting line of the DPO derivation, and the distillation objective are all the same expression pointed in deliberate directions.
  • Perplexity =2H(bits)=eH(nats)= 2^{H(\text{bits})} = e^{H(\text{nats})}=2H(bits)=eH(nats) converts loss into an effective branching factor — a loss of 3.23.23.2 nats/token means “as perplexed as a fair choice among ≈25\approx 25≈25 tokens.”
  • Loss curves plateau above zero because H(p,q)=H(p)+DKLH(p,q) = H(p) + \KLH(p,q)=H(p)+DKL​: the entropy of language itself is in the bill, and no model escapes it.
